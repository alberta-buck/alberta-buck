#+BEGIN_ABSTRACT
The BuckBasket rebalances commodities against each other.  It reads a
seven-scale filter bank over its pool prices, finds which constituent is rich
and which is poor, and moves value between them as excursions turn.  The
=pairs= engine works in /differential mode/ and cancels the common mode
deliberately, because "BUCK's own valuation is the K-controller's job".

But a Uniswap tick is a log price, so the MEAN of those legs is BUCK's own
valuation -- =basketValueInBuck=, the controller's process variable.  The
signal is already in the filter bank.  The basket computes it every cycle and
discards it.

This article puts it to work.  The same ladder drives two mandates: the
differences steer commodity rebalancing, the mean steers /monetary
operations/.  The basket becomes a market maker in BUCK whose quote skew is
set by the controller's error, and whose inventory changes /are/ the open
market operation.

The purpose is speed.  K works, but it works slowly: it acts only through
=creditLimit=, and a credit book turns over in months.  An attacker with real
money can push BUCK off parity faster than K can pull it back.  A basket that
trades the common mode acts in hours, absorbs the excursion while it is
forming, and books the attacker's loss as depositor profit.

In simulation, over nine seeds, an inflation attack's peak excursion falls
from 1538bp to 1099bp, the basket books $604k, and the attacker is worse off
in nine runs out of nine.  The same machinery LOSES when the move is real
rather than an attack, which is the property that makes the rest of it
trustworthy.

Getting there required three separate bounds, and each was invisible until
the one before it was in place.  Without them the basket accumulated half the
pool, showed a profit the whole way, and never once raised an alarm -- because
the act of absorbing suppresses the very signal that would say stop.  That
sequence is documented here in full, because it is more useful than the
result.

* The signal is already there

The =pairs= rebalance engine keeps, for each constituent, a ladder of seven
EMAs over the pool tick.  A tick is \(\log\) of the constituent's price /in
BUCK/.  Write \(c_i\) for leg \(i\)'s log price.  Then

\[ \underbrace{c_i - \bar{c}}_{\text{differential}} \qquad\text{and}\qquad
   \underbrace{\bar{c} = \tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_i c_i}_{\text{common}} \]

are the two halves of the same reading.  The differences say which commodity
is rich against which.  The mean says what the whole basket costs in BUCK --
which is =basketValueInBuck=, the number the PID steers.

The EMA is linear, so this costs nothing:

\[ \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \mathrm{EMA}_k(c_i) \;=\; \mathrm{EMA}_k\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_i c_i\right) \]

The common mode at every one of the seven scales is the mean of the per-leg
ladders the engine already maintains.  No new state, no new reads, no new
storage.  The basket has been computing BUCK's own mispricing at seven
timescales and throwing it away every cycle.

* Why speed matters

BUCK_K is a sound controller and a slow one.  It acts through =creditLimit=,
so its only lever is how much new credit-backed BUCK may be issued.  A credit
book turns over in months.  When someone mints a fortune against pledged
wealth and spends it, K responds by tightening the tap -- and the tap governs
a flow that takes quarters to matter.

Meanwhile the attack lands in days.

The BuckBasket is the largest pool of real assets in the system and it can
act immediately.  It already holds both sides of every pool.  Giving it the
common-mode signal turns the system's inertia into its fastest actuator:

- *K sets the standing policy.*  Slow, structural, and it decides how much
  BUCK the economy may carry.
- *The basket runs the operations.*  Fast, bounded, reversible, and it
  decides what happens this week.

That is the division a central bank already makes between its policy rate and
its open market desk.

* The four quadrants

Two questions decide the operation.  Is BUCK cheap or dear?  And has the
excursion turned, or is it persisting?

#+BEGIN_SRC text
                       BUCK CHEAP (bvib > 1)         BUCK DEAR (bvib < 1)
                       basket costs more BUCK        basket costs less BUCK
                       than it should                than it should

   REVERTING       Q1  ABSORB  (temporary)       Q3  SUPPLY  (temporary)
   the excursion       TOKEN-funded bids             BUCK-funded offers
   has turned          BELOW the price.              ABOVE the price.
                       Buy the dump; sell it         Sell into the bid; buy
                       back on recovery.             back on the return.
                       Net supply: unchanged.        Net supply: unchanged.

   PERSISTENT      Q2  RETIRE  (outright)        Q4  ISSUE   (outright)
   past the leash      Burn acquired BUCK            Mint against basket
   for N days          against outstanding.          TOKEN and sell.
                       Supply FALLS.                 Supply RISES.

** Q1 and Q3 are repo

A concentrated Uniswap V3 range order performs an operation and unwinds it
automatically.  Place BUCK above the price: as BUCK strengthens the position
sells it, and as BUCK returns the position buys it back.  The operation
reverses itself, and it earns the spread while it waits.

That is what a temporary open market operation is.  A central bank uses repo
for daily noise precisely because it self-reverses and needs no decision to
unwind.  The basket gets the same instrument for free from the AMM it already
lives in.

Temporary operations are the default.  They are cheap to be wrong with.

** Q2 and Q4 are outright

An outright operation changes the size of the balance sheet.  Burning BUCK
retires it permanently; minting creates it permanently.  These are reached
only when the temporary operation has been consumed /and/ the price has
stayed away.

Persistence is measured as a duration, not a velocity.  This matters: a
short-window velocity changes sign on noise, so a "has it turned" test built
on velocity almost never reports "no".  In the first version of the model the
outright quadrants were unreachable for exactly this reason -- supply never
moved, and half the mechanism was inert while looking healthy.

The rule is: the deviation exceeds the leash on every one of \(N\)
consecutive days.  Thirty days at 200bp in the model.

* What produces what

*Ordinary commodity dispersion.*  cbBTC rises against LABR; the basket's
weights drift apart; the mean does not move.  This is /differential/ and it
goes to commodity rebalancing exactly as today.  No monetary operation, because
BUCK is not mispriced -- the commodities have moved relative to each other.

*A whale mints and spends a fortune.*  Every pool's BUCK reserve rises
together.  The differences barely move -- the whale sold BUCK, not one
commodity -- so the rebalancer sees almost nothing.  The MEAN moves hard.
This is /common/ and it is Q1: the basket bids for BUCK below the market with
TOKEN it already holds, and buys the dump at a discount.

*The whale's position persists.*  The excursion does not turn.  After thirty
days past the leash the basket escalates to Q2, burning the BUCK it has
accumulated against =totalOutstandingBuck=.  Supply falls permanently.  The
whale must eventually buy BUCK back to retire the credit line that funded the
attack -- into a market whose float the basket has removed.

*Someone wants BUCK badly and bids it up.*  The mean moves the other way.
Q3: the basket offers BUCK above the market from inventory.  If the bid
persists, Q4 mints against basket TOKEN and sells into it, acquiring real
assets with money the market has over-valued.

*A genuine revaluation.*  Commodities really do become worth more BUCK, and
stay there.  This looks exactly like an attack to the signal, and it is the
case the design must lose gracefully.  See /When it should lose/ below.

* What it measures

The model is =alberta_buck/sim/monetary_ops.py=.  It abstracts the pools to a
single price with constant-product impact against depth, mean-reverting at a
120-day timescale, and runs an attacker who sells 6M BUCK over 20 days, holds
60 days, then unwinds -- because an inflation attack is funded by drawing
credit, and the credit must be retired by buying BUCK back.  Costs are 30bp a
leg.  Operations are bounded at 0.4% of depth per day, outright at 0.2%.

* The constraints, and one of them is a correctness bound

** Measure fast enough, or you become the destabilizer

=basketValueInBuck= is read from the basket's own pools, so a basket that
trades on it moves its own sensor.  The obvious guard is to measure on a
scale so slow that one bounded operation cannot shift it.  That was the first
design, measuring on the 160-day EMA.

It made things worse.  The excursion peaks at 1645bp on spot but only 712bp
on the 160-day average, and that 933bp of attenuation is phase lag.
Operations timed off a lagged signal arrive after the spot has turned, so
their flow lands pro-cyclically.  Peak deviation grew from 1645bp to 2116bp.

They still made money.  That is the uncomfortable part, and the central
result of this model:

#+begin_quote
Profitability and stabilization are not the same objective.  "The corrective
trade is the profitable trade" holds only when the signal is timely.  With
lag they come apart, and a lagged operator is a profitable destabilizer.
#+end_quote

Measured across the ladder, on the inflation scenario:

| measure on | peak dev | damping | basket P&L | attacker P&L | supply change |
|------------+----------+---------+------------+--------------+---------------|
| 10d        |   1004bp |  -641bp |   +623,186 |     -580,548 | -566,628      |
| 20d        |   1074bp |  -570bp |   +672,384 |     -622,660 | -801,908      |
| 40d        |   1185bp |  -460bp |   +694,414 |     -711,547 | -623,610      |
| 80d        |   1344bp |  -301bp |   +746,063 |     -985,063 | *+685,152*    |

Two things fall out.

*Stabilization and extraction trade off.*  Acting early damps the excursion,
and therefore forecloses the profit that excursion would have paid.  The
basket cannot both prevent the attack and maximally loot it.  This is a
policy choice, and it should be made explicitly rather than discovered.

*Past roughly 80 days the outright operations invert.*  The lagged reading
still says "dear" long after the market has gone cheap, so an inflation
attack is answered by ISSUING more.  That is not a preference, it is a
correctness bound on the measurement scale.

20d is the knee.  Collocation at that scale is handled by the size bound
instead: an operation capped at 0.4% of depth per day cannot dominate a
20-day average.

** Depositor claims must not be diluted

A depositor's claim is \(\text{redeemBuck} \times \mathrm{NAV} /
\text{totalOutstandingBuck}\).  BUCK minted for a monetary operation is not
backed by a depositor's TOKEN, so folding it into the same counter would
dilute every depositor.

Monetary issuance must be tracked as its own liability class.  Depositor
claims are computed against NAV net of the monetary book.

** Reserves are finite and the bound is public

A convertibility undertaking is only as good as its reserves.  The basket's
defensive capacity is its NAV, and any bound it publishes will be probed --
the 1992 ERM crisis is the standing lesson.  Size operations against NAV, and
expect the bound to be tested rather than respected.

** Anti-windup

Bounding the operation while K keeps demanding more winds the PID integrator
against the bound.  Feed the /achieved/ action back to the integrator, not
the requested one.

* Three runaways, each hiding behind the fix for the last

The =drift= scenario -- a genuine 8% revaluation, no attacker -- is the test
that matters, because a design which profits in every scenario is not being
tested.  Building it exposed three separate unbounded behaviours, and each
was invisible until the previous one was fixed.  This section is the most
useful thing in this article.

** One: the operator suppresses its own alarm

Absorbing holds the measured deviation down.  That is what absorbing IS.  So
a persistence test built on that deviation is silenced by the very act it
exists to police.

In the drift scenario the basket bought until its BUCK inventory reached
*49.9% of pool depth, and still climbing*, having spent essentially its
entire TOKEN reserve -- and the outright quadrants never once fired, because
its own bid held the price inside the leash the whole time.

Worse, it showed a PROFIT throughout, in 7 of 9 seeds.  The gain was real
volatility harvesting around the prevailing level; the loss was an unrealized
mark on a position that could not have been unwound.  A P&L that looks
healthy while the position becomes the market is the classic shape of a
blow-up, and nothing in the price signal reported it.

The fix is to escalate on INVENTORY rather than on price.  Inventory is the
one signal the operator's own action cannot suppress, because it /is/ the
operator's own action.  If a large position has been absorbed and the price
has not come back, the move is real -- whatever the price says.

** Two: the fix relocated the runaway

With inventory-based escalation the position limit held -- 49.9% of depth
fell to 5.3% -- and the basket promptly burned *18.65M BUCK, 93% of all
supply*, chasing a gap that 8% would have closed.  Escalation fired every
day and nothing bounded the cumulative balance-sheet change.

The fix is a cumulative bound on outright operations, 10% of supply here.  A
central bank announces the size of a programme; it does not run the desk
until the number comes right.

** Three: only then does the failure test pass honestly

With all three bounds -- per-operation size, position limit with
inventory-based escalation, and cumulative balance-sheet cap -- the drift
scenario loses in 7 seeds of 9, and the supply change lands at 1.99M against
a 2.0M cap for an 8% revaluation.  The right magnitude, in the right
direction, and a loss.

The general lesson is worth stating on its own.  Each bound was necessary,
each was invisible until the previous one was in place, and the system looked
healthy and profitable in between.  A bounded operator needs bounds on the
FLOW, on the POSITION, and on the CUMULATIVE BALANCE SHEET, and they are
three different constraints rather than three settings of one.

* Implementation

** The director gains a second output

=RebalanceDirectorBase= already caches per-leg ladders and folds them into
per-leg efforts.  The common mode is the mean of those ladders, so:

#+begin_src solidity
/// Signed monetary effort, bp of NAV per epoch.
///   > 0  issue BUCK      < 0  retire BUCK
/// `outright` distinguishes a balance-sheet change from a range order.
function monetaryEffort() external view returns (int32 effortBp, bool outright);
#+end_src

The computation is a mean over the cached =m[k]= the engine already holds, a
deadband, a leash, and a consecutive-day counter.  No new per-leg state.

** The basket gains one keeper entry point

=monetaryOperation()=, permissionless and bounded, mirroring the existing
=rebalanceSource= / =rebalanceSink= pattern:

1. read =monetaryEffort()=;
2. if not =outright=, place or refresh a concentrated range order on the
   indicated side, sized by the effort and capped by the per-epoch bound;
3. if =outright=, burn from the monetary book (retire) or mint into it
   (issue), within the same bound;
4. account to =monetaryOutstanding=, never to =totalOutstandingBuck=.

** Implementing the agent against the chain simulation

Step 2 below is the one to do first, and this section is what it needs.  Every
item here cost a debugging cycle while building =BuckPoolInvestorAgent= and
=BuckIssuerArbAgent=; none of it is visible from the contracts alone.

*** Where it goes

A =_ProxyAgent= subclass in =alberta_buck/sim/equilibrium_agents.py=,
registered with =@_register= and added to the =REBALANCING= roster.  The base
class provides =_bind_proxy=, =_proxy_exec=, =_buy_buck=, =_sell_capped= and
=_swap_via_simlp=.  The proxy is a =SimLP=, which matters: it can serve the
Uniswap V3 mint callback itself, so the agent can hold concentrated positions
directly.

*** Reading the signal

=basketValueInBuck()= off the basket is the spot common mode.  The agent
keeps its own EMA ladder over =log(bvib)= on the =PairsRebalanceDirector=
window geometry (5,10,20,40,80,160,320), measures the level on the 20-day
rung, and detects the turn on the 10-day rung.  Advance the ladder ONCE per
day, before any decision, or the level lags the action by a tick.

*** The three primitives, and their exact call paths

*Range order.*  The proxy mints directly:

#+begin_src python
d.chain.send(self.proxy.functions.mint(pool, tickLower, tickUpper, L, t0, t1))
#+end_src

Exiting takes TWO calls through =exec=, and a burn alone silently strands the
capital -- =burn= only credits the owed amounts, =collect= moves them:

#+begin_src python
self._proxy_exec(d, pool, pool_c.encode_abi(
    "burn(int24,int24,uint128)", args=[lo, hi, liquidity]))
self._proxy_exec(d, pool, pool_c.encode_abi(
    "collect(address,int24,int24,uint128,uint128)",
    args=[self.proxy.address, lo, hi, 2**128 - 1, 2**128 - 1]))
#+end_src

*Issue.*  =Buck.mint(amount)= ACTIVATES coverage against the agent's
BuckCredit; it does not credit the balance.  Spending afterwards is what
draws.  Two consequences that look like bugs if they are not expected:
=BuckCredit.currentValue= returns 0 while =activatedValue= is zero, so an
un-activated NFT contributes nothing to =creditLimit= and the agent can hold
collateral it cannot spend; and remaining capacity must be read from chain,
never tallied in Python, because depreciation moves it underneath any local
count.  Note the two halves live on DIFFERENT contracts:

#+begin_src python
face = d.credit.functions.depreciatedFaceValue(tid).call()   # BuckCredit
used = d.buck.functions.mintsBacked(tid).call()              # Buck
headroom = max(0, face - used)
#+end_src

A Python-side tally of what was activated drifts from the chain within days
and presents as "insufficient credit allocation" on most attempts.

*Retire.*  Buy BUCK back so the signed balance climbs toward zero, then burn
to release coverage.  =Buck.burn(uint256)= or the tokenIds overload.

*** An agent is not the basket, and two quadrants are weaker for it

This is the one place where the quadrant table above does not survive contact
with the contracts, and it is worth being exact because the difference is
what phases 3 and 4 exist to close.

=mintFromBasket= and =burnFromBasket= both begin

#+begin_src solidity
require(msg.sender == basket && basket != address(0), "BUCK: not basket");
#+end_src

so an agent reaches supply only through the ordinary credit path.  Three
consequences, none of them obvious from the quadrant table:

*Q4 is bounded by K; the basket's is not.*  An agent mints against its own
BuckCredit, so it is capped by =creditLimit = totalCurrentValue x buckK=.
=mintFromBasket= bypasses that machinery entirely -- direct-mint BUCK is
backed by the basket's TOKEN reserves rather than by insured-asset credit.
The funding factor is NOT the constraint here: it is \(1 + 10(b-p)/b\), which
returns ZERO once BUCK is about 10% over-valued, so the gate is switched off
exactly when Q4 wants to fire.

*Burning does not destroy tokens.*  =Buck.burn= deactivates coverage -- "you
repay the credit, then release the coverage, as with any loan" -- and the
post-burn check is =used <= creditLimit=.  Nothing is subtracted from the
caller's balance.

*So retirement happens on the BUY, not on the burn.*  The supply invariant is

\[ \text{totalSupply} \;=\; \sum_a \max(0,\ \text{signedRaw}(a)) \]

An account holding drawn credit has a negative signed balance and therefore
contributes nothing.  Buying BUCK while drawn moves float out of the pool and
into an account where it counts for nothing, and supply falls by exactly that
amount.  Buying BEYOND the draw just accumulates inventory and changes no
supply at all.  The burn's job is to close the line so the retirement cannot
simply be undone by redrawing -- which is precisely the temporary/outright
distinction, and it is the same distinction Q1 and Q2 encode.

Two things follow for the agent.

It needs a STANDING BOOK.  With no drawn line, Q2 has nothing to retire and
degrades to Q1 whatever the policy says.  So the agent opens by drawing a
fraction of its line and selling it, which both funds the TOKEN side that Q1
spends and creates the outstanding issue that Q2 contracts.  A central bank's
asset side is bought with money it issued, and the same structure is what
gives it room to move in either direction.

And the residual gap must be MEASURED rather than assumed away.  Count the
days the policy asked for an outright retirement and no drawn line was left,
and report that number: it is the size of what an agent cannot do, in the
units of the thing itself.  In the reverting scenario it is not small.

Two runs of the same scenario and seed differing only in whether
=MonetaryOpsAgent= is in the roster (=SIM_MONETARY_OPS=1=), compared by
=alberta_buck/sim/compare_ops.py=.  One agent, not a population: the basket

Report the desk's terminal INVENTORY beside its P&L, always.  A desk that
damps the deviation by accumulating a position it could never unwind has
moved the loss rather than removed it, and the deviation series alone will
not say so -- that is exactly the first runaway in this article, which showed
a profit in seven seeds of nine while holding half the pool.

*** Units and orientation, which are the two silent killers

=bvib= is BUCK PER BASKET.  TOKEN to BUCK multiplies; BUCK to TOKEN divides.
Getting this backwards still runs, still trades, and reports plausible
numbers -- it simply sizes every operation wrongly and marks the book wrong.

=Buck.balanceOf= on a Non-Carrying account is held BUCK PLUS unused credit
headroom, so before any draw it already reports =creditLimit= = K x
collateral.  That is the drawable amount and it is what a position should be
sized against.  =signedBalanceOf= is the raw balance and does NOT net the
demurrage lien, so sizing a transfer off it produces "amount exceeds
spendable".

Pool orientation decides which side of the current tick a directional
position sits on.  The pool price is token1/token0, so holding BUCK to sell
into a BUCK rally is ABOVE the tick when BUCK is token0 and BELOW it when
BUCK is token1.  Getting this wrong puts every position on exactly the wrong
side and looks like bad luck.

*** Concentrated liquidity is not full-range liquidity

The seeding formula in =deploy.py= is for full-range positions.  Reusing it
for a narrow band under-sizes by the ratio of the widths -- 20 to 30x at 300
to 600bp -- which presents as positions placing successfully while most of
the capital sits idle.  With the price inside the range both legs bind:

\[ a_0 = L\frac{\sqrt{B}-\sqrt{P}}{\sqrt{P}\sqrt{B}}, \qquad
   a_1 = L(\sqrt{P}-\sqrt{A}), \qquad L = \min(L_0, L_1) \]

A tick is a log price, so a `bp` move is \(\ln(1+x)/\ln(1.0001)\) ticks --
900bp is 862 ticks, not 900.  Using bp directly makes a wide range 4%
narrower than intended.

*** Telemetry, or the agent is invisible

Counters written into =ctr= are NOT in the vector unless =snapshot.py= copies
them into the frame dict.  A counter that is not copied reads 0 forever, and
that has produced two separate "the agent is dead" conclusions on this branch
about agents that were working.  Add the counters to =snapshot.py= in the
same commit as the agent, never later.

Report per-agent inventory through =arb_state()=, which the snapshotter
collects into =arb2[]=.  Aggregate counters hide the failure that matters
here: an agent whose position is quietly becoming the market shows healthy
totals right up until it cannot exit.

*** Where each bound is enforced

| bound             | value        | enforced                                  |
|-------------------+--------------+-------------------------------------------|
| per-operation     | 0.4% depth   | trade sizing, via =_impact_cap=           |
| position          | 10% depth    | refuse to add when inventory exceeds it   |
| escalation        | 5% depth     | switch temporary -> outright              |
| cumulative        | 10% supply   | net (minted - burned) across the run      |
| measurement scale | 20d rung     | never slower than 40d (80d inverts)       |

*** Verification checklist

Before trusting any result from it:

1. counters appear in the vector and are non-zero;
2. =arb_state= inventory is bounded and NOT monotonically growing;
3. outright operations fire at all -- if Q2/Q4 are zero the mechanism is half
   inert, which is how this model first behaved;
4. supply moves in the correct direction during an attack, not merely at the
   end of the run;
5. a run with the agent disabled, same seed, for the baseline;
6. several seeds, because the failure case reversed sign between one seed and
   nine.

1. The model in this article (done): does the mechanism work, and where does

** What the agent showed, including the part that did not survive

=MonetaryOpsAgent= runs the quadrants from outside the basket.  Over 365 days
in the reverting regime, against the same roster and seed with the agent
removed:

The first row alone reads as a 32% cut in the peak excursion.  It is a
single draw, and it does not replicate: on two further seeds the PEAK got
WORSE, by 56bp and 165bp.  What survives all three is different and narrower
-- the MEAN deviation falls every time, by 12 to 24%.

The distinction is the useful part.  A bounded desk absorbing a fraction of
depth per day can hold down the everyday wander of the common mode; it
cannot stand in front of the excursion that actually matters, and its own
standing issuance adds float while it tries.  Anyone quoting a peak-damping
number from one seed is quoting noise, and this article did exactly that
once already, in the =drift= row of the scenario table.

What also replicates, in all three seeds, is a transfer: depositors gain
about $100-140k and the treasury loses very nearly the same amount.  The
plausible mechanism is that holding BUCK nearer parity puts fewer
redemptions into BuckBasketProRata's deflation branch, which is the branch
that converts depositors' TOKEN to cover burns -- so the treasury accrues
less because it is expropriating depositors less.  That is an interpretation
of a consistent number, not a measured causal chain.

The agent also measured its own ceiling.  =moNoBook= counted 36 days on which
the policy called for an outright retirement and the agent had no drawn line
left to retire against -- the gap that only the contract can close.

* The contract: BuckBasketOps

=src/basket/BuckBasketOps.sol=, with =commonMode()= / =monetaryEffort()= on
=PairsRebalanceDirector=.  Selected with =--basket ops=, and inert until
=setOpsParams= enables it, so an ops basket with no policy installed is the
baseline it is compared against.

** The depositor separation is achieved by NOT writing to the payout path

This was expected to be the expensive part and turned out to be the cheapest,
because the architecture already had the shape.

A depositor's claim is \(V = \theta\,\mathrm{NAV}\) with \(\theta =
\text{redeemBuck}/\text{totalOutstandingBuck}\), allocated from
=poolBuckValues()=, which reads the basket's LP POSITION net of
=treasuryLiquidity=.  There is already a second, non-depositor class of
liquidity, and exactly ONE line excludes it:

#+begin_src solidity
if (totalL <= c.treasuryLiquidity) continue;
depL[i] = totalL - c.treasuryLiquidity;
#+end_src

So the monetary book is held as plain TOKEN and BUCK BALANCES and never
LP'd.  It is then outside =depL= by construction, and not one line of the
redemption allocator changes.  =mintFromBasket= does not touch
=totalOutstandingBuck= either -- only =depositToken=, =_depositBuck= and
=redeem= do -- so monetary issuance cannot enter a receipt's denominator even
by accident.

The reason to prefer this over netting a monetary term out of the payout
formula: \(\theta\) appears four times across =_allocateSellHigh= and
=_allocateSingleToken=, and a mistake in any of them is a silent transfer
away from depositors rather than a bad trade.  Two tests pin it -- the book
survives a full depositor exit, and no quadrant moves =totalOutstandingBuck=.

** What only the contract can do

- *Q2 retires float the basket never issued.*  =burnFromBasket= destroys BUCK
  bought from anyone.  An agent cannot: supply is \(\sum_a \max(0,
  \text{signedRaw}(a))\), so it removes float only while its own balance is
  drawn, and =Buck.burn= merely deactivates coverage.
- *Q4 is not capped by K.*  =mintFromBasket= is backed by basket TOKEN rather
  than by insured-asset credit, so it bypasses =creditLimit= entirely.

** A bound that is not politeness

=maxLegBp= caps each operation at a fraction of that pool's BUCK reserve.
That is a safety property, not good manners: =poolBuckValues= runs
=_enforceSlippageGuard= on every redemption, so a monetary swap large enough
to push spot off TWAP would revert every depositor exit until the window
caught up.  The basket must not be able to brick its own redemption path.

** The desk has to be capitalized, and that is the 1992 lesson

Q1 and Q2 are TOKEN-funded bids.  The desk may not fund them from depositor
TOKEN -- a depositor is paid in TOKEN only, so converting their commodity to
defend parity would charge them for the defence.  Its own Q4 issuance is the
other source, but that is exactly backwards for a desk whose first task is an
excursion the WRONG way: in the reverting regime BUCK is never dear, so the
desk never accumulates.  Uncapitalized, it wanted to act on 57 of 90 days and
had nothing to act with.

A currency board can only defend with reserves it built up earlier.  So the
desk is capitalized like one -- =capitalizeMonetary()= is the founding grant,
and in production the same job is done by treasury accrual, which is already
outside every depositor claim.  The size of that grant is a real policy
variable and it is the first thing to sweep.

Two seeds, 365 days, the same roster and seed in both arms; the only
difference is which shell is deployed (the monetary-ops AGENT is off in

The peak improves in BOTH seeds, which is the line the AGENT could not hold

*** The bound that prevented the escalation it was meant to trigger

Q2 fired ZERO times in the first pair of runs, while the inventory ceiling
bound on 150 and 95 days.  =_absorb= tested that ceiling at the top and
reverted before Q2 was ever considered -- so a desk pinned at its ceiling
could never escalate, though being pinned there IS the signal that the move
is real.  The bound meant to trigger escalation was preventing it: the first
runaway of this article, one layer further out.

Q2 now burns INVENTORY first, which needs no TOKEN (by the time persistence
is established the desk has usually spent its reserves absorbing) and
relieves the ceiling.  On seed 43981 that took the peak from -138bp to
-375bp against baseline, halved the supply increase, cut the ceiling hits
from 150 to 32, and moved the monetary book from +73k issued to -2.05M
retired.  On seed 7 the net supply change went from +870k to -9k: the
retirement exactly cancelled the issuance.

*** Reducing supply and profiting from it are the same budget spent twice

| seed  | desk P&L | float retired | retired per BUCK spent |
|-------+----------+---------------+------------------------|
| 43981 |    -1.99M |         2.05M |                   1.03 |
| 7     |    -0.49M |         0.59M |                   1.22 |

The desk LOSES on its own book and retires slightly MORE float than the book
it spent, because it is buying BUCK at a discount and destroying it.  That is
not a defect, it is the trade: Q1 and Q3 are the extraction path -- buy the
dump, sell it back, keep the spread -- and Q2 forgoes that recovery profit to
buy a permanent supply reduction instead.  The gain accrues to every BUCK
holder rather than to the desk, and it shows up here as depositors ending
$85-100k better off in both seeds.

So the answer to "can we profit from reducing BUCK supply AND reduce BUCK
supply" is: not with the same operation.  A desk tuned to extract holds and
sells; a desk tuned to stabilize burns.  What the persistence test buys is
the ability to do the first by default and the second only when the
excursion proves real.

*** K kept forcing

=buckK= railed for exactly one day in all four runs, and the desk retired
2.05M and 0.59M of float without changing that.  At this size the fast
operations are not damping K's error into silence, which is the precondition
for the desk's position being a bet worth placing at all.  Whether it
survives a larger desk is exactly what =SIM_OPS_CAPITAL_USD= is for, and it
is the open question rather than a settled one.

** Why the limits exist, and what they are actually for

The bounds are not primarily risk limits.  They exist to stop the desk
substituting a fast fix for the slow, structural withdrawal of BUCK that K
performs through =creditLimit= -- so they are the knob that sets where the
two mandates OVERLAP, and they are meant to be swept
(=SIM_OPS_POSITION_BP= / =SIM_OPS_OUTRIGHT_BP= / =SIM_OPS_CAPITAL_USD=).

This reframes what counts as success.  The desk's position is a BET that K
eventually forces the issue: it buys BUCK the market is dumping and holds it
while K tightens credit behind it.  Interim mark-to-market is therefore the
wrong scorecard, and a desk that loses in the short term is fine provided the
long-term forcing arrives.

What would invalidate the bet is subtler and is the thing to watch for.  The
desk damps the excursion by absorbing it, and =basketValueInBuck= is read
from the very pools it absorbs into -- so a SUCCESSFUL operation shrinks the
error K sees.  If K then stops tightening, the forcing never arrives and the
desk is left holding inventory with nothing behind it.  That is the
system-level form of the first runaway in this article: the operator
suppressing its own alarm, one loop further out.

=monetaryDeviationOffset()= exposes exactly that quantity -- what the desk's
own inventory has taken out of the deviation K measures.  It is EXPOSED
rather than acted on, because the right response is a controller-side choice
(feed K the undamped error, or feed the achieved action back to the
integrator as anti-windup) and it should be measured before it is picked.  In
a 90-day run the desk held 1.25M BUCK of float while =buckK= still fell from
0.745 to 0.584, so the forcing was not being suppressed at that size.  Whether
that survives a larger desk is the open question, and it is the reason the
bounds are a sweep rather than a constant.

* Limitations

- The model abstracts every pool to one price.  Real operations act on one
  TOKEN/BUCK pool at a time and must choose which, which is a routing problem
  the differential engine already solves and this model does not exercise.
- Attack sizing is a single shape: 6M over 20 days, held, then unwound.  A
  patient attacker who bleeds size in over months is a different problem and
  is not tested here.
- The reversion timescale is assumed, not fitted.  The basket's real
  reversion rate should come from the same OU fit that the rebalance article
  proposes for its deadbands.
- Nothing here models the interaction between monetary operations and the
  commodity rebalancer competing for the same pool depth.  They share an
  actuator, and that coupling is unexamined.
- The 1:K position sizing that a real range order needs -- placing an exact
  mix over the current price -- is solved in =BuckPoolInvestorAgent= but not
  reflected in this model, which trades a single abstract price.
- *The parameterization is not converged.*  The bounds above are the ones
  that stopped the runaways this model produced, at the sizes that happened
  to work; they are not fitted, and no claim is made that they are optimal
  or even good.  The end-of-run supply change also reflects the whole path
  rather than the attack alone -- an inflation attack contracts supply while
  it runs and expands it again as the attacker unwinds, so the terminal
  number is not a clean read on the response.
