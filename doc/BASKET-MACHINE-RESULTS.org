#+TITLE: The basket machine: what the lab shows
#+DATE: 2026-10-11
#+OPTIONS: ^:nil toc:2 num:t

This is what we learned by running [[file:BASKET-MACHINE.org][the basket machine]] on the real contracts. The work still to
do is in [[file:BASKET-MACHINE-TASKS.org][the task list]]. Every number can be rebuilt with the commands at the end.

* The short version

The basket makes money from prices that part and come back. In the savings world, five years of
five commodities with depositors coming and going, it does so better than the older upkeep, and
the BUCK holds.

| Five years, two seeds | Older upkeep | The machine |
|----------------------+--------------+-------------|
| Share price at the end, real prices | 1.146, 1.237 | 1.187, 1.294 |
| Share price at the end, reverting prices | 1.194, 1.302 | 1.276, 1.398 |
| Equity it could spend on an exit, at the least | 0% | 62% to 69% |
| K at the end, from 0.72 | 0.75 to 0.81 | 0.74 to 0.77 |

Those numbers are from the model as it stands on 2026-10-12. Every table below that is not
marked as settled was measured before three faults in the model were mended, and one of them
changes the reading of all of it.

The first five-year matrix seemed to show both upkeeps failing in seven worlds: K reached its
upper limit and the BUCK then rose against its basket without stopping. It was not the basket.
The model's depositors kept every BUCK an exit paid them and never spent one, so the worlds had
a demand for BUCKs that nothing could meet ([[*Who held the BUCKs][Who held the BUCKs]]). With depositors who sell what
they are paid, those worlds hold on both upkeeps: [[*The settled model][The settled model]] has the numbers.

The largest lesson about the basket came from the savings test, not from the lab. The machine as
first built left half of every deposit waiting, for months. The cause was in how its credit was
counted. A BUCK it drew and kept beside its own TOKEN counted as a BUCK spent, so it could pair
only K of what it held. Now K bounds only the BUCKs it has let out. Then nothing is sold to
place a deposit, nothing is sold to pay an exit, and most of the upkeep had no work left. We
took it out.

The lab was rerun on the rebuilt machine, and what it showed before still holds: a target that
leans against price earns most of the gain, a gap is best released at a steady rate, and when a
TOKEN moves and never comes back the basket loses. One thing changed. Counting how long a gap
has lasted now earns half again what a fixed line does. Before the rebuild the two were close.

Four more things to be careful about. The savings result is two seeds, which agree, and the
lab is one. The machine uses a sixth more gas than the older upkeep and makes many small trades.
The desk made no trade in the lab and few in the first seed of the savings world, so little
here tests it. And a young basket's share price shows the relief on its lien before the
demurrage on the BUCKs it holds.

* How to read the numbers

There are two tests.

/The lab/ is 120 days with three TOKENs and a basket that starts with one million. The wheel
turns four times a day. Every gain is measured against the same basket doing no rebalancing,
and valued "at reference": its TOKEN at outside prices, its BUCKs at what the BUCK is supposed
to be worth.

/The savings world/ is five years with five commodities: labour, construction, food, energy and
gold. It has depositors who come and go, retirees, a desk, K and arbitrageurs, and the wheel
turns twice a day. It runs on real prices and on a reverting copy of them. What it reports is
the share price: what one share of the basket is worth in BUCKs.

A share price moves about half a percent from one day to the next. So where two runs are
compared, each is averaged over its last 90 days.

No gas is charged in either. The [[*Gas][gas section]] says what it would cost.

* The five-year matrix

The matrix is the earlier program's 14 five-year worlds: different mixes of depositors, debtors
and disturbances, at four turns of the wheel a day. They were built on the pro-rata basket. Each
was run here on three baskets, with the five TOKENs: the pro-rata basket, the equity basket on
its older upkeep, and the equity basket on the machine ([[file:review/matrix-baskets.py][matrix-baskets.py]]).

As first run, half of them held and half failed. The failure was the model's and not the
basket's: [[*Who held the BUCKs][Who held the BUCKs]] says how that was found, and [[*The settled model][The settled model]] has the matrix
run again. The sections between are the first run, with depositors who kept their BUCKs. They
are kept because they are how the fault was found, and because what they measured of issuers
and of the funding gate is still true.

Between the two upkeeps there is little in it. The machine ends about 2% ahead in three worlds
and 1% or 2% behind in three. The seventh is decided by its first weeks, when the basket costs 3
BUCKs, and says nothing about either upkeep. The machine never runs short of room. The older

The larger difference is K. Under the older upkeep K ends at 0.92 to 0.94 in three of these

where it ends on the pro-rata basket, between 0.69 and 0.79. The likely reason is that the
machine lets BUCKs out when the BUCK is dear, which is what K would otherwise climb to allow.

** Seven worlds fail, on both upkeeps

These are the portcast world and the six with mature debtors and basketeers. K reaches its upper
limit, 0.95. Then the BUCK begins to rise against its basket and does not stop.

| basketeers, endog, raid | 26 | 74 | 0.23 | 0.16 | 1.89 | 0.96 |

On the pro-rata basket all seven hold: the BUCK stays within 0.3% to 5% of its basket for the
five years. In four of them K is at its limit there too, and nothing follows from it.

In the portcast world K reaches 0.95 on day 415 under the older upkeep and on day 524 under the
machine. On both the BUCK is 10% dear by day 650. From there its price in dollars goes from 1.2
to 7.2 under the older upkeep and to 12.2 under the machine, while its basket comes to cost 1.40
dollars. The supply of BUCKs hardly changes: it stays between 70 and 85 million.

The basket is in the loop. It owes BUCKs, and what it may owe is K times its equity, counted in
BUCKs. When the BUCK rises, the same TOKEN is worth fewer BUCKs, the limit falls, and the basket
sells TOKEN for BUCKs to repay. That is a bid for BUCKs when they are already scarce, and with K
at its limit nobody can issue the BUCKs it is buying. In the portcast world the older upkeep's
lien falls from 9.5 million to 2.0 million. The machine's falls from 18.2 million to 1.3 million,
and it sells over 50 million BUCKs' worth of TOKEN on the way down.

That is what the runs show the basket doing. That it is the cause, and not only carried along,
is not yet tested. The pro-rata basket owes nothing: its BUCKs are issued against deposits and
never called back. That is the visible difference between the basket that holds and the two
that fail.

Who loses depends on the upkeep. Counted in BUCKs every share falls, because its TOKEN is worth
fewer BUCKs. Counted in baskets, which is what a share buys, the older upkeep's share holds or
gains: it holds more BUCKs than it has let out. The machine's share loses, by 4% to 79%. The
machine leans against the BUCK's price, so when the BUCK is dear it has let out all its ceiling
allows, 47% of its equity, and it stays there while the BUCK rises many times over. Its lean is
a bet that parity returns. With K at its limit parity does not return, and the bet is leveraged.

Four things follow.

- The equity basket, on either upkeep, can feed a rise in the BUCK that K cannot stop. The
  machine did not create this and does not cure it.
- The machine postpones it. It reaches K's limit later in every world it finished, and in the
  worlds that hold it keeps K away from the limit where the older upkeep does not.
- The machine's depositors bear more of it when it comes.
- The savings world is not clear of it. It holds for five years on two seeds, but on reverting
  prices K was at 0.93 to 0.95 in its last two years under both upkeeps.

One caution about the worlds themselves. They were built for the pro-rata basket, which issues a
BUCK for every BUCK's worth deposited. On the equity basket the same people have fewer sources
of BUCKs, so some of this may be the worlds' doing. The loop is the basket's all the same.

** Who could issue

A dear BUCK should bring out issuers: anyone with assets can pledge them and sell BUCKs that
are worth more than their basket. In the portcast world we counted who could, on the older
upkeep's run. The machine's and the pro-rata basket's runs have the same people and the same
story until day 600.

| Who | How many | What they could issue | What happened |
|-----+----------+-----------------------+---------------|
| Mortgage debtors | 30, arriving over three years | 7.2 million in all | Refused by the funding gate until the BUCK was 9% dear |
| Credit arbitrageurs | 3 | 10.4 million | Issued it all between day 260 and day 600 |
| Dollar arbitrageurs | 5, with 22 million dollars | Nothing: they can only buy cheap BUCKs | Idle for five years |
| The desk | 1 | 0.4 million | Older upkeep: never turned. Machine: issued 27 times and lost its capital |

That is about 18 million of issuing capacity in a world with 75 million BUCKs in circulation.

/The funding gate./ To mint against a credit that carries an insurance premium, the minter must
already hold BUCKs worth the insurance deposit for that mint times a factor. The deposit is ten
years' premium: here 5% to 15% of what is minted, so 40,000 to 120,000 BUCKs for an 800,000
refinance. The factor was then one at parity, more when the BUCK was cheap, 0.7 when it was 3%
dear, and reached zero only when it was 9% dear.

These debtors cannot meet it, because the model gives them no savings. A debtor arrives with
nothing but the income that follows. One paid at harvest holds no dollars at all until its
first harvest, and its mortgage grows meanwhile. One on a salary builds up a few thousand a
month and keeps the first 20,000 back. Of the twelve who had arrived by day 600, four had got a
mint through. Of the other eight, five held no dollars and three about 20,000. They did buy
BUCKs toward the gate, 17 times by then, but the reserve was beyond what most of them had.

That is a fault of the model. A household that can refinance a mortgage has savings, and the
want of some would not keep it from retiring a debt that bears interest with BUCKs that bear
none. Three things were changed on this (2026-10-12), and the runs in this section were made
before any of them:

- The factor is one straight line: nothing once the BUCK is 2% dear, a half at parity, one at 2%
  cheap, and on up at the same slope. Near parity the gate now asks half a deposit, and none
  when BUCKs are scarce.
- A debtor arrives with dollars worth the insurance deposit its refinance asks. A household
  about to retire a mortgage does the arithmetic and finds that much, from savings or a
  relative.
- A debtor reads the factor a mint would face. The mint runs the controller's cycle before it
  reads the factor. The debtor read the factor of the last cycle, hours old, bought exactly
  that reserve, and was then refused for the one the mint asked. So some of the refusals
  counted below were of debtors who had bought what they were told to. With all three, half a
  year of this world has 18 refinances and no refusal.

| Up to day 600 | Older upkeep | Machine | Pro-rata basket |
|---------------+--------------+---------+-----------------|
| Debtors' mints refused | 59 | 53 | 42 |
| Debtors' mints that went through | 4 | 4 | 3 |
| BUCK's distance from its basket, at most | 4% dear | 3% dear | 1% |
| K | 0.76 to 0.95 | 0.75 to 0.95 | 0.75 |

From day 240 to day 600 the BUCK sat up to 4% dear and K climbed to its limit. That is the
stretch in which new issuers were wanted, and the gate turned them away. When the BUCK passed 9%
dear, around day 650, the factor fell to zero and every debtor who had arrived issued within
weeks. By then the credit arbitrageurs were spent and 7 million was not enough.

On the pro-rata basket the debtors are refused just the same, 156 times in the five years, and
it does not matter: the BUCK never leaves parity and the credit arbitrageurs never issue a
BUCK. That basket supplies the BUCKs itself, one for every BUCK's worth deposited.

So this world is short of issuers, and one rule of Buck's kept the willing ones out in the band
where they are wanted.

** Issuers enough

Three more runs of the portcast world put that to the test, on the factor as it then was
(=build/sim/issuers/=). In one the debtors' credits pay no premium, so the gate does not apply
to them. In two the three credit arbitrageurs have ten times the wealth to pledge, 109 million
in place of 11.

| Portcast world | K at its limit from day | BUCK from its basket, worst | BUCK at the end, dollars | BUCKs at the end, millions | A share in baskets |
|----------------+-------------------------+-----------------------------+--------------------------+----------------------------+--------------------|
| As it is, older upkeep | 415 | 81% | 7.17 | 74 | 1.00 |
| As it is, machine | 571 | 89% | 11.81 | 74 | 0.29 |
| Debtors free of the gate, machine | 656 | 89% | 11.84 | 73 | 0.30 |
| Richer arbitrageurs, older upkeep | 415 | 7% | 1.41 | 165 | 1.02 |
| Richer arbitrageurs, machine | 550 | 44% | 2.39 | 180 | 0.72 |

- Freeing the debtors is not enough. They refinance as they arrive and are never refused, and
  the BUCK runs from day 660 all the same. They have 7 million to issue.
- With issuers enough the BUCK holds, while they last. On the older upkeep it stays within 7% of
  its basket for the five years. The arbitrageurs issue 87 million BUCKs to keep it there.
- On the machine it holds until day 1,750. By then the arbitrageurs have issued 103.5 million,
  which is all they can, and the BUCK runs.
- K is at its limit from day 415 or 550 to the end in both. Parity is held by the issuers alone,
  and the world takes 25 million new BUCKs a year from them.

So more issuers do not cure this world. They feed it. Something in it takes BUCKs in without
end.

** Who held the BUCKs

Every BUCK is in some account, so we read them all ([[file:review/who-holds.py][who-holds.py]]). The portcast world on day 300,
millions of BUCKs:

| Held by | Older upkeep | Pro-rata basket |
|---------+--------------+-----------------|
| Depositors who had left the basket | 9.1 | 0 |
| Savers | 0 | 8.2 |
| The BUCK/dollar pool | 46.7 | 47.5 |
| The basket's pools | 7.8 | 8.9 |
| All BUCKs | 63.9 | 65.1 |
| K | 0.819 | 0.750 |
| The basket's cost in BUCK | 0.973 | 1.000 |

The depositors are the difference. The equity basket pays an exit in BUCKs. The pro-rata basket
returns TOKEN. The model's depositors were written for the pro-rata basket, and they did nothing
with what an exit paid them. So on the equity basket each one came in with fresh dollars, left
with BUCKs, and kept the BUCKs for good. After 124 exits they held 9 million. In five years
there are over a thousand exits.

That is a demand for BUCKs that nothing can satisfy. The basket must find BUCKs for every exit,
and they never come back to the market. The BUCK turns dear, the savers sell what they have,
K climbs to make room for issuers, the issuers are used up, and then nothing is left. It is the
whole course of the seven failing worlds.

It is not why K climbs in the worlds that held. The base world has no depositor who ever
leaves, and K still ends at 0.92 under the older upkeep. That has another cause: see
[[*The settled model][the settled model]].

It is a fault of the model and not of either basket. A depositor paid in BUCKs spends them or
sells them. The model's depositors now sell what an exit pays them for dollars, through the
BUCK/dollar pool, a little each tick. In the first 120 days of the same world they sold 2.4
million, held almost none, and K stayed at 0.747.

basket costing 3.5 BUCKs. K is thrown down to 0.42 on the first day, climbs to its limit within
the month, and nothing afterwards pulls it down, because the BUCK is then at parity. That is a
question about these worlds' opening and about a controller that rests wherever its error

these worlds, 2 to 3, are decided by the same first weeks and do not compare the upkeeps.

changed with the depositors, and it shows what the basket itself does to K. A basket that holds

its equity. It buys BUCKs whenever the BUCK is a little cheap, which here is half the time, and
has no rule that lets them go again when the BUCK is a little dear. This world says the lean
against parity, at a gain of 5, is too strong or should not be there. The worlds where the BUCK
is cheap for long are where it was meant to help, and the lab's history shape is the only one
measured.

The rest of the matrix is running. The sections above this one, and the lean's tables below,
were measured with depositors who kept their BUCKs.

** The same worlds without the lean against parity

Three of the worlds were run again on the machine with its lean against parity switched off:
the book then aims to have let out nothing, whatever the BUCK's price.

- The lean does not cause the rise, and taking it away does not prevent it. The BUCK still goes
  to 8 and 11 dollars.
- The lean does postpone it: K reaches its limit two to five months later with the lean than
  without.
- The lean is most of what the machine's depositors lose. Without it a share ends worth half a
  basket in the portcast world in place of a fifth, and two baskets in the other in place of
  three quarters.
- Even without the lean the machine has BUCKs out while the BUCK rises, up to 30% of its equity
  in the portcast world. It aims at none. But its positions give up BUCKs as the TOKEN
  cheapens, faster than it buys them back. That is why a share still ends at half a basket
  there, where the older upkeep's, which holds more BUCKs than it has let out, ends at one.
- In the world that holds, the lean costs. Without it the machine's share ends level with the
  older upkeep's, 1.246 against 1.249, where with it the machine was 1.8% behind. And K ends
  lowest of all, 0.745.

The lab shows the other side of the lean. In its history shape the BUCK is cheap for most of the
120 days, and the lean has the basket hold BUCKs. With the whole system running the basket ends
1,860 behind doing nothing. With the lean off it ends 37,689 behind, the BUCK sits 2.4% from its
basket in place of 1.4%, and K falls to 0.59 in place of 0.66.

So the lean helps when the BUCK is cheap and harms when it is dear, and the two are not alike. A
basket that holds cheap BUCKs can lose only what it paid. A basket that has let out dear BUCKs
loses without bound while they keep rising. In these runs the dear side costs a little where
the BUCK holds and most of a share where it does not, and what it buys is a delay. A lean on
the cheap side only is the obvious thing to try.

K stays away from its limit under the machine because the machine does not hoard BUCKs as the
older upkeep does, not because it leans.

On this the lean was made one-sided (2026-10-12): the book holds BUCKs while they are cheap and
lets none out because they are dear. The same three worlds, and the savings world, on that

* Why deposits waited

The first five-year run of the machine was not like this. It placed none of its deposits for 70
days, and after a year a sixth of its book was still TOKEN in a wallet. TOKEN waiting, as a
share of equity, real prices:

| Upkeep | Day 30 | Day 90 | Day 180 | Day 364 |
|--------+--------+--------+---------+---------|
| Older | 6.6% | 0.0% | 0.2% | 0.0% |
| Machine as first built | 47.8% | 42.2% | 23.3% | 16.7% |
| With placing no longer held back by a leash sale | 45.8% | 33.2% | 15.6% | 12.7% |
| The same, and a reserve of one exit | 42.1% | 26.3% | 20.7% | 16.1% |
| A trial that sold half of what waited | 10.8% | 5.0% | 0.0% | 0.0% |
| A trial of the net mark | 0.1% | 0.0% | 0.0% | 0.0% |
| The machine as rebuilt | 0.0% | 0.0% | 0.0% | 0.0% |

We peeled it in layers.

- /The turn./ Depositors bring an even mix, which put gold far above its weight. Every turn
  was spent selling gold, and a turn that sold placed nothing. Fixing that helped a little.
- /The reserve./ The basket kept a fifth of its assets unspent after a large deposit, for
  three months. A smaller reserve helped a little more.
- /The pace./ It placed one TOKEN a turn, under limits meant for swaps, though placing swaps
  nothing.
- /The credit./ Under all of these, the limit. Pairing a BUCK of TOKEN takes a BUCK. With K at
  0.75 the basket could pair three quarters of its book and no more.

The older upkeep gets round the limit by selling half of what credit does not cover. That
works, and it costs. On reverting prices the share price after a year was 1.075 with a fifth of
the book still waiting, and 1.049 when all of it was sold and placed at once.

* The net mark

A BUCK the basket owes and a BUCK it holds are the same thing. Holding both carries no risk.
So the limit should count only what it has let out:

#+begin_example
BUCKs drawn - BUCKs still held beside its own TOKEN  <=  K x equity
#+end_example

| How the limit counts | Pairs | Left for exits | Most TOKEN it can hold |
|----------------------+-------+----------------+------------------------|
| Every BUCK drawn (as it was) | K of the book | what pairing leaves | 1.75 x equity |
| Only BUCKs let out (now) | all of it | all of K x equity | 1.75 x equity |
| Everything it owns as collateral | all of it | 3 x equity | 4 x equity |

The last row is the loop that lending protocols offer. We did not take it. The middle row gives
what we wanted and risks no more than before.

With it:

- A deposit is paired on the next turn, in full, by issuing. Nothing is sold.
- An exit is paid in BUCKs. One exit of up to 43% of the book fits. What it leaves over is sold
  down on later turns.
- A cut in K forces no sale. It lowers a ceiling: in normal running the basket has let out at
  most half of K times equity.
- A sale and a purchase no longer have to meet. Each TOKEN is sold when it is high and bought
  when it is low, and the credit carries the difference.
- The size of the whole book leans against the BUCK's own price: it holds BUCKs when the BUCK
  is cheap and issues them when it is dear.

In five years the machine had let out between -5% and +14% of its equity, never near the
ceiling of 37%.

Three costs. After a large exit the depositors who stay hold the leavers' TOKEN until it is
sold. A TOKEN that collapses takes a third more BUCKs with it, because all of its position is
paired. And relief accrues on a lien twice the size: see below.

** Relief and demurrage

The basket's lien earns relief, 2% a year. The BUCKs it holds in its pools carry demurrage, 2%
a year. Over time the two cancel, because BUCKs that leave a pool take their share of it with
them.

But the books count relief as it accrues, and the demurrage only when the BUCKs come back. So
a share price runs ahead by what its held BUCKs carry. After five years that was 2.9% of equity
for the older upkeep and 2.7% to 3.6% for the machine. The table above gives the share price
net of it. The machine is still ahead by 3.9% and 6.7%.

* Leaning against price

Each TOKEN's target is its declared weight, lowered when its price stands above its reference
price and raised when it stands below. The reference is the price the TOKEN joined the basket
at. Governance can change it. A target never leans by more than 15% of its declared weight.

The size of the lean is one number, the tilt. At one half it asks for what someone holding the
TOKEN in a wallet, and restoring the declared weights, would hold: it undoes the pools' damping.
The lab runs at one half. Gain over 120 days, basket alone ([[file:review/lean-trial.py][lean-trial.py]]):

| Prices | No lean | Tilt 1/4 | Tilt 1/2 | Tilt 1 |
|--------+---------+----------+----------+--------|
| Clean cycles | +54,908 | +76,611 | +96,241 | +131,337 |
| Cycles with noise | +53,011 | +76,517 | +99,564 | +143,573 |
| The sudden jump | +54,271 | +76,131 | +97,051 | +133,508 |
| Commodity-like | +20,764 | +26,888 | +34,633 | +47,822 |
| One TOKEN reprices for good | -3,940 | -5,803 | -7,787 | -11,410 |

Every step up in the tilt buys more of both: more gain where prices return, more loss where one
does not. At one half the loss on the one-way move is 0.7% of the basket.

The rebuild lifted every column on cycles by a third or more, the column with no lean included,
and cut the loss on the one-way move by a quarter or more.

The lean trusts the reference. When a TOKEN reprices for good the reference is wrong, and the
basket stays short of that TOKEN, by at most the 15%, until someone corrects it.

* Urgency

A fixed line is the wrong shape for deciding which TOKENs may be rebalanced. A share that is
1.9 points off for a month never acts. One that touches 2.1 for an hour does.

So each TOKEN keeps a count. Every day the count grows by how far its share is from its target,
less a quarter point. When the count reaches a set level, that TOKEN may be rebalanced. A big
gap gets there soon. A small one gets there if it lasts. The set level is the /patience/, in
point-days. The lab runs at 2.

A trade does not start the count again: an urgent gap stays open until the share is back within
the quarter point.

With that change, how long the patience is matters little. Whether there is a count at all
matters a good deal. Whole system running:

* A sudden jump

One test lifts one TOKEN's price by 30% for ten days and then drops it back
([[file:review/shock-response.py][shock-response.py]]). Under the earlier rule the basket sold that TOKEN once in those ten days,
for 10,561 BUCKs.

It now sells eight times in two days, 24,897 BUCKs in all, and comes out 15,430 ahead of a
basket that sat still. The jump was worth 96,800 while it lasted, so it keeps about a sixth.
Under the earlier rule it kept a twelfth.

| Rule | Sales of the TOKEN | BUCKs | Gain across the jump |
|------+--------------------+-------+----------------------|
| As built | 8 | 24,897 | +15,430 |
| No lean | 6 | 15,339 | +7,325 |
| Tilt 1 | 9 | 32,832 | +21,478 |
| 2-point line | 5 | 24,960 | +13,425 |
| 2-point line, no lean | 5 | 18,770 | +4,220 |

Before the rebuild it sold 14,870 BUCKs' worth and came out 16,622 ahead. It now sells two
thirds more for a slightly smaller gain.

Three things still hold it back.

- It waits a day. The pools show the new price at once, but the policy will not sell a TOKEN
  it still reads as rising, and its filter takes a day to see that the rise has stopped.
- The pools sold first. A liquidity position sells a TOKEN as its price rises. The basket's
  shed 355 TOKEN on the way up, at an average of 108 BUCKs on the way to 116. That is two
  thirds more than its own sales then sold. In a wallet the TOKEN would have stood at 36.3% of
  the book. In the pools it stood at 33.7%.
- It sells only to its target. By the third day the TOKEN was back at its leaned target, and
  the basket sold no more of it while the price stayed high.

When the price dropped back the basket bought the TOKEN again within a day.

* Realistic prices

The idea behind the BUCK is that basic commodities follow inflation over the long run, each
with its own delay. We checked this against fifty years of data. Six of eight series stayed
within 0.3% a year of US inflation. Gold ran 1.7% a year ahead. They wander a long way in
between, for years at a time.

So we built a test with three made-up commodities that behave this way: a fast one like gold,
a middle one like energy, a slow one like wages. They share a rising price level and wander
around it independently. One day in the test stands for one month, so a run covers a decade.

Over the run the basket of commodities came to cost 47% more dollars. The BUCK kept pace: it
ended at 1.42 dollars, where its basket cost 1.42, and stayed within 0.3% of it on average. The
desk never had to act. That is the main result. (The BUCK opened 3.5% too dear, because the
pools start at long-run prices, and was within 1% in under three days.)

Rebalancing added 2.3% over the decade. Steering to declared weights it added 1.2%, and with
the old 2-point line and no lean, 0.3%. Real prices do not swing far enough, often enough, for
more. On this evidence the basket's job is to hold value through inflation. Rebalancing is a
small bonus unless the things in the basket swing much more than these do.

* Arbitrage

The basket can own the arbitrage between its pools and the outside market. In the earlier lab
that looked like the biggest win: +18,739 on clean cycles. Most of it was an artifact. The
lab's outside market was one pool for each TOKEN, which the arbitrageur itself pushed off the
feed, so the basket's prices sat 1.3% from the feed and a gap was always waiting.

The outside market is now too deep to move ([[file:review/price-forcing.py][price-forcing.py]]). The basket's prices sit 0.66%
from the feed, which is the three pools' fees. Owning the arbitrage is then worth:

| Prices | Gain from owning the arbitrage |
|--------+--------------------------------|
| Clean cycles | +7,076 |
| Cycles with noise | +19,042 |
| Flat, noisy | +5,209 |
| Commodity-like | +8,238 |

It is real income, and noise makes more of it. But it still comes from the way the lab steps
prices, with one arbitrageur and no competitor. Before the rebuild, with hourly steps in place
of six-hour ones, it fell to a fifth on clean cycles and doubled on noisy ones, because the lab
draws its noise at every step. That was not rerun. Do not count on it.

* Gas

Nothing above charges gas. Here is what the work costs, measured over a run on clean cycles
with everything switched on ([[file:review/wheel-gas.py][wheel-gas.py]]). The lab turns the wheel four times a day.

| Call | Gas each | Gas a day |
|------+----------+-----------|
| Wheel turn with a trade | 1,900,000 | 7.4 million |
| Wheel turn, nothing traded | 1,000,000 | 0.1 million |
| Price observation | 160,000 | 0.7 million |
| Arbitrage turn | 500,000 | 40 million |

The basket's own upkeep is about 8 million gas a day, nearly all of it trading. Arbitrage uses
five times that, because the deep market lets the lab's arbitrageur turn 78 times a day. It
pays its own way out of what it captures. The rebuild changed these figures little.

What this costs depends on the chain. At 1 gwei, 8 million gas is 0.008 ETH a day, or about
1 ETH over a run. At 10 gwei it is ten times that. On a rollup it is close to nothing.

Gas does not grow with the basket. The gain does. So there is a size below which upkeep costs
more than rebalancing earns, and that size is much smaller on a rollup than on mainnet. For
this one-million basket the gain was 96,623 BUCKs on clean cycles and 33,263 on commodity-like
prices. Price the gas for your chain and compare.

A turn that trades uses about 900,000 more gas than one that does not. It makes two trades on
average and earns about 200 BUCKs. That sets the smallest trade worth making, and today nothing
enforces it: the basket trades whenever a gap is open, however small the piece. Turned every
block, it would trade every block.

On commodity-like prices two turns in five trade nothing, and those still use 1.8 million gas
a day beside the trading turns' 3.7 million. A turn that trades nothing re-reads every pool and
re-asks the policy about every constituent. It must cost far less.

The wheel already knows how to pay its callers. It holds a reserve of BUCKs, fed by a slice of
the basket's income, and pays each caller who does real work a fixed share of what is in it.
If nobody calls, the reserve grows until someone does. If everybody calls, each gets less. The
price finds itself, and the basket never needs to know the gas price or the price of ETH. What
the basket spends is fixed by the slice. What that buys, in turns a day, is set by the gas
price.

This is agreed as the way to pay, with a tenth of the fees the basket collects and of the
arbitrage credited to it as the slice. It is not built yet. Nothing in the contracts sends the
slice to the reserve, and the lab has the reserve switched off. Building that, so that every
result is net of what callers were paid, comes next.

* What we do not know yet

- Whether two seeds are enough. They agree on the five-year result. The lab has one.
- Why K rests at its limit in three worlds that hold. Their opening throws the BUCK far from
  parity, K is carried to 0.95 in a month, and at parity nothing brings it back.
- What the lean against parity is worth on the settled model. Its tables were measured in
  worlds where the BUCK was made dear by the depositors' hoard.
- What the basket does if the BUCK does rise without stopping. Its limit is K times an equity
  counted in BUCKs, so it would buy BUCKs all the way up. No world with a sound cast now does
  this, so it is no longer first.
- What the desk does. It hardly acted in the first seed of the savings world and not at all in
  the lab, where the BUCK never stays outside its band. In the second seed it moved half a
  million BUCKs and came out about even. It needs runs built to take the BUCK outside its band
  and hold it there.
- What the results look like net of paying the callers, and what the smallest trade should be.
- Whether the lean against parity is the right size, and whether half of K times equity is the
  right ceiling. Both are first values.
- Whether equity should deduct the demurrage on held BUCKs as it accrues.
- Whether urgency's extra gain over a plain line survives the cost of its extra trades.
- How the machine behaves at real speed. The lab's cycles are far faster than real commodities.
- What holds the BUCK's price inside 3% of its basket. The basket's lean helps, K is slow, and
  the desk waits outside.
- What a run of large exits does, and a cut in K to its floor.
