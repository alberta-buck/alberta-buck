#+TITLE: The basket machine: what the lab shows
#+DATE: 2026-10-10
#+OPTIONS: ^:nil toc:2 num:t

This is what we learned by running [[file:BASKET-MACHINE.org][the basket machine]] on the real contracts. The work still to
do is in [[file:BASKET-MACHINE-TASKS.org][the task list]]. Every number can be rebuilt with the commands at the end.

* The short version

The basket now makes money from prices that swing apart and come back. It did not before: it
moved too little, too late. With one change to how much it moves, it earns about ten times more.

What it may rebalance is now decided by urgency that builds up. A big gap acts soon. A small one
acts if it lasts. On realistic prices this earns more than twice what a fixed threshold did.

The BUCK itself held up well. When its basket of commodities rose 47% in dollars, the BUCK
kept pace.

Three things to be careful about. The income from arbitrage is mostly an artifact of how the lab
steps prices. Rebalancing slowly turns the basket's spare TOKEN into BUCKs, which pushes the
BUCK's price up when nobody is issuing new ones. And when a TOKEN moves and never comes back,
the new rule loses more than the old threshold did.

* How to read the numbers

A run is 120 days with three TOKENs and a basket that starts with one million.

Every gain below is measured against the same basket doing no rebalancing. So +38,234 means the
basket ended 38,234 richer than it would have by sitting still.

We value the basket "at reference": its TOKEN at outside prices, its BUCKs at what the BUCK is
supposed to be worth. We do this because in some runs nothing holds the BUCK's price steady, and
then gains measured in BUCKs and in dollars can point in opposite directions.

No gas is charged. Keep that in mind wherever a result depends on many small trades.
The [[*Gas][gas section]] says what it would cost.

* Rebalancing

The basket sells a TOKEN that has become expensive against another and buys the cheap one. It
waits for evidence that the gap has stopped widening.

The first version waited too long and then moved too little. Over 120 days it moved 36,307
BUCKs out of a book of 1.72 million. The typical trade was 231 BUCKs.

We made the amount it moves a setting, and raised it. Then we replaced the fixed threshold that
decided which TOKENs it may touch with urgency. Gain over 120 days:

| Prices | First version | More moved | With urgency | With urgency, whole system running |
|--------+---------------+------------+--------------+------------------------------------|
| Clean cycles | +4,059 | +35,300 | +35,541 | +38,234 |
| Cycles with noise | +3,807 | +34,495 | +35,938 | +38,180 |
| Flat, noisy | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| One TOKEN reprices for good | -232 | -1,265 | -4,446 | -4,953 |
| Commodity-like | +210 | +4,506 | +13,833 | +16,307 |

Three things to notice. On cycles the gain is about 3.8% in 120 days. On flat prices it does
nothing, which is right. And when a TOKEN moves and never comes back, rebalancing loses,
because it sold on the way.

For scale: someone holding the same TOKENs in a wallet, rebalancing by a simple rule, could
have made about 8% on the clean cycles. The basket gets less because most of its TOKEN sits in
liquidity pools, which damp how far its shares move.

* Urgency

The basket used to act on a TOKEN only once its share was 2 points off target. That is the
wrong shape. A share that is 1.9 points off for a month never acts. One that touches 2.1 for an
hour does. On commodity-like prices shares are 2 points off only 4% of the time, and 1 point off
70% of the time.

So each TOKEN now keeps a count. Every day the count grows by how far its share is off target,
less a quarter point. When the count reaches a set level, that TOKEN may be rebalanced. After a
transfer its count starts again from zero. A big gap gets there soon. A small one gets there if
it lasts.

The set level is the one knob. We call it /patience/, in point-days. At a patience of 2, a share
2 points off waits about a day, and a share 1 point off waits under three. The lab runs at 2.

With the whole system running:

| Commodity-like | 2-point line | +6,915 | 16 | 432 |

On commodity-like prices, a patience of 2 earns more than twice what the 2-point line did. It
earns more than a 1-point line too, with a third of the transfers. On clean cycles it changes
little. On noisy cycles it earns a little less than the 2-point line: 38,180 against 39,878.

When a TOKEN never comes back it loses more: over twice what the 2-point line lost, and a
little more than a 1-point line. That is the price of acting on small gaps.

More patience means fewer and larger transfers and a smaller loss in the bad case. Too much
gives up the harvest on fast cycles: at 8, a third of it.

Gain per transfer is the number to watch once callers are paid. A transfer adds about 840,000
gas to a turn. A 1-point line earns 90 to 150 BUCKs a transfer. Patience 2 earns about 470.

Two cautions. This is one price path for each shape, and neighbouring settings do not line up
neatly, so read the table for its shape and treat 2 as a starting value. And a version that did
not start the count again after a transfer was no better than a low line: it made at least as
many transfers as the lowest line, each earning about 90.

Before building the rule we tried it from outside, by steering the old threshold turn by turn
([[file:review/urgency-trial.py][urgency-trial.py]]). The built rule gives much the same answers.

* Realistic prices

The idea behind the BUCK is that basic commodities follow inflation over the long run, each
with its own delay. We checked this against fifty years of data. Six of eight series stayed
within 0.3% a year of US inflation. Gold ran 1.7% a year ahead. They wander a long way in
between, for years at a time.

So we built a test with three made-up commodities that behave this way: a fast one like gold,
a middle one like energy, a slow one like wages. They share a rising price level and wander
around it independently. One day in the test stands for one month, so a run covers a decade.

Over the run the basket of commodities came to cost 47% more dollars. The BUCK kept pace: it
ended at 1.42 dollars, where its basket cost 1.42, and stayed within 0.4% of it on average. The
desk never had to act. That is the main result. (The BUCK opened 3.5% too dear, because the pools
start at long-run prices, and closed most of that gap in two or three days.)

Rebalancing added 1.6% over the decade. With the fixed threshold it was 0.7%. Real prices do not
swing far enough, often enough, for more. On this evidence the basket's job is to hold value
through inflation. Rebalancing is a small bonus unless the things in the basket swing much more
than these do.

* Arbitrage

Giving the basket the arbitrage profit looked like the biggest win: +18,739 on clean cycles.
Do not count on it. Nearly all of that profit appears right after the lab moves prices, which it
does in six-hour jumps. With hourly jumps the same profit falls to a fifth. A real market moves
continuously and has competing arbitrageurs.

The rebalancing gain is not like that. With hourly jumps it is a little larger: +39,879 on
clean cycles against +35,541.

* A side effect

When the basket rebalances, half of what it buys goes in as BUCKs beside the TOKEN. Over about
90 days this turns its spare TOKEN into BUCKs: it ends up holding 13% of its value in BUCKs.

Those BUCKs have to come from somewhere. Clean cycles, 120 days:

| Who else is in the market | K at the end | BUCK price at the end |
|---------------------------+--------------+-----------------------|
| Nobody | 0.750 | 1.085 |
| K alone | 0.950 | 1.064 |
| K and the desk | 0.950 | 1.031 |
| A seller of BUCKs | 0.682 | 0.983 |
| Buyers and sellers | 0.762 | 1.008 |

With someone issuing BUCKs, all is well. Without, the BUCK becomes 6% to 9% too expensive and K
runs to its limit. We need to decide whether the basket should do this at all.

* Gas

Nothing above charges gas. Here is what the work costs, measured over a run with everything
switched on ([[file:review/wheel-gas.py][wheel-gas.py]]). The lab turns the wheel four times a day.

| Call | Gas each | Gas a day |
|------+----------+-----------|
| Wheel turn, nothing traded | 930,000 | 3.1 million |
| Wheel turn with a transfer | 1,770,000 | 1.2 million |
| Price observation | 160,000 | 0.6 million |
| Arbitrage turn | 460,000 | 9.6 million |

The basket's own upkeep is about 5 million gas a day. Arbitrage uses twice that, but it pays
its own way out of what it captures.

What this costs depends on the chain. At 1 gwei, 5 million gas is 0.005 ETH a day, or 0.6 ETH
over a run. At 10 gwei it is ten times that. On a rollup it is close to nothing.

Gas does not grow with the basket. The gain does. So there is a size below which upkeep costs
more than rebalancing earns, and that size is much smaller on a rollup than on mainnet. For
this one-million basket the gain was 38,234 BUCKs on clean cycles and 16,307 on commodity-like
prices. Price the gas for your chain and compare.

Most of that gas is spent looking, not trading. On commodity-like prices the 35 transfers added
29 million gas between them. The other 513 million was the cost of looking. And that run
squeezes a decade into 120 days. At real speed, four turns a day would mean thirty times as
much looking for the same 35 transfers.

So two things follow. A transfer adds about 840,000 gas to a turn, which sets the smallest
trade worth making. And a turn that trades nothing must cost far less than 930,000. Today it
re-reads every pool and re-asks the policy about every pair.

The wheel already knows how to pay its callers. It holds a reserve of BUCKs, fed by a slice of
the basket's income, and pays each caller who does real work a fixed share of what is in it.
If nobody calls, the reserve grows until someone does. If everybody calls, each gets less. The
price finds itself, and the basket never needs to know the gas price or the price of ETH. What
the basket spends is fixed by the slice. What that buys, in turns a day, is set by the gas
price.

This is agreed as the way to pay, and it is not built yet. Nothing in the contracts sends the
basket's slice to the reserve, and the lab has the reserve switched off. Building that, so that
every result is net of what callers were paid, comes next.

* What we do not know yet

- How the old basket and desk compare on the same tests.
- What the results look like net of paying the callers.
- Whether a patience of 2 is right. It was chosen on one price path of each shape.
- Whether one seed and one bad case are representative.
- How the machine behaves at real speed. The cycles are far faster than real commodities.
