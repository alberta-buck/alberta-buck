#+begin_abstract
A currency is only as steady as what it is tied to.  The Alberta Buck (BUCK) is tied to a basket of
base commodities -- energy, metals, food -- whose prices are held in place, over the long run, by
physics and by human ingenuity rather than by anyone's promise.  This article is about that
basket: why it has to be heavy, why the things in it keep coming back, and how the *BuckBasket*
turns their coming back into a return for anyone who parks savings in it.

It is written for everyone.  The machinery, and the arithmetic behind each claim, is in the
companion paper, [[file:alberta-buck-ethereum-wheel.org][The BasketWheel]].
([[https://perry.kundert.ca/images/alberta-buck-ethereum-basket.pdf][PDF]], [[https://perry.kundert.ca/images/alberta-buck-ethereum-basket.txt][Text]])

* Anchored to what?
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: heavy-anchor
:END:

Every kind of money eventually has to answer one question: /anchored to what?/  Gold standards
tied paper to a metal.  Modern money is tied to a committee's judgement.  Stablecoins are tied to
the dollar, which is tied to the same committee.

The BUCK is tied to a basket of the things every economy runs on, and it is measured against that
basket /directly/: one BUCK is, by definition, one basket's worth.  There is no dollar in between.
That matters more than it sounds.  Measure yourself with a tape measure that shrinks a little every
year and you will conclude that you are growing.  Measure a currency in dollars and it inherits the
dollar's shrinkage without anyone noticing.  So the BUCK measures itself against the basket, and a
slow on-chain controller adjusts how many BUCKs the system lets people draw, so that the two stay
together.

For that to work the basket has to be *heavy*.  A light anchor is worse than none: one big trade,
a thin market or a passing fashion could drag it.  A heavy one -- deep, wide, and constantly traded
against the rest of the world -- barely moves.  Its weight is not a side effect.  It is the
product.

* Why basics come back
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: argument-from-physics
:END:

A share can compound to the moon or fall to zero; nothing in nature forbids either.  A bushel of
wheat is different, and the difference is physical.

The price of a basic commodity lives between a floor and a ceiling that markets cannot escape for
long:

- *The floor is thermodynamic.*  You cannot mine copper, smelt iron or grow wheat for less than the
  energy it physically takes.  Sell below that and production stops, supply shrinks, and the price
  climbs back.  The periodic table enforces the floor.
- *The ceiling is substitution.*  When a basic gets dear, people switch (aluminium for copper, gas
  for oil), conserve, recycle, or do without.  Demand bends.  Human ingenuity, which is plentiful
  and impatient, enforces the ceiling.

Between them the real price of a basic -- in hours of work, or joules of energy -- wanders but does
not run away.  Over years it is noisy.  Over generations it is remarkably flat: a barrel of oil, a
bushel of wheat and an ounce of silver have bought roughly the same amount of human effort for a
very long time.

Three things can break this: acts of God, war, and autocrats who seize a market by force.  All
three are real, and all three are historically local and temporary.  A /wide/ basket of
independent basics is diversified against each.  Coming back is not a hope about the future; it is
what the physical economy does unless someone is shooting at it.

* A pantry, not a portfolio
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: facility-not-fund
:END:

The simplest way to picture the BuckBasket is as a shared pantry.

You bring something to it -- a commodity token, or BUCKs -- and you are given a share of the whole
pantry for it.  From then on your share rises and falls with everything in the pantry together, not
just with what you brought.  When you want out, you hand back your share and receive its worth in
BUCKs, whenever you like (or, on a day the basket is short of BUCKs, your slice of what it holds).
There is no lock-up, no notice period, and nobody to ask.

Two small rules keep it fair to the people already inside:

- *You can't buy into yesterday's harvest.*  A newcomer's goods are valued at the cautious end of
  the market and the pantry at the generous end, so nobody joins at a price that quietly takes a
  slice of what others have earned.  Leaving works the same way in mirror.  The difference is a
  small fee, about what it costs to trade.
- *The house takes a quarter of your winnings and none of your losses.*  When you leave with more
  than you brought, a quarter of the gain goes to the basket's treasury.  If you leave with less,
  it takes nothing.

There is no fund manager, no forecast and no opinion about the market.  The BUCK system cannot
offer professional asset management and should never pretend to.  What it offers is a published,
mechanical process that anyone can inspect: less a fund than a mill, whose wheel turns because the
water runs.

* Where the return comes from
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: shannons-demon
:END:

Here is the part that surprises people.  A basket of things that wander but come back does not
merely hold its value.  Kept in balance, it /earns/.

#+BEGIN_SRC text
  Two assets, each just wobbling around a flat average (no trend at all):

     hold either one alone ................ you end where you started
     hold half of each, kept at half ...... you end UP

  Keeping the halves even means selling a little of whatever just rose
  and buying a little of whatever just fell: buy low, sell high, done
  mechanically on every wobble.  The wobbles themselves pay.
#+END_SRC

Claude Shannon described this decades ago, and it has gone by many names since: /Shannon's demon/,
/volatility harvesting/, /the rebalancing premium/.  It pays most when the parts are volatile, keep
coming back, and do not move together -- which is a fair description of energy, metals and food.
An oil shock is not a wheat shock is not a copper shock.

The BuckBasket earns from three places:

1. *Tolls.*  Each commodity in the basket sits in a public trading pool, paired with BUCKs.  Anyone
   in the world who trades through it pays a small fee, and the fee is the basket's.
2. *Keeping its own prices straight.*  Every time a commodity's price moves elsewhere, the basket's
   pool is briefly out of date.  Early in this design we believed the world's arbitrageurs fixed
   that for free.  They do fix it, but not for free: whoever brings a stale pool back to the market
   price pockets the difference, and in a market that keeps coming back that difference is almost
   exactly the rebalancing premium above.  The demon works for whoever gets there first.  So the
   basket goes for it too: it corrects its own pools, through a loop of three trades that needs no
   money up front and cannot lose, and keeps the change.
3. *Leaning against the tides.*  New money does not reach every price at once.  It reaches
   money-like assets first, energy and building materials in about a year, and wages and food a
   year or two after that -- so the basket's commodities drift apart and come home again on a tide
   about a year long.  A trading pool harvests the chop, the day-to-day wobbles, but it cannot
   harvest a tide at all.  Only a deliberate lean can.  So each commodity's share has a target
   that leans against its price: a commodity that is dear against the others is held a little
   light, and one that is cheap a little heavy.  The basket works toward those targets slowly,
   and it waits.  It does not sell a commodity that is still pulling away from the rest.  /Let a
   run run/, then lean against it once it stops.

The details -- how the three trades close a gap, how the basket decides a tide has stopped, and
the simulations behind every claim here -- are in [[file:alberta-buck-ethereum-wheel.org][The BasketWheel]].

* The world's traders, working for the basket
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: arbitrage-engine
:END:

The basket's pools sit in the open sea of decentralised finance.  A trader hunting for a cheaper
route from dollars to gold neither knows nor cares what a BUCK is.  If the cheapest route runs
dollars to BUCKs to gold, they take it, and pay the basket's toll on the way.  In our simulations
such BUCK-unaware traders route through the basket's pools as a matter of course.

That is why weight and traffic reinforce each other.  The deeper and wider the basket, the more
routes run through it, the more tolls it collects, and the harder it is to push.  A heavy basket is
a busy basket, and a busy basket pays.

* The basket is a customer too
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: how-it-works
:END:

Everyone in the Alberta Buck system draws BUCKs the same way: against something of value, up to a
fraction of it.  That fraction is called =K=, and it is the one number the on-chain controller
moves to keep the BUCK steady against the basket.

The basket gets no special privileges.  It is an ordinary account drawing on a credit like
anyone else's, and its collateral is its depositors' share of everything it holds.

One thing about it is worth understanding, because the rest follows from it.  A trading pool
needs two sides: the commodity, and BUCKs to trade it against.  The basket supplies the BUCK side
itself, by issuing BUCKs on its credit.  But a BUCK that it issues and sets down in a pool beside
its own commodity has not gone anywhere.  The basket still holds it.  So that BUCK does not count
against its limit.  =K= limits only the BUCKs the basket has /let out/ into the world.

Three good things come of this:

- *Your deposit goes to work at once.*  Whatever you bring is paired with BUCKs the basket issues
  for the purpose.  Nothing is sold to make room for it.
- *You are paid before anything is sold.*  When you leave, the basket pays you in BUCKs from its
  credit, and sells a little of what it holds afterwards, in its own time.  It keeps half of its
  limit free for exactly this.
- *It never borrows to buy more.*  The most it can come to hold is what its depositors put in,
  plus what =K= lets it issue against that.  It does not pledge the same goods twice.

When =K= falls, the limit shrinks; nothing already issued is called back.  There are no margin
calls, in the basket or anywhere else in the system.

The basket also keeps one eye on the BUCK itself.  When the BUCK is cheap against the basket, it
buys some and holds them.  When the BUCK is dear, it lets a few more out.  Both steady the price.
The second is the riskier, since a dear BUCK can go on rising, so the basket never lets out more
than half of what =K= allows.

The upkeep -- pairing new deposits, collecting tolls, selling what is over its target and buying
what is under -- happens on a /work wheel/: a small machine that anyone may turn with a
transaction, and that pays whoever turns it out of what the turning captures.  There is no keeper
on a salary.  If nobody turns it, new deposits wait in the basket's wallet and it earns less;
nothing already in it is put at risk.

* A tugboat beside the tanker
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: the-desk
:END:

The controller that moves =K= steers like a supertanker.  It works only through how much credit
people may draw, and a book of credit takes months to turn.  Someone with deep pockets can shove
the BUCK off course in days.

So beside the basket there is a /monetary desk/: a tugboat.  Most days it does nothing at all.
Small wanderings of the BUCK are left to people who profit by closing them, to the basket's own
habit of leaning against the price, and to =K=.  The desk wakes only when the BUCK is more than
three percent from its basket.  Then it buys BUCKs that are cheap, or sells BUCKs that are dear, a
little at a time, with what it already holds.  It issues new BUCKs only when the BUCK has stayed
dear for thirty days running.  That is how a central bank splits the work between its policy rate
and its open-market desk.

The desk has hard limits, and each one is there because an earlier desk needed it.  It may trade
only so much in its whole life, and nothing refills that budget.  It stops when its own capital
falls to a floor.  And it may not push the price back through the band: its job is to lean on a
runaway, not to do =K='s work for it.  An early version of ours had none of these.  It seemed to
profit from everything, right up until we noticed it had quietly bought half the pool it traded
in.  A desk that seems to profit from everything has not been tested properly.

The desk is a separate household, with its own account and its own credit.  Nothing it holds or
owes appears in the basket's books.  We learned that one the honest way, when a simulated desk's
capital briefly counted toward the depositors' borrowing, and a test now guards the wall between
them.

It is also the least proven part of the design.  In our simulated economies the BUCK has seldom
strayed far enough to wake it, so it has had little to do.  The details are in
[[file:alberta-buck-ethereum-wheel.org::#desk][The BasketWheel]].

* Yield against demurrage
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: yield-vs-demurrage
:END:

The BUCK is designed to /circulate/.  Idle balances pay a small holding fee, demurrage, which keeps
money moving (see [[file:alberta-buck-demurrage.org][Demurrage and the Jubilee Fund]]).  That is
good for the economy and mildly annoying for a saver, who would like a rainy-day fund to at least
hold its ground.

The BuckBasket is the saver's answer.  Savings parked in it earn the tolls and the harvest instead
of paying demurrage, and remain available on demand.  In a simulated economy that ran for five
years on the real price histories of five commodities, a share of the basket ended 17% to 29%
ahead of simply holding the same commodities: three to five percent a year.  Where prices came
home more faithfully it was five to seven.  Where a price wanders off and does not come back, the
basket loses a little instead.  Treat those as a simulation's numbers, not a promise.

It is a medium-to-long-term proposition.  On the day you leave, a commodity may be below its
average, and you get what the basket is worth that day.  Two things shrink that risk: *time*,
because coming back is the physical default, and *width*, because the more independent basics the
basket holds, the smaller the chance that they are all down at once.  The basket rewards patience
and diversification -- exactly the habits a stable money should encourage.

* What it is for
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: what-its-for
:END:

Anywhere money has to stay available but should not sit idle:

- *a household's savings account*, earning the basket's harvest instead of paying demurrage;
- *a business's working cash*, kept liquid for payroll and suppliers, and working in between;
- *an insurer's reserves*, which prudence requires to be both available and preserved -- the two
  things the basket is built to be at once.

* The one real risk: the asset that never comes back
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: the-asset-that-never-reverts
:END:

Every honest design names the case in which it loses.  The BuckBasket's is simple.

Rebalancing pays /because/ things come back.  If something in the basket simply rises forever, the
basket keeps dutifully trimming the one thing it should have held, and a plain holder of that asset
does better, permanently.

This is not hypothetical; it is the explicit pitch of an entire asset class.  An asset designed to
be scarce, like Bitcoin, is built to rise and not come back -- "number go up" is the whole idea.
Put much of it in the basket and you have installed a gear that turns the wrong way in a machine
whose every gear assumes coming back.  The rebalancing premium and the ever-scarcer store of value
are mathematically opposed.[fn::None of which makes the BUCK Bitcoin's enemy.  Quite the reverse:
hold your Bitcoin, draw BUCK credit against it to pay your bills, and keep accumulating.  The BUCK
is the Bitcoin maximalist's best friend -- just not a basket ingredient.]

* The discipline: only civilization's basics
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: civilizational-basics
:END:

So the rule is strict.  *The BuckBasket admits only low-level, civilizationally basic commodities*
-- energy, labour, structural materials, staple foods: the raw inputs every society needs and
physics forces back toward their costs.  It excludes anything whose value rests on scarcity by
design, a network effect or speculation.  Not because those things are bad, but because they are
the wrong ingredients for this recipe.

The reward for the discipline is the BUCK's whole reason for being: value that holds across
generations.  A unit of money your grandchildren will recognise has to be tied to something with the
weight of the physical economy, not to the fashion of a decade or the supply schedule of a
protocol.  The basket gives up the spectacular upside of speculative assets for the one thing a
monetary base must have and a speculative asset can never offer: the near certainty of coming back.

* Conclusion: an anchor of stone, kept honest by the crowd
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: anchor-of-stone
:END:

The BuckBasket is a heavy, open, liquid pantry of civilization's basics.  The whole world's
trading keeps it priced, it corrects its own prices before strangers can profit from them, it
leans against whatever has run too far, and it pays its depositors what reversion to the mean has
always paid the patient.

It gives the BUCK the heavy anchor a generational currency needs, and savers, businesses and
insurers a place for idle value to earn rather than decay.  It needs no manager, no forecast and no
promise -- only a published mechanism, the appetite of strangers for a bargain, and the fact that
the crust of the Earth and the laws of thermodynamics will not, short of catastrophe, let the basics
run away.

It cannot manage your portfolio, and was never meant to.  It does something rarer: it stands still
under the weight of the physical economy, and lets you lean on it.
