#+description: Commodities trail the money supply with measurable lags, so a basket's weights take long, slow excursions and return.  A rebalancer that reacts to instantaneous deviation fights every excursion and bleeds; one that waits for the excursion to level off commits its flow at maximum mispricing.  This article derives that policy from measured M2 lags, proves it in simulation, recasts it in differential mode -- pairwise log ratios across a seven-scale filter bank, the polyphase machine's trick of separating common from differential -- and encodes it as a Solidity state machine whose work is amortized across whoever shows up, so more granular triggering means less gas per agent.

#+BEGIN_ABSTRACT
The BuckBasket (see /The BuckBasket/) stays roughly balanced at no cost.
Redemptions draw from the overweight pools.  Deposits and treasury sweeps
fill the underweight ones.  The world's arbitrageurs do the rest.

What this leaves unused is /timing/.  Basket constituents do not move at
random.  They follow the M2 money supply with lags we can measure, and each
commodity has its own lag.  A constituent's share of basket value therefore
makes long, slow excursions away from its target weight, and comes home when
the slower constituents catch up.

This article does four things.

First, it measures those lags and builds a rebalancing policy from them.
Scale each constituent's effort by two quantities: how far it sits from its
target weight, and how hard its delayed moving average turns back toward
that target.  Wait while the excursion still grows.  Trade when it levels
off.

Second, it tests the policy in simulation.  The gated policies collect
75-90% of the continuous-rebalancing premium, at a quarter to a half of the
turnover, with far better per-trade timing.

Third, it changes coordinates.  One window on one share-vs-target deviation
collects one timescale only, and it carries the numeraire's noise.
/Differential mode/ removes both faults: pairwise log ratios, which cancel
the common mode exactly, across a filter bank of seven scales.  It collects
more than continuous rebalancing does, and it is the engine we deploy.

Fourth, and most important, it encodes the policy as an EVM state machine
that /amortizes its own maintenance/.  Every deposit, redemption or keeper
trigger does a small, bounded part of the work.  A closed-form catch-up
makes a poke after a quiet week cost the same as a poke after a busy hour.
The machine then advises the basket through two O(1) reads: where a deposit
should go, and which pool best satisfies a redemption.
#+END_ABSTRACT

* The premise: money moves first, commodities follow

New money does not reach all prices at once.  This is the Cantillon effect.
Monetary assets reprice in weeks, industrial commodities in months, and wages
and food over a year or more.

For the BUCK basket we can measure it.  Correlate each constituent's
year-over-year growth against M2SL, over a post-1995 window, with lags swept
from 0 to 36 months (see =alberta_buck/sim/quotes/fetch_feedstock.py= and
=rebalance_policy.py --fit-lags=):

| constituent | peak lag (months) | peak correlation | tier             |
|-------------+-------------------+------------------+------------------|
| cbBTC       | 0                 | 0.08 (weak)      | monetary (fast)  |
| PAXG        | censored @36      | 0.10 (weak)      | monetary (prior) |
| CNST        | 9-10              | 0.48-0.52        | industrial (mid) |
| NRGC        | 13                | 0.33-0.39        | industrial (mid) |
| LABR        | 15-16             | 0.32-0.38        | sticky (slow)    |
| FOOD        | 23                | 0.21-0.26        | sticky (slow)    |

The order matches the intuition: monetary assets first, then energy and
construction, then labour and food.  Gold fits poorly, because it trades on
real rates and on crisis as much as on M2, so it gets a fast "monetary asset"
prior instead of a fitted lag.

The consequence for the basket is this.  When M2 surges, the fast
constituents overshoot their target share and stay overweight until the slow
ones catch up.  The lag spread sets how long that takes: roughly a year.

These excursions are the basket's harvest.  A rebalancer that reacts to the
instantaneous deviation starts to sell the riser immediately, and keeps
selling all the way up.  It pays fees and bleeds against the trend.  A
rebalancer that waits for the excursion to level off makes fewer and larger
trades, at the maximum mispricing.

* The policy: quench while diverging, pour on the turn

Per constituent \(i\), at a fixed sampling cadence (one epoch ~ one day):

\begin{align*}
  \delta_i &= w_i / w^*_i - 1
    && \text{-- raw share deviation, right now};\\
  m_i &= \mathrm{EMA}_{X_i}(\delta_i)
    && \text{-- the delayed view (\(X_i\)-day window)};\\
  v_i &= \Delta m_i
    && \text{-- its velocity: receding / level / gaining};\\
  \dot{d}_i &= \mathrm{EMA}_{7\mathrm{d}}(\Delta\delta_i)
    && \text{-- the observed closure rate}.
\end{align*}

Each rule below was a /measured failure/ in the Python model before it was a
rule (=alberta_buck/sim/rebalance_policy.py=, =make nix-sim-policy=):

1. *The raw deviation gives direction and size.  The moving average gives
   timing only.*  A lagging average can still read "overweight" after the raw
   share has already crossed back through its target.  It must not trade
   then.  Require ~sign(m) == sign(delta)~.
2. *The velocity of the moving average classifies the regime.*  Still
   receding, so the deviation grows: do nothing, because the move is not
   finished.  Level, so it turns: start, but slowly.  Gaining, so the gap
   closes: complete the rebalancing.
3. *Size by rate-matching.*  Trade at \(\rho\) times the closure rate that
   the 1-week EMA observes: the rate at which we would lose half the distance
   to balance.  A prior of "half the gap in one window" floors it, and that
   prior applies alone while the regime is level:
   \[ \mathrm{effort}_i \;=\; \min\bigl(\rho\,\hat{c}_i\,w^*_i,\ \mathrm{cap}\bigr),
      \qquad
      \hat{c}_i \;=\; \max\Bigl(-\mathrm{sign}(\delta_i)\,\dot{d}_i,\
                                \tfrac{\ln 2}{2X_i}\,\lvert\delta_i\rvert\Bigr). \]
   This calibrates itself: there is no hand-set gain.  It also limits itself,
   because our own flow feeds the rate we observe, so a larger \(\rho\)
   saturates instead of overshooting.  Effort peaks just after the turn, when
   closure accelerates and the deviation is still near its maximum, then
   falls away as the gap closes.
4. *A leash bounds the wait.*  A secular trend never levels off (BTC,
   2020-25), so without a bound the deviation would run away.  Past
   |delta| > 30% the policy trades at the cap whatever the gate says, and
   releases at 25%.  The gate times the trades /inside/ the leash.  The
   leash enforces the mandate.
5. *Proceeds recycle, but never wash.*  Sell-side flow returns to the
   underweight pools through =sweepTreasury= / =investFromBucks=.  It must
   never return to a constituent the policy sold in the same round: a
   stale-signal sell against a raw-underweight rebuy only donates the AMM
   fee.

* Differential mode: the basket as a polyphase machine

The policy above has two weaknesses.  Both come from the /coordinate system/,
not from the gating.

First, *one window collects one timescale.*  Windows fitted to the M2 lag
spread catch the year-long excursions, and concede every mid-size swing to
whoever rebalances continuously.

Second, *share of basket value is not a clean observable.*  We measure a
constituent's share from pool BUCK reserves, so it carries the numeraire's
own noise.  When BUCK moves against the dollar, every share moves, and the
signal cannot tell that from a commodity that has really become rich.

The reconception (2026-07-11) is to change coordinates.  In a three-phase
machine you do not reason about the three line voltages.  You split them into
a *common mode*, the zero sequence, which carries no torque, and the
*differential modes*, which carry all of it.  The basket has the same
structure:

#+begin_quote
The profit lives in the differentials between commodity legs.  The common
mode -- BUCK's own valuation against the numeraire -- is the K-controller's
job, and it *cancels exactly* in cross-commodity log price ratios.
#+end_quote

So the =pairs= engine works pairwise, and in logarithms.  For legs \(i,j\)
the imbalance is
\[ d_{ij} \;=\; \ln\frac{1+\delta_i}{1+\delta_j}, \]
This is numeraire-free by construction: any factor common to both legs
subtracts out.  A Uniswap /tick is already a log price/, so the engine reads
ticks straight from =slot0=, and never needs =ln()= on-chain.

** A filter bank instead of a window

Each leg carries \(K=7\) EMAs of its normalized tick, on geometrically spaced
windows: 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160 and 320 days.  The EMA is linear, so /every
pair's moving average, velocity and curvature at every scale is the
difference of two legs' ladders/.  The full \(N(N-1)/2\) differential graph
therefore falls out of \(O(NK)\) state, with no pairwise storage at all.

The difference of two adjacent EMAs is a band-pass filter, so the ladder is
an octave filter bank.  The short windows see mid-size swings.  The long ones
see the M2 excursions.  The same gating logic applies at each scale, and each
scale decides on its own.

** The quorum turn detector

Each scale casts one vote per pair.  A scale votes only when both
single-scale rules hold at that scale: its moving-average gap agrees in sign
with the raw pairwise imbalance, /and/ its curvature points back toward
equilibrium.  The pair opens when the votes reach a quorum, 4 of 7 by
default.  No scale can fire alone, which is what makes a fast window safe to
include.

Effort is \(\kappa\,\lvert d_{ij}\rvert\,(\text{votes}/K)\), capped, under a
pairwise leash with hysteresis at 30% and 25%.  Trades are *matched pairs*:
sell the rich leg, buy the poor leg, equal value.  They are therefore
self-financing, they leave no cash residue, and they are wash-proof by
construction, because the engine cannot sell a constituent and rebuy it in
the same round.  The single-scale policy needed an explicit no-wash rule to
prevent that.  A progressive warmup lets the short windows vote from about
day 20, so the engine is not silent while a 116-day window fills.

** What it measures

Synthetic 20-year paths, 5 seeds, quorum 4, premium vs hold in bp/yr:

| cost/leg | prop | band | factor | vrate | pairs |
|----------+------+------+--------+-------+-------|
| 30bp     | +430 | +417 | +393   | +340  | *+496* |
| 100bp    | +365 | +308 | +357   | +313  | *+422* |
| 250bp    | +228 |  +89 | *+283* | +271  | +262  |

=pairs= beats even /prop's gross harvest/, for two reasons: multi-scale
coverage recovers the mid-size swings, and pair-matched execution collects
more per unit of imbalance.  It dominates every policy through roughly
200bp/leg of cost.  Only at extreme costs does =factor= retake the lead.

The quorum trades premium against cost: q3 gives the most premium (+508, at
1.39 turnover), q5 the least turnover (0.65, at +460), and *q4 is the knee*.

The vote criterion is the other knob, and it decides the engine's character
(quorum 4):

| vote   | criterion                                  | synth premium | turnover | capture90 | hist CAGR |
|--------+--------------------------------------------+---------------+----------+-----------+-----------|
| =curv= | divergence /decelerating/ -- early, at the top | +496       |     0.96 | +201 bp   |     17.9% |
| =vel=  | gap /already closing/ -- the confirmed turn   | +421         |     0.60 | +270 bp   |     *18.4%* |

=curv= buys about 75bp/yr more gross harvest, for 60% more turnover and
worse trend bleed.  The two cross at roughly 235bp/leg of cost.  =vel= trades
later and less, with better per-trade capture and trend robustness equal to
prop's.  =vel= is *the deployment default*, and it is the criterion
=PairsRebalanceDirector.sol= implements.

** Which engine, and when

The whole study comes down to a regime split:

- *=pairs= is the reverting-regime pick* -- the regime the BuckBasket is
  built for, and the one the physics argument in /The BuckBasket/ says is the
  default behaviour of the physical economy.
- *=factor= remains the trend-regime pick* -- best historical CAGR (19.3%)
  through the 2020-25 replay, where BTC ran x9 and reversion never arrived.

Between the two engines that /exist on-chain/ the choice is not close.
=pairs= beats =vrate= on the premium and on the risk measure.  =vrate=
concentrates its trades where the observed closure is fastest.  In a trend
that means selling into sharp V-shaped pullbacks which then resume, and its
historical capture90 is the worst of any policy studied: -676bp, against
+270bp for =pairs= / =vel=.  In the reverting regime the same concentration
is exactly right, at +514bp, the best of any policy.  That is what makes
=vrate= the wrong default: of all the policies, its result depends most on
the regime being the one you hoped for.

* Encoding it on-chain: work that amortizes across whoever shows up

The policy needs a clock, because moving averages advance with time.  The EVM
has no scheduler, and nobody pays to run one.  The established answer is the
pattern behind Compound's =accrueInterest=, MakerDAO's per-collateral =drip=
and Uniswap's oracle observations:

#+begin_quote
*Lazy time-indexed accrual.*  State is advanced on first touch, as a
closed-form function of elapsed time -- never per block.
#+end_quote

Three refinements make it fit a /multi-pool signal aggregator/:

** Closed-form EMA catch-up

Under sample-and-hold (the deviation observed now stands in for the quiet
gap), \(n\) missed epochs of EMA recursion collapse exactly, with
\(q = 1-\beta\) (and \(q_7\) the one-week EMA's decay):

\begin{align*}
  m' &= \delta + (m - \delta)\,q^n
    && \text{-- the average};\\
  v' &= q^n\,v \;-\; (1-q)^2\,n\,q^{\,n-1}\,(m - \delta)
    && \text{-- its velocity};\\
  \dot{d}' &= q_7^{\,n-1}\bigl((\delta - \delta_{\mathrm{prev}})(1-q_7)
              + q_7\,\dot{d}\bigr)
    && \text{-- the closure rate};
\end{align*}

Binary exponentiation computes each in \(O(\log n)\).  One poke after a quiet
month costs the same as one poke after a busy hour, /and it lands the signals
where n diligent pokes would have left them/.  This is not approximately
true.  It is an invariant that the test suite fuzzes over 256 random
gap-and-imbalance combinations, and enforces to fixed-point rounding.

The closed form for the velocity matters.  A naive "average dm over the gap"
update classifies the regime differently from the diligent recursion.  That
was a real bug, and the regime test matrix caught it.

** The round-robin work wheel

Take N constituents, a persistent cursor and =poke(maxWork)=.  Each call
advances at most =maxWork= stale constituents, starting from the cursor.
Every basket activation -- a deposit, a redeem, a treasury sweep or a bare
keeper trigger -- carries a small budget of 1 to 2.  The measured gas, on
6-token mocks with via-IR and solc 0.8.28:

| operation                            | gas     |
|--------------------------------------+---------|
| poke one constituent, 1-epoch gap    | ~36,500 |
| poke one constituent, 50-epoch gap   | ~19,900 |
| poke when everything is fresh        | ~3,100  |

Catch-up is /flat in the size of the gap/.  The 50-epoch poke is cheaper than
the 1-epoch poke only because its storage is already warm.  The fresh-epoch
guard is almost free.  So *the more often the machine is triggered, the less
work each agent does*.  If nobody arrives for a week, whoever arrives next
pays one closed-form update, not a week of them.  Work is conserved.
Granularity trades per-call gas against signal staleness, and never against
total cost.

** Cached observations with running sums

A constituent's deviation is its share of the total, so it needs
/everyone's/ pool values.  Reading \(N\) pools per poke would defeat the
sharding.  So each poke reads only /its own/ pool: one =balanceOf= for the
BUCK reserve \(bv_i\) (a full-range position has pool value \(2\,bv_i\)), and
one =slot0= for the spot, which gives the price-scaled target share
\(s_i = a_i\,P_{0,i}^2/\mathrm{spot}_i\).  That is the same
fixed-quantity-index target the pro-rata redemption uses.  The poke then
folds its reading into cached running sums, by difference:

\[ B \mathrel{{+}{=}} bv_i^{\mathrm{new}} - bv_i^{\mathrm{cached}}, \qquad
   S \mathrel{{+}{=}} s_i^{\mathrm{new}} - s_i^{\mathrm{cached}}, \qquad
   \delta_i = \frac{bv_i / B}{\,s_i / S\,} - 1 . \]

The sums are slightly stale, and a moving-average policy is insensitive to
that by construction.  Decision data aggregates the same way.  Each poke
stores its constituent's signed advisory effort, so the two decision reads
are simple scans over N cached int32s: =depositHint()= returns the strongest
buy-side effort, and =redeemHint()= the strongest sell-side.

** The trust model: hints, not authority

The director holds no funds and executes nothing.  Its outputs are
/advisory/.  On every trade it performs, the basket's venue re-verifies
everything material: the spot-versus-TWAP manipulation guard, the liquidity
bounds and the conversion-loss budget.

A hint that is stale, wrong or even adversarial can therefore only re-order
flows -- send a deposit to the second-best pool, say.  It cannot break
solvency, and it cannot extract value beyond what the venue's own guards
already bound.  That is why the signal state is safe to maintain lazily,
through unauthenticated public pokes.

* The contracts: two engines on a shared chassis

=RebalanceDirectorBase.sol= is the policy-agnostic chassis -- epoch clock,
=poke(maxWork)= work wheel, metadata sync, cached observations with running
sums, binary exponentiation, and the =depositHint= / =redeemHint= /
=effortOf= surface -- and two signal engines plug into it behind the common
=IRebalanceDirector= advisory interface:

- =BasketRebalanceDirector.sol= (*vrate*) -- the single-window
  share-vs-target policy derived above.
- =PairsRebalanceDirector.sol= (*pairs*) -- differential mode: per-leg
  7-window tick-EMA ladders, pair quorum votes on the confirmed turn
  (=vel=), arithmetic weight-ratio differential, pairwise leash bitmask,
  and =bestPair()= naming the top matched trade for keepers.  Pair efforts
  are folded into per-leg /net/ efforts at poke time, so the chassis's
  advisory reads stay O(N) cached -- fully API-compatible with =vrate=.

*=pairs= at quorum 4 is what deploys*, and what the rebalancing simulation
runs.  The rest of this section describes =vrate=, because its state layout
shows the shared machinery more simply.

=src/basket/BasketRebalanceDirector.sol= (~2.5 slots of hot state per
constituent, packed):

#+begin_src solidity
struct Signal {
    int128  m;         // EMA_X of deviation (1e18)
    int128  vEma;      // EMA_X of per-epoch d(m)
    int128  ddotEma;   // 1-week EMA of per-epoch d(delta)
    int128  prevDelta;
    uint128 bv;        // cached pool BUCK reserve
    uint128 s;         // cached price-scaled target share
    int32   effortBp;  // signed advisory effort (bp of NAV per epoch)
    uint32  lastEpoch;
    uint32  firstEpoch;
    bool    leashed;
}
#+end_src

The API surface:

- =syncConstituents()= -- permissionless; pulls constituent metadata from the
  basket's public getters (re-callable: =addBasketToken= renormalizes).
- =poke(maxWork)= / =pokeAll()= -- the work wheel.  Skips fresh constituents
  for ~2 warm SLOADs; advances stale ones with the closed forms above and the
  vrate effort rule (regime gate, rate-matched size, leash, warmup).
- =depositHint()= / =redeemHint()= / =effortOf(i)= / =effortsAll()= -- the
  O(1)/O(N)-cached advisory reads.  A =type(uint256).max= hint means "no
  signal; use the default most-underweight routing".
- =setParams(...)= -- governance: epoch length, window, \rho, deadband,
  regime band, leash bounds, per-epoch cap.

How =BuckBasketProRata= consumes it (the integration increments, in order of
invasiveness):

1. *Increment 0 -- flow routing, no new trades.*  =_depositBuck= and
   =sweepTreasury= pass =director.depositHint()= to =investFromBucks= as the
   =poolHint=, and fall back to most-underweight on the sentinel.  The
   single-token redeem path reads =redeemHint()=.  Each of these activations
   also calls =director.poke(1)=, the bounded slice that keeps the signals
   alive.  The basket's ordinary deposit, redeem and sweep traffic then
   performs well-timed rebalancing /for free/.
2. *Increment 1 -- keeper rebalance steps.*  A permissionless pair,
   =rebalanceSource(i)= (withdraw + convert from the strongest sell-side
   pool, bounded by =effortBp=) and =rebalanceSink()= (invest the pending
   BUCK into the deposit hint), each one small transaction -- the
   =sweepTreasury= pattern, directed by the signals.
3. *Weighted draws.*  =_allocateSellHigh= can tilt its per-pool draw weights
   by =effortsAll()= rather than spot overweight alone.

* Verification

Three layers, all runnable from the Makefile:

(The model figures above regenerate via =make nix-sim-policy= then
=make nix-sim-plot-article=.)

- *Unit and fuzz tests (Forge)* -- =make nix-test-director=.  Ten tests
  cover six things.  The amortization invariant, as diligent-versus-lazy
  twins, plus a 256-run fuzz over random gaps and imbalances.  The regime
  transitions on a scripted excursion: receding waits, level starts, gaining
  saturates, and sign-agreement blocks the wrong-way trade from a stale
  moving average.  Leash engagement and its hysteresis.  Both hints.  Wheel
  budgets and cursor rotation.  And the gas figures quoted above.
- *Parallel regime matrix* -- =make nix-test-director-regimes=.  The same
  suite re-run across a window x rho grid (defaults: {6, 8, 16} epochs x
  {1.5, 3, 6}), one forge process per regime, in parallel.  This matrix is
  what exposed the velocity catch-up approximation: windows of 6 classified
  a post-gap regime differently between the lazy and diligent paths until
  the closed form replaced the average-velocity shortcut.
- *End-to-end on Anvil* -- =make nix-sim-director=.  This runs the
  rebalancing scenario, about 400 agents: arbs, LPs, direct-mint depositors
  and a whale.  A =DirectorKeeperAgent= pokes the deployed director with a
  2-constituent budget each tick, and executes its advice as 2-hop Universal
  Router swaps, sell-hint to BUCK to buy-hint, sized by the advised bp of
  basket NAV.  The output vector records =directorPokes= and
  =directorTrades= per day, beside the existing basket telemetry.

* Variants worth investigating

Nothing in this section is measured.  These are the directions the machine's
own structure suggests, each with the literature that would supply the
theory, ordered by expected return on effort.  Two analogies organize them,
and both do real work rather than decoration.

** Taking the machine analogy literally

The three-phase intuition that produced differential mode has more in it.  A
polyphase drive does not stop at "separate the common mode".  It applies two
specific transforms, and each has an exact basket counterpart:

- The *Clarke transform* (abc to alpha-beta-zero) splits a three-phase
  vector into a zero sequence, which carries no torque, and two /orthogonal/
  differential components.  The basket's log-weight vector splits the same
  way: one common mode, BUCK against the numeraire, which is the
  K-controller's business, and \(N-1\) differential modes.
- The *Park transform* (alpha-beta to d-q) then rotates into a frame that
  turns with the field.  Quantities that were sinusoidal become constant, and
  a plain PI controller is then enough.  Field-oriented control uses this to
  split the current into a torque-producing part and a flux-producing part,
  and regulates each separately.

The basket's d-q split is the useful part.  One part of an imbalance is
/harvestable/, because it reverts: that is torque.  The other part is
/secular/, a trend that must be tolerated and bounded: that is flux.  The
leash is a crude form of this distinction today, applied after the fact and
with a hand-set bound.

*Slip* is the concept the engine already rediscovered.  Induction torque is
proportional to slip, the /relative/ rate between field and rotor, and it
falls to zero at zero slip however fast both are turning.  That is the
rate-matching rule exactly: the harvest scales with the rate of relative
movement, not with absolute price motion.  The machine analogy therefore
confirms the policy's central choice after the fact.

** Taking the frequency analogy literally

The EMA ladder is already a decomposition in time: geometrically spaced
windows, with the difference of two adjacent EMAs acting as a band-pass
filter.  That is an octave filter bank, which is a wavelet transform under
another name.  The cross-sectional axis is /not/ decomposed.  The full object
is two-dimensional, *mode x timescale*, and the engine does one axis properly
and the other by brute force.

*** Orthogonal modes instead of pairs

The sharpest criticism of the =pairs= engine is that its basis is redundant.
Six legs carry *five* independent differential modes, but the engine tracks
*fifteen* pairs.  The quorum therefore counts overlapping evidence as if it
were independent, and the effort sizing counts the same excursion two or
three times.

The fix is the Clarke transform, generalized: diagonalize the covariance of
the leg log-returns, and work in the eigenbasis.  PC1 is the common mode, and
here it is /discarded/, because it is the controller's job.  PC2 to PCN are
orthogonal, so each can be sized on its own without double-counting, and a
quorum over modes is a quorum over genuinely independent evidence.  This is
the standard construction in statistical arbitrage (Avellaneda and Lee,
/Statistical Arbitrage in the US Equities Market/, 2010: PCA eigenportfolios
with mean-reverting residuals).

The on-chain cost is an \(N \times N\) fixed-point rotation of the cached
ladders.  At \(N=6\) that is small, and the eigenvectors are governance
parameters, refitted rarely rather than per poke.  The cointegration
literature (Johansen) gives a more principled version again: for constituents
bound to each other by physics, the object to trade is the /stationary/
linear combination, not the raw pairwise imbalance.

*** Trade to the boundary, not to the target

Under proportional transaction costs, the optimal policy is provably *not*
to trade toward the target.  It is a *no-trade region*, and one trades only
far enough to return to its boundary (Davis and Norman 1990; Shreve and Soner
1994).  The current engine sizes effort toward proportion, and relies on the
cap and the deadband to stop it overshooting.  Make the deadband a genuine
no-trade wedge, and size effort to reach the near edge instead of the centre.
This is the cheapest change on this list, and the one with the firmest theory
behind it.

*** Bands fitted to the reversion, not set by hand

The deadband is a flat 150bp, and the leash a flat 30%.  For a
mean-reverting spread, the optimal entry and exit thresholds are known in
closed form, as a function of the reversion rate, the volatility and the cost
(Bertram 2010; Leung and Li on optimal Ornstein-Uhlenbeck trading).  They
scale like \(\sigma/\sqrt{2\theta}\).  The M2-lag study already fits a
timescale per constituent, so fitting \((\theta,\sigma)\) per /pair/ or per
/mode/ is the same kind of exercise.  It would replace two hand-set constants
with measured ones.

*** Weights are bounded, so bound them properly

"Strict upper and lower bounds" has an exact stochastic counterpart.  Weights
live on a simplex, and the natural diffusion there is the *Jacobi
(Wright-Fisher)* process: its stationary distribution is Beta, and its
first-passage behaviour is known.  Fit it, and the leash becomes a
/quantile/ instead of a guess.  "This excursion is at the 99th percentile of
what this constituent's share does" is a defensible trigger.  "30%" is not.

*** A target-share family with a theorem attached

Constant-mix is one point in a family.  Fernholz's stochastic portfolio
theory shows two things.  A portfolio generated by a concave function of the
market weights decomposes into a generating term plus a drift term, and that
drift term is /non-negative/.  And *diversity-weighted* portfolios
(\(w_i \propto \mu_i^{\,p}\), \(0<p<1\)) beat the market portfolio under a
diversity condition -- which is exactly the bounded-weights premise the
BuckBasket rests on.

The rebalancing premium this article measures /is/ Fernholz's excess growth
rate.  Expose \(p\) as a governance parameter and you get a continuous knob,
from equal-weight (maximum premium, maximum turnover) to cap-weight (neither),
with a proof behind the interpolation instead of a simulation.

*** Phase, not just velocity

"Wait for the turn" is a statement about /phase/: trade when an excursion
nears the end of its cycle.  The engine infers this from the sign of a
velocity or a curvature, which is a one-bit reading of a continuous quantity.
The analytic-signal construction -- a Hilbert transform, approximated cheaply
by an EMA quadrature pair -- gives instantaneous amplitude and phase
directly.  Effort could then be a smooth function of how far through its
excursion a pair actually is.

Energy and food have real seasonality, so genuine periodicity is worth
detecting.  The *Goertzel algorithm* computes one DFT bin from two state
variables, at O(1) work per sample.  It is the one FFT-family primitive cheap
enough to run on-chain unmodified.  It pays only if a fitted seasonal
component turns out to be material, which is a reason to /test/ for one.

** What not to do

Two directions sound attractive and are traps.  The article's own data says
so.

*Do not chase gross premium.*  =curv= and quorum 3 each buy more harvest with
more turnover, and the result of this whole study is that timing beats
volume.

*Do not add a non-reverting constituent to sharpen the signal.*  Every gear
in this machine assumes reversion, so feeding it something built not to
revert cannot improve it.  /The BuckBasket/ makes the argument in full, and
the 2020-25 replay is the measurement.

* Limitations and next increments

- The lazy catch-up is exact under sample-and-hold, but a real gap has a
  price path.  More pokes give more fidelity at the same amortized price, and
  the basket's own activity supplies them exactly when flows are high, which
  is when fidelity matters most.
- The director reads whole-pool BUCK reserves, while the venue nets out
  treasury liquidity.  The bias is small, and it does not affect the /order/
  of the hints.
- Windows are governance parameters, seeded from the M2-lag analysis.  The
  model says the premium is flat from 30 to 180 days, so mis-tuning them
  costs little.
- The basket-side consumption is the next contract pass: the increment-0
  wiring in =BuckBasketProRata=, and the =rebalanceSource/Sink= pair.  The
  director itself, its tests and the sim keeper are complete.
- Keeper bounty economics are not modelled yet.  A public network needs them:
  a few bp of each productive step, paid from the treasury.
- *The trend-regime pick has no on-chain engine.*  =factor= wins the
  historical replay, but it exists only as a Python policy: the two Solidity
  engines are =vrate= and =pairs=.  So either we accept the regime split as a
  deliberate bet on reversion -- which the basket's whole thesis already is --
  or =factor= needs an engine, so that governance can switch.
- The =pairs= basis is redundant (fifteen pairs over five independent modes
  at \(N=6\)); see /Variants worth investigating/.
