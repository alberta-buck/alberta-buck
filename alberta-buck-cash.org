#+BEGIN_ABSTRACT
For forty years cryptographers have tried to digitize physical cash and failed.  Chaum's
DigiCash (1990s) had the mathematics but went bankrupt for want of merchant adoption.
Bitcoin gave us digital scarcity but has no native bearer-paper form -- Casascius coins
and BIP-38 paper wallets are aftermarket wrappers, not first-class instruments.  Central
bank digital currencies threaten the opposite: digital surveillance with no bearer privacy
at all.

The Alberta Buck identity, wormhole, and reserve machinery -- combined with one new primitive, a
global commitment pool ([[https://perry.kundert.ca/range/finance/alberta-buck-wormhole/#global-committment-pool][Wormhole "Global Commitment Pool"]]) -- finally permit a /native/
digital-physical bridge.  A *BUCK Note* is a slip of paper carrying a QR-encoded opening of an
on-chain commitment.  The same cryptographic primitive instantiates three radically different
financial instruments depending on the spend predicate baked into the commitment:

- *Bearer notes* (Mode A): anyone who scans the QR can deposit.  Walking-around money.
- *Targeted notes* (Mode B): only a specific identity can deposit.  Private gifts;
  cold-storage.
- *Programmable notes* (Mode C): time-locked, oracle-conditioned, multi-signature.
  Escrow, traveler's cheques, conditional payouts.

Demurrage -- the same mechanism that drives BUCK circulation on chain -- solves
physical cash's oldest problem: lost notes.  A BUCK Note ages in your pocket at the
same rate a wallet balance does.  After 50 years, a forgotten note's full value has
returned to the Jubilee account and been redistributed to issuers.  The 5-10% of
physical currency that humanity loses every century is no longer a permanent monetary
extraction -- it is a temporary loan to the holder, repaid by demurrage.

Issuance is on chain and identity-bound; circulation is private and offline; deposit
finality is cryptographic.  Regulators retain selective-disclosure access to the
issuance trail without observing day-to-day circulation.  This is stronger compliance
than physical cash provides today, and stronger privacy than any CBDC has ever
proposed. ([[https://perry.kundert.ca/images/alberta-buck-cash.pdf][PDF]],
[[https://perry.kundert.ca/images/alberta-buck-cash.txt][Text]])

* The Cash Paradox

Physical cash is simultaneously irreplaceable and impossible to digitize.

It is irreplaceable because it does things no digital instrument has ever matched.
A $20 bill works without a network, a battery, a bank, an ID check, or a counterparty
to your transaction.  It clears instantly, finally, and without fees.  It is the
single most successful monetary instrument in human history -- so successful that
roughly half the value of US currency in circulation is held overseas as a global
store of value, in defiance of every theoretical reason it shouldn't be.[fn:cashshare]

It is impossible to digitize because every attempt has reproduced the surveillance
properties of bank money instead of the privacy properties of cash.  Modern card
networks log every transaction.  Bank wire transfers require both parties to be
identified.  Stablecoins record every transfer publicly on chain.  Central bank
digital currencies, where they exist or are being designed (China's e-CNY,
Europe's digital euro proposals), are explicitly programmable surveillance tools --
the central bank can see, restrict, expire, or geofence any holder's balance at
will.[fn:cbdc]

The closest existing analogues to digital cash are aftermarket bearer wrappers
around cryptocurrencies: Casascius physical Bitcoin coins (2011-2013), OpenDime
USB sticks, BIP-38 paper wallets, and the various scratch-card "crypto gift cards"
sold at convenience stores.  These work, technically -- a Casascius coin is a
genuine bearer instrument -- but they are workarounds, not designs.  The underlying
token (Bitcoin) does not natively understand bearer-paper form.  Loss of the
physical coin is permanent loss of the value.  Counterfeiting requires faith in
the manufacturer's hologram, not in the cryptography.[fn:casascius]

What if the bearer-paper form were a /first-class/ instrument of the system?
Designed in from the start, with the same cryptographic and economic properties
as on-chain balances, integrated with the identity and reserve mechanics that
already exist?

That is BUCK Cash.

** Why Forty Years of Trying

David Chaum invented digital cash in 1982 with the blind-signature e-cash
scheme.[fn:chaum82]  By 1989 he had founded DigiCash to commercialize it.  By 1998
DigiCash was bankrupt -- not because the cryptography failed (it worked), but because
no merchant wanted to be the first to accept it and no consumer wanted to be the
first to hold it.  The two-sided market never bootstrapped.

Stefan Brands extended Chaum's work in 1993 with offline divisible coins that revealed
the spender's identity if (and only if) they double-spent.[fn:brands93]  Camenisch,
Hohenberger, and Lysyanskaya improved on this in 2005 with compact divisible
e-cash.[fn:chl05]  None of these schemes ever shipped in production at scale.

Bitcoin, when it arrived in 2009, was deliberately /not/ digital cash in the Chaumian
sense.  It was digital scarcity with public ledger transparency -- closer to digital
gold than digital cash.  The bearer-paper market that grew up around it (Casascius,
OpenDime, paper wallets) was an afterthought, not a design goal.

Zerocash (2014)[fn:zerocash] and its production deployment as Zcash gave us the
first scalable on-chain commitment-and-nullifier UTXO model -- the cryptographic
primitive that BUCK Notes use.  Zcash showed that you can have private on-chain
balances; it did not extend that to printable physical bearer instruments.  Aztec
extended the model with predicate-encrypted notes for private smart contract calls,
but again, no paper.

The reason all of this never produced "BUCK Cash" -- a printable, demurrage-aware,
identity-aware bearer instrument -- is that it required four pieces to come together
simultaneously, and they did not until now:

1. A *commitment-and-nullifier UTXO model* on a smart contract platform
   (Zerocash gave us this in 2014; Aztec deployed it on Ethereum in 2018-2020).

2. A *cryptographic identity layer* that lets some notes be targeted to a specific
   recipient and others be left bearer (the BUCK identity system, with PS signatures,
   ElGamal credentials, and Chaum-Pedersen re-encryption).

3. A *demurrage mechanism* that solves the lost-cash problem and gives bearer notes
   a finite lifespan (BUCK's demurrage and Jubilee are unique among monetary systems).

4. A *jurisdictional reserve* that backs the value, so the bearer note represents a
   real claim on real assets, not a speculative crypto-token whose price might collapse
   between print and deposit (Alberta Buck's commodity-basket-stabilized reserve and
   the BuckK PID controller).

Alberta Buck has all four.  No prior system had all four.  That is why BUCK Cash is
a /design/, not a workaround.

* The Primitive: A BUCK Note

A BUCK Note is, physically, a slip of paper -- or a card, or a steel plate, or a
QR sticker on a fridge magnet.  Cryptographically, it is a tuple:

#+begin_example
(secret s, value v, age_at_print, t_print, predicate P, merkle_path)
#+end_example

These six values are encoded as a QR code (or NFC payload, or printed digits, or all
three for redundancy).  The slip of paper is the bearer instrument.

On chain, the BUCK contract maintains a Merkle tree of *note commitments*.  Each
commitment is computed as:

\[
cm \;=\; H(s,\; v,\; age_{0},\; t_{print},\; P)
\]

where \( H \) is a SNARK-friendly hash (Poseidon or Pedersen, per the wormhole
construction).  When a holder wants to deposit a note, they:

1. Scan the QR code into their wallet app
2. The app constructs a SNARK proving:
   - Knowledge of \( (s, v, age_0, t_{print}, P) \) opening some commitment in the tree
   - That the predicate \( P \) evaluates to true under the spender's witness
   - That the destination mint amount equals \( v \) less accumulated demurrage
3. The app submits the SNARK + a fresh nullifier \( nf = \text{PRF}(s) \) to the chain
4. The contract verifies the proof, checks that \( nf \) is unspent, marks it spent,
   and mints to the spender's account

The contract sees only "valid SNARK + fresh nullifier + mint amount + recipient."  It
does not see which commitment was opened, who the original printer was, or what kind
of note this is.

The note is now permanently dead.  The paper is just a piece of paper.  Tearing it in
half is a *human* signal of finality, not a cryptographic one -- but it's a useful
ritual to prevent confused future holders from trying to redeposit a dead QR.

** Counterfeiting is Cryptographically Impossible

A "fake" BUCK Note is just a random QR code.  Its claimed commitment does not appear
in the on-chain tree.  The Merkle inclusion proof inside the SNARK fails.  No mint
occurs.

This is a stronger anti-counterfeit guarantee than the Bank of Canada provides for
physical $20 bills.  Polymer banknotes can be counterfeited (the techniques are
well-published; the question is only whether the counterfeit passes casual
inspection).  A BUCK Note cannot be counterfeited at all -- the commitment registry
is the authentic-bill list, and there is no inspection threshold to deceive.

Wallet apps that scan a BUCK Note display its status immediately:

#+LATEX: {\scriptsize
| Display             | Meaning                                                       |
|---------------------+---------------------------------------------------------------|
| =VALID: 1,000 BUCK= | Commitment in tree, nullifier unspent                         |
| =DEAD=              | Commitment in tree, nullifier already spent                   |
| =FAKE=              | Commitment not in tree                                        |
| =EXPIRED=           | Predicate includes an expiry that has passed                  |
| =NOT FOR YOU=       | Predicate is targeted (Mode B), and you are not the recipient |
#+LATEX: }

Casual users do not need to understand the underlying cryptography any more than
they need to understand the security features of a $20 bill.  They scan, see VALID,
and accept the note as payment.

* The Three Modes

The same primitive instantiates three radically different financial instruments
by varying the spend predicate \( P \).

** Mode A: Bearer Notes ("Cash")

The simplest predicate:

\[
P(\text{witness}) \;=\; \text{true if the witness reveals } s
\]

Anyone who knows \( s \) can spend the note.  This is exactly how a $20 bill works:
the note is bearer, possession is the entirety of ownership, no identity is checked,
finality is immediate.

The cryptographic risk that physical cash does not have: anyone who *photographs*
a bearer QR can spend it.  No physical possession is required.  A merchant's
security camera that captures the back of a note while you tap-to-pay is a credible
threat.  This is solvable, but requires that bearer notes be printed under a
*tamper-evident seal*:

- A peelable hologram covering the QR (the Casascius pattern -- the act of peeling
  is the act of taking ownership)
- A scratch-off panel covering a separate one-time code that must be combined with
  the QR to spend (lottery-card pattern; lets the note be displayed without being
  live)
- A laminated sleeve that visibly damages on opening (mechanical seal, no electronic
  components)

A bearer note that has not yet had its seal broken is "in print but not yet alive."
Once the seal is broken, the QR is exposed and the note becomes spendable by whoever
holds it.  This restores the physical-exclusivity property of paper cash.

The race condition that emerges if two people somehow both photograph an unsealed
bearer QR is *exactly* the race that occurs if two people both find the same $20
on the sidewalk: first to grab it (first to broadcast the spend transaction) owns it.
The chain enforces atomic finality.

#+begin_quote
*Use cases*: tipping; small purchases; emergency money for travel; "kid's allowance"
in physical form; merchant change-making; pocket-cash distributions in cash-poor
communities.
#+end_quote

** Mode B: Targeted Notes ("Personal Cheques")

A more interesting predicate:

\[
P(\text{witness}) \;=\; \text{true if the witness reveals } s \text{ AND proves knowledge of } sk \text{ for } pk^*
\]

where \( pk^* \) is a *stealth address* derived as

\[
pk^* \;=\; H(s) \cdot pk_{\text{recipient}}
\]

This binds the spendability to a specific identity without revealing whose identity it
is to anyone reading the QR.  Anyone can scan the note; only the recipient can prove
knowledge of \( sk \) for \( pk^* \) (because \( sk^* = H(s) \cdot sk_{\text{recipient}} \)
and only the recipient knows \( sk_{\text{recipient}} \)).

The stealth address pattern is borrowed from Monero[fn:cryptonote] and Zcash
shielded addresses.  It guarantees that the note's targeting is *not observable on
chain* until the recipient deposits.  An attacker examining the commitment tree
cannot tell which notes are bearer, which are targeted, or to whom the targeted
ones are addressed.

The cryptographic machinery that makes this work -- specifically, the Chaum-Pedersen
proof of equality of discrete logarithms -- is *exactly the same* machinery used in
the BUCK Identity protocol's =approve= flow ([[https://perry.kundert.ca/range/finance/alberta-buck-identity][Identity]]).
Mode B targeting is not a new cryptographic component; it is a re-application of an
existing one.

#+begin_quote
*Use cases*: gift cards; remittances to family abroad; private payments to specific
counterparties; cold-storage savings targeted to your own future identity; estate
planning (notes targeted to heirs, stored in a safe-deposit box); donations to
specific charities without observable on-chain transfers.
#+end_quote

*** Untraceable Targeted Transfer

The most novel use of Mode B: an untraceable transfer between two consenting
identities.

Alice wants to send 1,000 BUCK to Bob without producing any observable on-chain link
between them.  She prints a Mode B note targeted to Bob's identity, hands him the
paper (or sends him a photograph of the QR), and Bob deposits it.

The on-chain history shows:
- Alice's account decremented by 1,000 BUCK at the burn (issuance) step
- One commitment added to the tree
- Some time later, a SNARK consumes some commitment from the tree, mints to Bob's
  account

There is no link between Alice's burn and Bob's mint /except/ the temporal correlation,
which is dissolved by the size of the global anonymity set.  If a thousand other
people are also burning and minting around the same time, Alice's transfer is
indistinguishable from any of theirs.

This is *better than physical cash* in one respect: physical cash transfers are
traceable to the bank ATM where the cash was withdrawn, if the bank chooses to
correlate serial numbers.  BUCK Cash transfers are not traceable to the issuing
account by anyone, including the issuer themselves, after the issuance step
completes.

** Mode C: Programmable Notes ("Smart Cheques")

The fully general case: \( P \) is an arbitrary boolean predicate over public state
and private witness data.

\[
P(\text{witness, public state}) \;=\; \text{any SNARK-encodable boolean function}
\]

Examples worth singling out:

#+LATEX: {\scriptsize
| Predicate                                          | Instrument                                  |
|----------------------------------------------------+---------------------------------------------|
| =know(s) AND own(pk*)= AND =t > t_unlock=          | Time-locked targeted note (savings)         |
| =know(s) AND own(pk*)= AND =t < t_expiry=          | Expiring gift card                          |
| =know(s) AND multisig(pk_1, ..., pk_k of n)=       | Notarized escrow                            |
| =know(s) AND oracle(BTC_price > X)=                | Conditional payout / prediction voucher     |
| =know(s) OR (own(pk_issuer) AND t > t_recovery)=   | Recoverable bearer note (issuer can recall) |
| =know(s) AND (own(pk_a) OR own(pk_b))=             | Either-of-two recipients                    |
| =know(s) AND signature(pk_authority, transaction)= | Authority-cosigned transfer                 |
#+LATEX: }

The contract does not need to understand any of these.  It sees only a SNARK proving
"I opened a commitment in the tree and its predicate evaluates to true under my
witness."  The predicate logic is entirely inside the SNARK circuit, written by the
note's printer at issuance time.

This is the substrate for BUCK Cash to extend beyond simple notes into a full family
of programmable financial instruments, all sharing the same on-chain footprint and
the same privacy properties.

#+begin_quote
*Use cases*: traveler's cheques (recoverable by issuer if lost, claimable by holder
otherwise); inheritance instruments (claimable by heir after probate date); insurance
payouts (claimable on oracle-confirmed event); escrow for real-estate closings;
conditional bonuses (claimable if performance metric oracle-attested); payment
streams (claimable in increments over time); subscription pre-pays (claimable by
service provider in periodic increments).
#+end_quote

* Demurrage Solves the Lost-Cash Problem

This is the part of BUCK Cash that I think is genuinely novel, and I want to spend
some time on it because it has implications well beyond the BUCK system.

** The Hidden Inflation of Lost Cash

Every monetary system that uses physical bearer instruments suffers a constant slow
loss to *unrecoverable currency*.  Banknotes are lost in fires, dropped down storm
drains, forgotten in the pockets of jeans that get washed and shredded, buried in
landfills, sealed in walls, hidden by the elderly and forgotten by their heirs.

The Federal Reserve estimates that 5-10% of US paper currency is permanently
unrecoverable at any given moment.[fn:lostcash]  At a US M0 of approximately
$2.3 trillion, this represents *$115-230 billion in permanently extracted value* --
seigniorage to the Federal Reserve, since the lost notes never get redeemed but the
backing assets remain on the Fed's books.

This is not theft, but it is not neutral either.  Lost cash is a regressive transfer:
the people most likely to lose cash are the people who use cash most (the elderly,
the unbanked, low-income workers paid in cash).  The beneficiary is whoever issued
the cash, generally the central bank, ultimately the government.

For private bank-issued cash (historical era; modern era in unusual cases like the
Royal Bank of Scotland's banknotes still in circulation), the same dynamic applies
to the issuing bank.  Lost notes are pure profit.

** What Demurrage Does

A BUCK Note carries two timestamps:
- \( age_0 \): the BUCK-age the printer chose to bake into the note at issuance
  (typically 0 for "fresh", but could be positive to absorb a fractional remainder)
- \( t_{print} \): the block timestamp at which the note was printed

When the note is deposited at time \( t_{spend} \), the SNARK enforces:

\[
v_{\text{deposited}} \;=\; v - 0.02 \times \bigl(age_0 + v \cdot (t_{spend} - t_{print})\bigr)
\]

In English: the note's effective value decreases by 2% of its face value per year of
elapsed time since printing.  A 1,000 BUCK note printed today and deposited in five
years deposits as 900 BUCK.  In 25 years it deposits as 500 BUCK.  In 50 years it is
worthless.

The 50 BUCK of demurrage on a five-year-old note does not vanish.  It flows to the
*Jubilee account* described in [[https://perry.kundert.ca/range/finance/alberta-buck-wormhole][Wormhole]],
which redistributes it pro rata to the issuers who pledged collateral to mint the
original BUCK supply.

** What This Means for Lost Notes

A BUCK Note that is lost in a fire, dropped down a storm drain, or hidden in a wall
by a paranoid grandparent is *not* permanently extracted from the system.  Its value
decays to zero over 50 years, and during that decay, every BUCK of value lost to
demurrage is automatically redistributed to BUCK issuers via the Jubilee account.

The 5-10% of currency that physical cash systems lose forever -- BUCK Cash returns
to the commons within a generation.

This has cascading consequences:

1. *Money supply self-correction.*  No system-wide accounting tail of "missing"
   currency that the central authority must track and write off.  Lost notes write
   themselves off automatically.

2. *Anti-hoarding incentive matches on-chain balances.*  A BUCK held in your wallet
   or in your pocket as cash decays at the same rate.  There is no advantage to
   converting on-chain BUCK to physical cash to evade demurrage; the demurrage clock
   runs the same way in both forms.

3. *Inheritance is automatic.*  A grandparent who hides 100,000 BUCK in cash and
   forgets to tell anyone does not extract 100,000 BUCK from the economy on their
   death.  Over the following 50 years, the value flows back to the Jubilee
   distribution -- which (since BUCK issuers tend to be Albertan asset owners
   pledging real wealth) means it flows back, broadly, into the same economy where
   the grandparent originally earned it.

4. *No central bank profit from currency loss.*  The seigniorage that accrues to
   central banks today from unrecoverable cash does not exist in BUCK Cash.  Lost
   value does not benefit the issuer; it benefits the system as a whole.

5. *Counterfeit-recovery is automatic.*  Even if a note printed years ago is
   somehow forged (which is cryptographically impossible, but humour the
   counterfactual), the value at stake is bounded by the demurrage decay.  An
   attacker forging old notes would be forging worthless paper.

This is, I believe, the first monetary system in history where lost cash returns
to the commons by automatic mechanism rather than by accounting fiat.  It is a
property that physical cash cannot have, because physical cash has no clock.  It
emerges naturally from BUCK's existing demurrage mechanism applied to the
note-as-commitment primitive.

** The Symmetry With On-Chain Balances

A second elegance worth pointing out: the demurrage rate on a BUCK Note is /the same/
as the demurrage rate on an on-chain BUCK balance.  This is by design, and it
matters.

If physical BUCK Cash demurraged faster than on-chain BUCK, holders would prefer
on-chain (and bearer cash would be discouraged, defeating the design's purpose).  If
on-chain BUCK demurraged faster than physical Cash, holders would race to print all
their on-chain balances as physical notes (and the chain would lose its primary use
as a settlement layer).

The matching demurrage rate makes the choice between on-chain and physical purely
about *use case fit*: on-chain for routine commerce, automated payments, large
transactions; physical for offline payments, gifts, savings under the mattress,
disaster preparedness, and so on.  Neither form is privileged by the demurrage
mechanism.

* Physical Security Model

The cryptography only protects what the cryptography touches.  The physical paper
itself needs a security model.  This section sketches one.

** Layered Defense

A printed BUCK Note has three concentric layers of security:

#+LATEX: {\scriptsize
| Layer                   | What it protects                               | Mechanism                                                     |
|-------------------------+------------------------------------------------+---------------------------------------------------------------|
| Inner: secret           | The cryptographic ability to spend             | QR code containing \( s \)                                    |
| Middle: physical seal   | Bearer-mode exclusivity                        | Hologram, scratch-off, laminate sleeve                        |
| Outer: identity binding | Mode B targeting (only intended holder spends) | Stealth address \( pk^* = H(s) \cdot pk_{\text{recipient}} \) |
#+LATEX: }

The inner layer is the cryptographic core: as long as \( s \) is unknown, the note
cannot be spent.  This is unconditional protection against any threat that does not
include observing \( s \).

The middle layer is what makes bearer mode safe to circulate.  An unsealed Mode A
note is photographable, and once photographed by any observer, that observer can
race to spend it.  Sealing the QR under a tamper-evident covering means the note can
be displayed (passed hand-to-hand, stored in a wallet, mailed in an envelope)
without exposure.  The seal must visibly damage on opening so that recipients can
verify they were the first to expose the secret.

The outer layer is what makes Mode B notes safe to *publicly* distribute.  A Mode B
note can be photographed with no security implication: the photographer cannot spend
it without also possessing \( sk_{\text{recipient}} \).  The targeting binding is
cryptographic, not physical.

** Recommended Manufacturing Profile

A practical BUCK Note specification, suitable for industrial-scale printing:

- *Substrate*: cotton-blend banknote paper or polymer (Bank of Canada-style).  Steel
  or titanium plates for high-denomination "permanent" notes.

- *Printing*: standard inkjet or laser printing of QR codes is sufficient for
  cryptographic correctness.  For visual anti-confusion (so people can tell a
  legitimate note from a printout), use UV-reactive ink, microprinting, or
  watermarks -- the same techniques used for traditional banknotes.  These do not
  add cryptographic security; they add *reassurance*.

- *Seal*: peelable hologram covering the secret QR (Mode A), or scratch-off panel
  covering a separate code (also Mode A), or no seal at all (Mode B and Mode C,
  since the cryptography handles exclusivity).

- *Redundancy*: print the QR twice (front and back) with Reed-Solomon error
  correction.  A note that loses one QR to wear or damage can still be deposited
  via the second.  For higher-value notes, also print the secret as base32 digits in
  human-readable form.

- *Issuer mark*: a non-cryptographic logo identifying the issuer (the Alberta Buck
  reserve, a credit union, a commercial issuer).  This is for visual recognition
  only; the cryptographic authenticity check is the on-chain commitment.

- *Denomination indication*: visible face value (1, 5, 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000 BUCK)
  for human convenience.  The actual value is what the on-chain commitment encodes;
  the printed face value is metadata.  A wallet app that scans an apparent 1,000 BUCK
  note and reads "actual value: 100 BUCK" warns the user before deposit.

** The Wallet App

The user-facing software is critical to the system's UX.  A reasonable wallet app
flow when scanning a BUCK Note:

1. Read the QR (or NFC payload, or human-typed digits)
2. Query the chain for the commitment's status:
   - In tree, nullifier unspent → VALID
   - In tree, nullifier spent → DEAD
   - Not in tree → FAKE
3. Check the predicate:
   - Mode A (bearer) → "Tap to Deposit"
   - Mode B (targeted) → if your stored identity matches \( pk^* \), "Tap to Deposit";
     else, "Not For You"
   - Mode C (programmable) → display the predicate in plain English, gate appropriately
4. On Deposit: construct the SNARK locally (or via a delegated prover service for
   thin clients), submit the transaction, display the result

This is a smartphone-class workload.  SNARK construction for a single-note deposit
is on the order of seconds on a modern phone (Aztec's Noir compiler and Zcash's
Halo2 prover both run on mobile).

* Use Cases

The same primitive serves a spectrum of use cases that today require entirely
separate financial instruments.

** Walking-Around Money

Print 100 x 10-BUCK bearer notes, fold them into your wallet, hand them out as
cash.  Recipients deposit when convenient or pass them on as change.  The notes age
at 2% per year, encouraging rapid circulation rather than hoarding.

This is the simplest use case and the closest analogue to a $20 bill.  The user
experience is identical: hand someone paper, they have value.

** Cross-Border Remittances

A BUCK Note is just paper.  It crosses borders in pockets, envelopes, and parcels
at zero marginal cost.  Mode B (targeted to recipient's identity) makes the transfer
useless to anyone except the intended recipient, so loss in transit is not a
financial risk.

This compares favorably to the existing remittance industry, which extracts roughly
6.5% in fees globally on $700+ billion in annual transfers.[fn:remit]  A BUCK Note
remittance has a fixed cost: print + mail.  The mail might cost $1; print costs
fractions of a cent; deposit gas costs a few dollars at most.  At any reasonable
remittance value, the fee compression is enormous.

The recipient does not need a smartphone or internet at the moment of receipt -- they
need them only at the moment they want to convert the note to on-chain BUCK or to
local fiat.  In countries with intermittent connectivity, this is a major usability
improvement over digital remittance services.

** Tip Jars and Charitable Donations

A printed sheet of small-denomination Mode A notes, perforated for tear-off, can be
left next to a tip jar or a donation box.  Patrons take notes (denominated at, say,
2 BUCK each), scan them with their phone, and the value flows to the tip jar's
identity address.

This works without any intermediary, any payment processor fee, or any opening of an
account.  The patron pays in physical cash; the recipient receives in on-chain BUCK.

** Cold Storage and Disaster Preparedness

A Mode C self-targeted note, time-locked until your future use, is a more durable
form of cold storage than a 12-word seed phrase.  The note is *self-evidently* an
instrument of value -- a customs officer, a relative, or your future self
immediately recognizes a paper labeled "1,000 BUCK Note" as something worth keeping
safe.  A seed phrase, by contrast, is opaque to anyone who has not been trained to
recognize it.

For disaster preparedness: a small folder of BUCK Notes in a fireproof safe survives
infrastructure failures that would render exchange accounts and even hardware
wallets unusable.  As long as the chain is reachable somewhere in the world, the
notes can be deposited.  In a true grid-down emergency, the notes circulate
hand-to-hand at face value (with the matching demurrage clock running uniformly,
so prices remain stable in BUCK terms even during a multi-week outage).

** Estate Planning

Mode B notes targeted to your heir's identity, stored in a safe-deposit box: zero
probate process, zero tax-event delay, zero court intervention.  The heir scans on
your death, deposits, the BUCK is theirs.

For larger estates: Mode C notes with a predicate like "spendable by heir after
death-certificate oracle-attested" enable conditional inheritance without a lawyer.
The oracle could be a probate court, a notary network, or a death-registry
integration -- the SNARK does not care, as long as the predicate is well-defined.

** Gift Cards Without Issuer Risk

A traditional gift card carries the risk that the issuing merchant goes bankrupt
between purchase and redemption (cumulative consumer losses to bankrupt gift-card
issuers run into the hundreds of millions annually in North America[fn:giftcard]).
A Mode B BUCK Note targeted to "anyone" (Mode A) or to the recipient's identity
(Mode B) carries no issuer risk: the value is on chain in the BUCK reserve, not
on the merchant's books.

Specialized merchant gift cards become unnecessary -- replaced by BUCK Notes with a
small denomination, optionally bundled with a paper merchant receipt indicating the
intended use.

** Tax-Compliant Compensation

An employer paying a contractor in BUCK Notes faces the same tax-reporting
obligations as paying in cash: payment is income, must be reported.  The on-chain
issuance trail makes this easier to comply with than physical cash, not harder --
the employer's burn transaction is an auditable record of the payment, even though
the contractor's deposit is private.

For employees: employers can issue Mode B notes targeted to each employee's
registered identity, automating payroll while preserving employee privacy from
external observers.

** Underbanked and Cash-Economy Inclusion

This is, perhaps, the most important use case.

The Bank of Canada estimates 1-2% of Canadians are unbanked, with rates an order of
magnitude higher in rural and Indigenous communities.[fn:unbanked]  Globally the
World Bank estimates 1.4 billion adults are unbanked.[fn:globalunbanked]

For these populations, physical cash is not a privacy preference; it is the only
viable financial instrument.  A digital-only currency (CBDC, stablecoin, traditional
e-payment) excludes them by definition.  BUCK Cash includes them: a person without
a bank account, without a smartphone, without literacy in the local language, can
hold and transfer BUCK Notes by physical handover, exactly as they hold and transfer
paper currency today.

When and if they choose to interact with the digital economy, they deposit a note
and the value moves on chain.  Until then, they participate in the BUCK economy on
exactly the terms they participate in the cash economy.

* Selective Disclosure: Compliance Without Surveillance

Bearer instruments are politically controversial.  Most jurisdictions either
regulate them heavily (US bearer bonds were effectively abolished in 1982 by the Tax
Equity and Fiscal Responsibility Act) or enforce strict reporting thresholds on
large cash transactions (Canada's FINTRAC reports any cash transaction above
$10,000).

The concern is not bearer instruments per se; it is the *opacity to law enforcement
and tax authorities* of large bearer-instrument flows.  Money laundering, sanctions
evasion, and tax evasion all exploit bearer-instrument anonymity.

BUCK Cash threads this needle in a way that physical cash and CBDCs both fail.

** The Asymmetry: Issuance vs. Circulation

Every BUCK Note's *issuance* is on chain and identity-bound.  When Alice burns
5,000 BUCK from her registered identity to print five 1,000-BUCK Mode A notes, the
chain records:

- Alice's identity (via the existing PS signature + ElGamal credential machinery)
- The amount burned
- The block timestamp
- Five new commitments added to the tree

This record is *permanent* and *cryptographically traceable to Alice's identity*.

The *circulation* of those notes -- who holds them, who passes them on, who
eventually deposits them -- is private.  The chain sees only the deposit
transaction, which reveals only the recipient's address (the depositor) and the
amount.

This produces an asymmetry:

- *Anyone* can see that "5,000 BUCK was issued as five Mode A notes by [some
  identity] at block 1,234,567"
- *Only Alice or a regulator with Alice's selective-disclosure key* can see that
  "the issuer was Alice"
- *Nobody* can see who held the notes between issuance and deposit
- *Anyone* can see "[some address] deposited a 1,000 BUCK Mode A note at block
  1,234,890"

Linking issuance to deposit requires either: temporal correlation (defeated by a
sufficiently large global note pool), or knowledge of \( s \) (which only a holder
of the note has at any given time).

** The Regulatory Audit Path

A regulator investigating illicit flows has a clean audit path:

1. Identify a suspect deposit on chain (via subpoena to the depositing party's
   identity, KYC obligations on the depositing wallet operator, etc.)
2. Subpoena the issuer of any BUCK held by the suspect: the *issuer* is
   identifiable from the on-chain issuance record (selective disclosure of the
   issuer's identity)
3. Examine the issuer's records for who they paid the note to (this is the issuer's
   responsibility, the same way a bank that issues a cashier's cheque is responsible
   for knowing who they issued it to)

The audit path stops at the first hand-to-hand transfer (which is intentionally
private, as physical cash is private).  But the *origination* of every note is
traceable in a way that physical cash is not -- a $20 bill issued by the Bank of
Canada is identical to every other $20 bill, with no per-bill issuance trail at all.

** What Compliance Looks Like

A reasonable regulatory regime around BUCK Cash:

- *Issuers* (anyone who burns on-chain BUCK to mint notes) must be identity-registered
  and may face reporting obligations on large issuances (analogous to current cash
  withdrawal reporting)

- *Depositors* (anyone who deposits a note to mint on-chain BUCK) face the same
  reporting obligations as cash deposits at a bank today

- *Holders* who simply pass notes hand-to-hand have no reporting obligation, the same
  way you have no obligation to report having $200 in your wallet

- *Demurrage rate* makes large-scale long-term cash hoarding economically unviable
  (the 2%/year decay caps the value of cash-as-store-of-value at roughly the same
  level as a moderate-yield savings account loses to inflation)

This regime is *strictly more privacy-preserving* than the current Canadian dollar
regime (which surveils every electronic transaction) and *strictly more
compliance-friendly* than the current physical cash regime (which provides no
issuance trail at all).

** The CBDC Comparison

China's e-CNY, the European Central Bank's proposed digital euro, the Bank of
Canada's CBDC research -- all of these designs include either explicit or implicit
surveillance capabilities.  The central bank can see, freeze, expire, restrict,
geofence, or retroactively examine any holder's balance.[fn:cbdc]

This is not an accident of the technology; it is a design goal.  The pitch to
governments is precisely that CBDCs give them visibility into civilian payment
flows that physical cash denies them.

BUCK Cash is the opposite design.  Visibility is at the edges (issuance, deposit),
not the center (circulation).  The issuer is identifiable, but only by selective
disclosure with proper authorization (subpoena, court order, regulatory audit
authority), not by everyday observation.  The bearer-circulation phase is private
the way cash is private.

A jurisdiction that adopts BUCK Cash gets *better compliance tools than physical
cash provides today* without giving up *the privacy properties that make cash
worth using*.  This is a genuine Pareto improvement on the current regulatory
trade-off and would, I believe, be politically defensible to even cash-skeptical
authorities.

* Comparison With Prior Art

This section places BUCK Cash in the lineage of digital cash designs from 1982 to
the present, and identifies what each predecessor lacked.

** Chaum's E-Cash (1982-1998)

David Chaum's blind-signature scheme[fn:chaum82] introduced the core idea: an
issuer (a bank) signs a token blinded by the holder so that the bank cannot link
the issuance to the redemption.  When the holder spends the token, the merchant
verifies the signature without revealing who originally received it.

This is a beautiful design and operationally similar to BUCK Cash in spirit.  Its
failures were *institutional*, not cryptographic:

- Required a centralized issuer (the bank)
- Required online verification at spend time (no offline transferability)
- Two-sided market never bootstrapped (no merchants accepted, so no consumers held)
- DigiCash (the commercial implementation) went bankrupt in 1998

BUCK Cash has the same privacy properties without the centralized issuer (any
identity-registered party can mint notes against their own collateral).  Spend-time
verification is on chain rather than via the issuer (so the issuer cannot
selectively block redemptions).  The two-sided market problem is mitigated by
BUCK's existing on-chain ecosystem -- BUCK already exists as a usable currency, so
adopting BUCK Cash is a UX upgrade rather than a greenfield bootstrapping.

** Brands' Offline Divisible E-Cash (1993)

Stefan Brands' scheme[fn:brands93] addressed the offline-spend problem: a
divisible coin can be spent in pieces without contacting the issuer, but
*double-spending mathematically reveals the spender's identity*.

This is the "deterrent rather than prevention" model.  It works in theory but has
two operational issues:

- The deterrent only deters if identity revelation has consequences (it relied on
  a legal/social enforcement layer external to the cryptography)
- The complexity of divisible-coin management was substantial and never produced a
  shipped product

BUCK Cash handles double-spend by *prevention* (global nullifier set), not
deterrence.  No legal layer is required.  Divisibility is achieved by issuing
multiple notes at burn time rather than by a single divisible coin.

** Casascius Coins (2011-2013)

Mike Caldwell's Casascius coins[fn:casascius] were physical Bitcoin: a brass or
silver coin with a holographic seal covering a private-key QR code.  Snap the
seal, scan the QR, the coin's Bitcoin balance was yours.

Casascius coins remain the closest existing analogue to BUCK Cash.  What they
lacked:

- Native integration with the underlying token (the Bitcoin protocol does not
  understand bearer-coin form; the coin is an aftermarket wrapper)
- Mode B targeting (all coins were bearer; no way to issue a coin claimable only by
  a specific recipient)
- Demurrage (a Casascius coin holds its full Bitcoin balance forever, unrelated to
  any economic mechanism)
- Programmable predicates (only one mode: bearer)
- Issuer was a single individual, who eventually stopped issuing in 2013 due to
  FinCEN money-transmitter concerns -- an issuance bottleneck that BUCK Cash
  distributes to all identity-registered participants

BUCK Cash is, in essence, what Casascius would have been if Bitcoin had been
designed with bearer-paper as a first-class instrument from the start, and if the
underlying token had a demurrage and identity layer.

** BIP-38 Paper Wallets

Encrypted Bitcoin private keys printed on paper, with a passphrase needed to spend.
BIP-38 paper wallets are an aftermarket bearer instrument similar to Casascius but
without the physical coin form factor.

Same limitations as Casascius (no native integration, no targeting, no demurrage,
no predicates), plus the additional UX burden of remembering or transmitting the
passphrase.

** Zerocash / Zcash (2014-)

Ben-Sasson, Chiesa, Garman, Green, Miers, Tromer, and Virza's Zerocash[fn:zerocash]
gave us the first scalable on-chain commitment-and-nullifier UTXO model with full
zero-knowledge.  Zcash deployed it in 2016.

Zcash provides on-chain shielded transactions but does *not* extend the model to
physical bearer instruments.  All Zcash value lives on chain in shielded notes.  No
mechanism exists for printing a shielded Zcash note as a paper instrument that
someone else could deposit later.

BUCK Cash uses Zerocash's commitment-and-nullifier primitive for its on-chain
machinery and extends it with the printable-note layer.  The contribution is in the
extension, not the primitive.

** Aztec / Zk.Money

Aztec's predicate-encrypted notes[fn:aztec] generalize Zerocash to private smart
contract calls -- a note can encode arbitrary state operated on by arbitrary
predicates.  This is the cryptographic ancestor of Mode C (programmable BUCK
Notes).

Aztec does not have physical bearer instruments either.  Its predicates are designed
for on-chain DeFi applications (private DEX trades, private lending), not for paper
notes.  BUCK Cash applies the same predicate machinery to a different problem:
specifying spend conditions for a printable bearer instrument.

** Tornado Cash and Privacy Pools

Tornado Cash[fn:tornado] was the simplest possible commitment-and-nullifier mixer:
fixed-denomination deposits in, anonymous withdrawals out.  Sanctioned by OFAC in
2022 for money-laundering use.

Privacy Pools (Buterin, Schaefer, Tromer, et al., 2023)[fn:privacypool] extends
Tornado with per-spend "association sets" -- a depositor can prove their deposit is
in a non-incriminating subset, allowing compliance without identification.

Both are mixer designs, not cash-instrument designs.  Their relevance to BUCK Cash
is the lineage: BUCK's commitment pool is a Tornado-style global mixer, but with
predicates and identity bound in.  The selective-disclosure compliance story
borrows from Privacy Pools' association-set ideas.

* Implementation Path

This section sketches what would actually need to be built to ship BUCK Cash.

** Phase 1: On-Chain Note Pool

The prerequisite for everything else is the global commitment pool described in
[[https://perry.kundert.ca/range/finance/alberta-buck-wormhole][Wormhole]],
"Global Commitment Pool" section.  This is a v2 of the BUCK contract:

- Add a Merkle tree of note commitments
- Add a global nullifier set
- Add a SNARK verifier for the standard "spend a note" predicate (Mode A bearer)
- Add a =burn_to_notes= entry point that takes a value and a vector of commitments,
  appending to the tree

Engineering scope: roughly the size of an Aztec-style note pool.  Substantial but
well-understood; multiple production reference implementations exist (Aztec, Zcash
Sapling, Tornado Cash variants).

** Phase 2: Mode A Bearer Notes

With the note pool in place, Mode A is essentially "Tornado Cash with a paper
output."  Build:

- A wallet app that prints QR codes after a successful =burn_to_notes=
- A wallet app that scans QR codes and submits spend SNARKs
- A printer driver / template library for note layouts
- A scratch-off / hologram seal vendor relationship for high-trust deployments

Engineering scope: a smartphone app and a small print-shop integration.  Not large.

** Phase 3: Mode B Targeted Notes

Add the stealth-address derivation \( pk^* = H(s) \cdot pk_{\text{recipient}} \) and
the corresponding spend predicate.  This re-uses the Chaum-Pedersen machinery from
the Identity protocol.

Engineering scope: a SNARK circuit extension and corresponding wallet-app UX.
Modest.

** Phase 4: Mode C Programmable Notes

Add a circuit-compiler integration so that note printers can supply arbitrary
predicates at issuance time.  Aztec's Noir compiler is the model here -- a
domain-specific language for SNARK predicates that compiles to verifiable circuits.

Engineering scope: substantial, but draws heavily on existing Aztec/Noir tooling.
Could be deferred until after Phases 1-3 prove out the core concept.

** Phase 5: Off-Chain Re-Targeting (Optional)

Proxy re-encryption (e.g., Umbral[fn:umbral]) over Mode B notes would permit
off-chain transfer between identities without consuming the nullifier.  This is
the "Lightning Network for bearer instruments" extension.

Engineering scope: requires a network of proxy nodes (the Identity Guardians could
play this role) and a re-encryption circuit.  Defer until Phases 1-4 demonstrate
demand.

** Regulatory and Legal Engagement

In parallel with engineering, the legal and regulatory work:

- Confirm that BUCK Cash falls within Alberta's provincial jurisdiction over
  property and civil rights (s 92(13)) -- the analysis in the
  [[https://perry.kundert.ca/range/finance/alberta-buck-legal][Legal Foundation]]
  document covers the underlying primitive; bearer notes are an extension that may
  warrant additional treatment
- Engage FINTRAC on the issuance-side reporting model
- Engage the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) on
  reserve treatment
- Coordinate with Alberta Treasury Branches (ATB) as a candidate first issuer

* The Larger Picture

I want to close by stepping back from the technical detail and considering what
BUCK Cash is, in the broader arc of this series.

The Alberta Buck began as an answer to a specific question: how can civilians
access the third monetary element -- Claim money -- that banks currently monopolize?
([[https://perry.kundert.ca/range/finance/missing-monetary-element][The Missing
Monetary Element]] develops the framework.)

The system that emerged -- BUCK_CREDIT, BUCK, BuckK, demurrage, Jubilee, the
Identity Fountain, the Wormhole -- is a complete monetary infrastructure that
operates on chain, with smartphone-mediated user access.  It is sufficient for an
all-digital economy.

But Alberta is not an all-digital economy, and neither is anywhere else.  Cash --
real, physical, hand-to-hand, no-infrastructure-required cash -- remains essential
for billions of people, in normal times and especially in abnormal ones.  A
monetary system that does not have a cash form excludes the populations who need it
most.

BUCK Cash extends Alberta Buck into the cash-using world without compromising any
of the on-chain properties.  The same demurrage, the same identity, the same
reserve, the same Jubilee -- in a form factor that works at a farmers' market, in
a remote community without reliable cellular coverage, in a power outage, across
a border, in a wallet, under a mattress, in an envelope to a relative abroad.

It is, I think, the missing layer that turns Alberta Buck from a sophisticated
financial protocol into something the unbanked elderly can use as easily as the
crypto-literate young.  And it is, I think, the design that finally answers the
question Chaum was asking in 1982: what does digital cash look like, when you take
"cash" seriously?

The answer, forty years later: it looks like a piece of paper with a QR code, a
hologram, a denomination, an issuer mark, and a value that decays at the same rate
as your bank balance does.  Indistinguishable from cash to the user; cryptographically
distinguishable from anything counterfeit; integrated with a complete monetary
system that doesn't require a central bank to function.

Alberta has, uniquely in the world, all four pieces required to build this.  Whether
to assemble them is, as ever, the only question that matters.
