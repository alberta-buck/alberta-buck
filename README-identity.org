#+BEGIN_ABSTRACT
  Every operation in the Alberta Buck ecosystem has identity requirements.  Some
  operations require fully public identity (oracle claims, governance votes).
  Others require only that the participant is a verified identity, without
  disclosing who they are (BUCK transfers).  DeFi integrations (Uniswap pools,
  lending protocols) introduce contract addresses as counterparties, which also
  need identities associated with their deployers.

  Identity credentials are ElGamal ciphertexts encrypted under the participant's
  public key, signed by the issuing authority's private key, and published to the
  issuer's Holochain source chain -- immutable, auditable, and verifiable against
  the issuer's well-known public key.  All participants use ElGamal over the same
  elliptic curve group (alt_bn128), enabling Chaum-Pedersen proofs of correct
  re-encryption: when Alice must share her identity with counterparty Bob, she
  re-encrypts her credential under Bob's public key and produces a proof --
  verifiable by anyone -- that the re-encrypted ciphertext contains the same
  identity as her issuer-signed credential, without revealing the identity
  itself.  On-chain verification costs ~27,000 gas (3-4 elliptic curve scalar
  multiplications via Ethereum precompiles), compared to ~200,000+ gas for
  SNARK-based alternatives.

  This document tabulates the identity requirements for every operation, details
  the ElGamal Chaum-Pedersen cryptographic architecture, and demonstrates through
  attack scenarios that the scheme defeats identity forgery, substitution, and
  collusion attacks while maintaining very low on-chain costs.
#+END_ABSTRACT

* Identity with Anonymity

  The expectation to be able to transact in complete freedom (no one else can prevent it) and
  anonymity (no one else can identify the parties) is paramount.  This is consistent with the
  expectation that one is innocent until proven guilty.  Asking permission from anyone before we can
  buy, sell or trade is unacceptable to any free people.  Anything less turns a free citizen into a
  serf.  If the government can lock your account and freeze you on a park bench at will, how free
  are you?

  However, the expectation that I can trust that my counterparty's identity is /at least/ as
  reliable as the group(s) it is part of is also critical.  Allowing arbitrary hostile groups to
  ravage a community under the cover of darkness has been used throughout history to subjugate free
  people.  If the government can issue identity documents to whomever they wish, and you are forced
  to trust them, how free are you?

  The Alberta Buck enforces these principles.

** Revealing Identity

   If I am suspected of a crime, an Alberta court can compel me to decrypt transactions they suspect
   are related to that crime, and reveal the identity of counterparties I've transacted with.  I may
   resist doing so, and face the consequences: the organization that the Alberta government tasks
   with overseeing the authenticity of my Alberta identity cannot simply authorize decryption of my
   transaction counterparties!  They can only vouch for my (decrypted) identity, just as I can vouch for
   my daughter's.

   Only the participants in a transaction can decrypt the re-encrypted identity
   blobs exchanged during that transaction.  Each re-encrypted identity is an
   ElGamal ciphertext under the specific counterparty's public key -- only that
   counterparty's private key can recover the plaintext.  The identity issuer
   cannot decrypt it (they encrypted under Alice's public key, not their own).
   The government cannot decrypt it.  No registry operator or Holochain node
   can decrypt it.  A court must compel a /specific participant/ to decrypt a
   /specific transaction's/ identity data.  The attack cost is O(N): each
   individual's credential must be attacked separately, rather than compromising
   a single custodian.


** Accounts Belong to Someone

   I may be a citizen of Alberta and have a minor child.  My child's identity is vouched by me, and
   I am vouched by Alberta.  I have several accounts associated with encrypted copies of my identity
   (income, savings, etc) and several unencrypted (walking around money).  I can transfer money from
   my savings to my walking around account (anonymously, via my BUCK account at ATB).

   My encrypted savings account Identity has been authorized by ATB to send funds.  My walking
   around money account has a plaintext Identity, so Starbucks' BUCK account doesn't need to
   pre-authorize incoming transactions -- it accepts BUCK transfers from any BUCK account with a
   public identity.

   This just cuts out the middleman, and gives every citizen what government and institutions
   already have: knowledge of the participants in transactions with public accounts.  If I prefer, I
   can set up a Starbucks account, and ask them to authorize me to send money to them.  Again,
   allowing with BUCKs what is already allowed with a pre-loaded Starbucks card -- private
   transactions with a public account.  Starbucks and Visa both know who paid to fund the Starbucks
   card, and who funded their Starbucks account with that card.  Now, citizens can set up similar
   arrangements and retain that knowledge amongst themselves.

   If a court compels one of the participants (for example, as the result of an arrest in a sting
   operation against some members of a group performing some illicit activity), then that persons
   transactions with other members of the group can be discovered.  Otherwise: /financial activity
   remains confidential, at the sole choice the participants/.

   An expectation that any government or bank functionary (or any random attacker, due to government
   or bank incompetence) can know the details of a citizens financial activities is unacceptable.
   The attack cost is O(N) vs. O(1); every individual's credential must be attacked, instead of just
   being able to attack or compel one disgruntled government or bank employee.
   

** Counterparties Exchange Identities

   Each transaction is between parties identified to each other by provably
   untampered copies of their identity, encrypted by each party specifically for
   that counterparty.

   The identity credential lifecycle:

   1. *Issuance*: A trusted issuer (the Alberta government, ATB Financial, etc.)
      verifies Alice's identity through a KYC ceremony.  The issuer encrypts
      Alice's identity data ~I~ under Alice's public key using ElGamal over
      alt_bn128:

      ~E_alice = (r * G,  r * pk_alice + I * G)~

      The issuer signs this ciphertext with their well-known private key:

      ~sigma = Sign(sk_issuer, E_alice)~

      The signed credential ~(E_alice, sigma)~ is published to the issuer's
      Holochain source chain, where it is immutable, auditable by anyone, and
      verifiable against the issuer's well-known public key.  A reference (hash
      of ~E_alice~) is registered in the on-chain IdentityRegistry alongside
      Alice's Ethereum address.

   2. *Re-encryption*: When Alice must identify herself to counterparty Bob
      (e.g., during an ~approve~ call on the BUCK contract), she decrypts her
      credential locally (she knows ~sk_alice~) and re-encrypts the identity
      under Bob's public key with fresh randomness:

      ~E_bob = (r' * G,  r' * pk_bob + I * G)~

      She produces a Chaum-Pedersen proof ~pi~ that ~E_bob~ encrypts the same
      point ~I * G~ as her issuer-signed ~E_alice~ -- without revealing ~I~.

   3. *Verification*: Anyone (including the BUCK smart contract) can verify:
      - ~sigma~ is a valid signature from the issuer on ~E_alice~
        (the credential is genuine, not self-issued)
      - ~pi~ proves ~E_bob~ encrypts the same plaintext as ~E_alice~
        (Alice did not substitute false data)

      Verification requires only 3-4 elliptic curve scalar multiplications
      (~27,000 gas on Ethereum via the alt_bn128 precompiles).  No SNARK
      circuits, no trusted setup, no pairing operations.

   4. *Decryption*: Only Bob can decrypt ~E_bob~ (he has ~sk_bob~).  Only Alice
      can decrypt ~E_alice~ (she has ~sk_alice~).  The issuer cannot decrypt
      either (they encrypted under Alice's public key, not their own).  No third
      party can decrypt any re-encrypted copy.

   There is no need for Proxy Re-Encryption (PRE), because Alice is online when
   she calls ~approve~ and can perform the re-encryption directly.  The
   Chaum-Pedersen proof is the trust anchor: it is a mathematical guarantee --
   unforgeable under the discrete logarithm assumption -- that Alice
   re-encrypted her genuine, issuer-signed identity and not a substitute.


** ElGamal Over a Mandated Curve Group

   Alberta Buck mandates that all identity credentials, all participant key
   pairs, and all re-encryption operations use ElGamal over the alt_bn128
   elliptic curve -- the curve supported by Ethereum's ~ecAdd~ / ~ecMul~
   precompiles at addresses 0x06 and 0x07.  This mandate is a design strength,
   not a limitation:

   - *Uniform proof verification*: every Chaum-Pedersen proof uses the same
     group, so a single on-chain verifier handles all identity proofs.
   - *Precompile efficiency*: alt_bn128 scalar multiplication costs 6,000 gas
     via the EVM precompile, vs. ~100,000+ gas for in-contract EC math.
   - *No cross-group compatibility issues*: since all participants use the same
     curve, re-encryption between any two participants is always algebraically
     compatible.

   Participant key pairs for identity purposes are distinct from their Ethereum
   signing keys (which use secp256k1).  The identity key pair is generated
   during the KYC ceremony and the public key is registered in the
   IdentityRegistry alongside the participant's Ethereum address.

   When Alice re-encrypts for N different counterparties, each re-encryption
   uses independent randomness.  The resulting ciphertexts are IND-CPA secure:
   computationally indistinguishable from random curve points.  An observer
   who collects all N ciphertexts cannot determine that they encrypt the same
   plaintext (without access to a counterparty's private key).


** Chaum-Pedersen Proof of Re-Encryption Correctness

   The Chaum-Pedersen protocol is a sigma protocol (Schnorr family) that proves
   two ElGamal ciphertexts encrypt the same message point, without revealing the
   message.  Made non-interactive via the Fiat-Shamir heuristic, the proof is
   compact (~128 bytes: two scalars and two curve points) and verification
   requires only 3-4 multi-scalar multiplications.

*** Setup

    #+begin_example
    Issuer-signed credential (public, on issuer's Holochain source chain):
      E_alice = (R_a, C_a) = (r_a * G,  r_a * pk_alice + M)
      where M = I * G  (identity encoded as a curve point)
      sigma = Sign(sk_issuer, E_alice)

    Alice's re-encryption for Bob:
      E_bob = (R_b, C_b) = (r_b * G,  r_b * pk_bob + M)
      using the SAME message point M
    #+end_example

*** What Alice Proves (Non-Interactively)

    #+begin_example
    "I know (sk_alice, r_b) such that:
      1. pk_alice = sk_alice * G                    -- I own this public key
      2. R_b = r_b * G                              -- I know E_bob's randomness
      3. C_a - sk_alice * R_a = C_b - r_b * pk_bob  -- same message point M"
    #+end_example

    Statement 3 is the core.  The left side decrypts ~E_alice~ (only Alice can
    compute this, since she knows ~sk_alice~), yielding the message point ~M~.
    The right side extracts ~M~ from ~E_bob~ (only Bob could alternatively
    compute this with ~sk_bob~).  If Alice encrypted different data ~I_fake~,
    then ~I * G != I_fake * G~ and no valid proof exists -- it is not merely hard
    to compute, it is mathematically impossible under the discrete logarithm
    assumption.

*** Verification (What the Contract Computes)

    #+begin_example
    Given public inputs:
      E_alice = (R_a, C_a)    -- from IdentityRegistry (issuer-signed, read
                                  from storage -- NOT supplied by Alice)
      E_bob   = (R_b, C_b)    -- from Alice's approve() calldata
      pk_alice                 -- from IdentityRegistry
      pk_bob                   -- from IdentityRegistry
      proof   = (e, s1, s2)   -- Fiat-Shamir challenge and responses

    Verify:
      Recompute challenge e from commitments
      Check consistency of s1, s2 against e, the public keys, and ciphertexts
      3-4 ecMul + 3-4 ecAdd operations
    #+end_example

    Critical implementation requirement: ~E_alice~ is *read from on-chain
    storage* (the IdentityRegistry), not supplied by Alice as calldata.  This
    anchors the proof to the issuer-signed credential.  If Alice could supply
    her own ~E_alice~, she could substitute a fake credential and produce a
    valid proof against it.

*** Gas Cost

    | Operation               | Count | Gas each | Total  |
    |-------------------------+-------+----------+--------|
    | ~ecMul~ (precompile)    |     4 |    6,000 | 24,000 |
    | ~ecAdd~ (precompile)    |     4 |      150 |    600 |
    | ~keccak256~ (Fiat-Shamir) | 1   |       36 |     36 |
    | Calldata (128 bytes)    |       |          |  2,048 |
    | Storage read (~E_alice~) |   1  |    2,100 |  2,100 |
    | *Total*                 |       |          | *~29,000 gas* |

    Compare: Groth16 SNARK verification requires a pairing check (~113,000 gas
    for the ~ecPairing~ precompile alone) plus additional EC operations, totaling
    ~200,000-300,000 gas.  The Chaum-Pedersen approach is *7-10x cheaper*.

    The ~approve~ call (which includes the proof verification) costs ~29,000 gas
    for the identity proof plus the standard ERC-20 approve cost (~46,000 gas),
    totaling ~75,000 gas.  This is paid once per counterparty pair.  Subsequent
    ~transfer~ and ~transferFrom~ calls emit only minimal receipt events
    (addresses + opaque hashes, ~2,000 gas) with no additional proof work.


* Attacks and Defenses

  The following scenarios demonstrate that the ElGamal Chaum-Pedersen proof
  scheme defeats identity fraud, even when participants are actively criminal.
  In each scenario we show what the attacker attempts, why it fails
  mathematically, and what the on-chain verifier sees.

  The trust chain in every scenario is:

  #+begin_example
  Issuer signs E_alice           --> credential is genuine
  E_alice stored in registry     --> contract reads the genuine credential
  Chaum-Pedersen proof verified  --> E_bob encrypts same identity as E_alice
  Only Bob can decrypt E_bob     --> identity revealed only to counterparty
  #+end_example

  Breaking any link requires breaking either ECDSA signature unforgeability or
  the discrete logarithm assumption on alt_bn128 -- both are standard hardness
  assumptions underpinning all of Ethereum's security.


** Attack 1: Alice Substitutes a False Identity for Bob

*** Scenario

    Alice is a sanctioned individual.  She completed KYC under her real identity
    and received a legitimate issuer-signed credential ~E_alice~ containing her
    true identity ~I~.  She now wants to transact with Bob but wants Bob (and
    any future court) to believe she is someone else -- say, her clean associate
    Carol.

    Alice constructs a fake re-encryption using Carol's identity ~I_fake~:

    #+begin_example
    E_bob_fake = (r' * G,  r' * pk_bob + I_fake * G)
    #+end_example

    She submits ~E_bob_fake~ to ~approve()~ and attempts to produce a
    Chaum-Pedersen proof tying it to her registry credential.

*** Why It Fails

    The Chaum-Pedersen verification checks:

    #+begin_example
    C_a - sk_alice * R_a  ==  C_b - r' * pk_bob
    #+end_example

    Expanding the left side (from the issuer-signed ~E_alice~ in the registry):

    #+begin_example
    (r_a * pk_alice + I * G) - sk_alice * (r_a * G)
      = r_a * sk_alice * G + I * G - sk_alice * r_a * G
      = I * G
    #+end_example

    Expanding the right side (from Alice's fake ~E_bob~):

    #+begin_example
    (r' * pk_bob + I_fake * G) - r' * pk_bob
      = I_fake * G
    #+end_example

    The proof requires ~I * G == I_fake * G~, which requires ~I == I_fake~.
    Since ~I != I_fake~ (Alice is not Carol), these are distinct curve points.
    Under the discrete logarithm assumption, Alice *cannot produce valid proof
    scalars (s1, s2)* that satisfy the verification equations.  The proof does
    not exist -- not "hard to find," but mathematically non-existent.

*** What the Contract Sees

    ~approve()~ reverts: "Re-encryption proof invalid."  The transaction fails
    on-chain.  Alice cannot complete the identity handshake with false data.
    The revert is indistinguishable from a malformed transaction -- no
    information about Alice's intent leaks.

*** Cost to Alice

    She wasted gas on a failed transaction.  Her address is still associated with
    her real issuer-signed credential in the registry.  Nothing has changed.


** Attack 2: Alice Forges an Issuer Credential

*** Scenario

    Alice never completed KYC, or completed KYC but wants a credential with
    different identity data.  She generates her own ElGamal key pair, encrypts a
    fabricated identity ~I_fake~ under her public key, and signs it herself:

    #+begin_example
    E_alice_fake = (r * G,  r * pk_alice + I_fake * G)
    sigma_fake   = Sign(sk_alice, E_alice_fake)
    #+end_example

    She attempts to register this credential in the IdentityRegistry and publish
    it to a Holochain source chain.

*** Why It Fails

    The IdentityRegistry accepts credentials only from *trusted issuers* --
    public keys registered in the TrustedIssuersRegistry (part of the ERC-3643
    framework).  Registration verifies:

    #+begin_example
    SigVerify(pk_trusted_issuer, E_alice, sigma) == true
    #+end_example

    Alice's ~sigma_fake~ was signed with ~sk_alice~, not with any trusted
    issuer's key.  The signature check fails.  Her credential is rejected at
    registration, long before any ~approve~ call.

    Even if Alice publishes ~(E_alice_fake, sigma_fake)~ to a Holochain source
    chain, the on-chain registry will not accept it.  Holochain provides
    immutability and auditability of the /issuer's/ source chain; it does not
    confer trust on self-issued credentials.  The trust anchor is the issuer's
    signature, verified on-chain.

*** What the Contract Sees

    ~registerIdentity()~ reverts: "Invalid issuer signature."  Alice is not
    registered; ~isVerified(alice) == false~.  She cannot participate in any
    BUCK operation requiring identity.


** Attack 3: Alice and Complicit Bob Launder With False Identities

*** Scenario

    Alice and Bob are both criminals.  Both have legitimate issuer-signed
    credentials (they passed KYC under their real identities).  They want to
    transact with each other, but want the encrypted identity records to show
    /different/ people -- so that if a court later compels Bob to decrypt
    Alice's identity blob, it points to an innocent third party.

    They agree: Alice will encrypt a false identity ~I_fake~ for Bob and submit
    it.  Bob will claim (if asked) that the decrypted identity is genuine.

*** Why It Fails

    Alice's ~approve()~ call must pass Chaum-Pedersen verification.  The
    contract reads ~E_alice~ from the IdentityRegistry -- not from Alice's
    calldata.

    #+begin_example
    // Inside approve():
    (E_alice, sigma) = identityRegistry.getCredential(msg.sender);
    require(chaumPedersenVerify(E_alice, E_bob_submitted, proof));
    #+end_example

    Alice cannot substitute a different ~E_alice~ because the contract reads it
    from storage.  She cannot produce a valid Chaum-Pedersen proof linking her
    fake ~E_bob~ to the real ~E_alice~ because the message points differ (same
    math as Attack 1).

    *Bob's willingness to accept false data is irrelevant.*  The proof was
    verified against the issuer-signed credential /before/ ~E_bob~ was stored.
    If ~approve()~ succeeded, ~E_bob~ necessarily contains Alice's real identity.
    When a court later compels Bob to decrypt, he produces Alice's true identity
    -- regardless of any prior agreement between them.

    Bob cannot produce a /different/ plaintext from the same ~E_bob~ (ElGamal
    decryption is deterministic given the private key).  Bob cannot claim the
    ~E_bob~ is unrelated to Alice (the on-chain ~IdentityExchange~ event records
    that Alice submitted it during ~approve~, and the proof verification
    succeeded).

*** What the Contract Sees

    If Alice submits false data: ~approve()~ reverts ("Re-encryption proof
    invalid") -- same as Attack 1.

    If Alice submits real data (because she has no choice): ~approve()~ succeeds,
    and ~E_bob~ provably contains her real identity.  The laundering scheme
    fails silently -- Alice and Bob transacted, but the identity trail is
    genuine and court-recoverable.


** Attack 4: Alice Repudiates a Transaction She Made

*** Scenario

    Alice transacted legitimately with Bob (a law-abiding merchant).  Later,
    Alice is investigated.  She claims she never transacted with Bob, or that the
    identity Bob received was fabricated by Bob.  Alice hopes that because the
    re-encrypted blob is opaque to everyone except Bob, a court cannot verify
    its authenticity without trusting Bob's word.

*** Why It Fails

    The on-chain record contains everything needed to verify, without trusting
    Bob:

    1. The ~IdentityExchange(alice, bob, E_bob)~ event is on-chain, emitted
       during Alice's ~approve()~ call.  It was sent from Alice's address
       (verified by ~msg.sender~).

    2. The ~approve()~ transaction succeeded on-chain, which means the
       Chaum-Pedersen proof verified against Alice's issuer-signed ~E_alice~
       from the registry.  This is an immutable execution fact.

    3. Therefore ~E_bob~ provably encrypts the same identity as ~E_alice~.
       This follows from the soundness of the Chaum-Pedersen protocol -- it is a
       mathematical fact, not testimony.

    4. When the court compels Bob to decrypt ~E_bob~, the resulting plaintext ~I~
       is provably Alice's issuer-certified identity.  The chain of trust:

    #+begin_example
    Issuer signed E_alice                        (verified: sigma)
    E_alice stored in IdentityRegistry           (on-chain, immutable)
    Alice called approve() with E_bob + proof    (on-chain tx, msg.sender = alice)
    Chaum-Pedersen proof verified on-chain        (execution succeeded)
    ==> E_bob encrypts Alice's real identity      (mathematical fact)
    Bob decrypts E_bob --> I                      (compelled, deterministic)
    I is Alice's issuer-certified identity        (proven)
    #+end_example

*** What the Court Sees

    An unbroken, cryptographically verified chain from issuer to decrypted
    identity.  Alice's repudiation is contradicted by on-chain proof
    verification records that she cannot alter or dispute.


** Attack 5: Criminal Bob Denies Receiving Alice's Identity

*** Scenario

    Bob is a criminal merchant.  Alice (law-abiding) transacted with Bob.
    Authorities investigate Bob.  Bob claims he never received Alice's identity
    and cannot produce it -- hoping to deny knowledge of his counterparties,
    or to shield Alice from being identified as his customer.

*** Why It Fails

    The ~IdentityExchange(alice, bob, E_bob)~ event is on-chain and immutable.
    The court retrieves ~E_bob~ directly from the blockchain -- Bob's cooperation
    is not needed to find the ciphertext.

    Bob is compelled to decrypt:

    #+begin_example
    M = C_b - sk_bob * R_b
    #+end_example

    If Bob claims he "lost" his private key:

    - The court observes that Bob's address continues to transact on Ethereum
      (signing transactions requires the corresponding private key).
    - The court holds Bob in contempt, as it would for any refusal to produce
      subpoenaed records.
    - Even without Bob's cooperation, the court can establish from the on-chain
      proof verification that ~E_bob~ /does/ contain a genuine identity.
      Bob's refusal to decrypt is itself evidence of obstruction.

    Bob cannot claim ~E_bob~ is meaningless or fabricated: the successful
    Chaum-Pedersen proof verification is an on-chain fact.

*** What the Court Sees

    ~E_bob~ is on-chain.  The proof that it contains Alice's real identity is
    on-chain.  Bob decrypts or faces contempt.  The cryptographic record is
    self-authenticating.


** Attack 6: Eve Links Alice's Identities Across Counterparties

*** Scenario

    Eve is a surveillance actor (competitor, stalker, rogue state).  She observes
    that Alice's address called ~approve~ for multiple counterparties: Bob,
    Carol, and Dave.  Eve collects the ~IdentityExchange~ events:

    #+begin_example
    IdentityExchange(alice_addr, bob_addr,   E_bob)
    IdentityExchange(alice_addr, carol_addr, E_carol)
    IdentityExchange(alice_addr, dave_addr,  E_dave)
    #+end_example

    Eve wants to learn Alice's real identity, or to determine whether these
    transactions reveal a pattern (e.g., Alice's spending habits).

*** Why It Fails (Content Privacy Holds)

    Each re-encryption uses independent randomness:

    #+begin_example
    E_bob   = (r_1 * G,  r_1 * pk_bob   + I * G)
    E_carol = (r_2 * G,  r_2 * pk_carol + I * G)
    E_dave  = (r_3 * G,  r_3 * pk_dave  + I * G)
    #+end_example

    The ciphertexts are IND-CPA secure (semantic security of ElGamal): each is
    computationally indistinguishable from a random pair of curve points.  Eve
    cannot decrypt any of them without the counterparty's private key.  She
    cannot determine from the ciphertexts alone what identity they encode, nor
    can she compare them to determine if they encode the same identity.

    *What Eve does know*: all three events originate from ~alice_addr~.  But
    this is exactly the information already visible in standard ERC-20 ~Approval~
    events.  The encrypted identity blobs add *zero additional linkability*
    beyond Ethereum's inherent pseudonymous address model.

    *What Eve does not know*: Alice's name, jurisdiction, ID number, or any
    attribute of her identity.  She sees an address; she does not see a person.

*** What Eve Sees

    Pseudonymous Ethereum addresses and opaque ciphertexts that are
    computationally indistinguishable from random.  The same view she has of any
    ERC-20 token, plus meaningless (to her) encrypted blobs.


** Attack 7: Alice Uses a Revoked or Expired Credential

*** Scenario

    Alice's identity credential was revoked by the issuer (e.g., her Alberta
    residency expired, fraud was detected in her original KYC documents, or she
    is subject to new sanctions).  Alice still holds her old ~E_alice~ and
    ~sigma~ and attempts to continue transacting.

*** Why It Fails

    The IdentityRegistry tracks credential validity.  When the issuer revokes
    Alice's credential:

    #+begin_example
    identityRegistry.revokeIdentity(alice_address)
    #+end_example

    Subsequent calls to ~isVerified(alice)~ return false.  Alice's ~approve~
    call -- and any ~transfer~ or ~transferFrom~ -- reverts at the identity
    check, before the Chaum-Pedersen proof is even evaluated.

    Alice's old credential and proofs remain mathematically valid (the
    cryptography is eternal), but the contract refuses to accept them because
    her registration is no longer active.  The revocation is a separate on-chain
    state, controlled by the trusted issuer.

*** What the Contract Sees

    ~isVerified(alice) == false~.  Transaction reverts immediately.  The
    still-valid-but-revoked credential is never consulted.


** Attack 8: Unwitting Bob Receives a Transaction From Criminal Alice

*** Scenario

    Bob is a law-abiding merchant running a BUCK-accepting storefront.  Alice is
    a criminal who passed KYC (perhaps before she was sanctioned, or under a
    jurisdiction that has not yet flagged her).  Alice sends BUCK to Bob through
    a normal transaction.  Bob has no way to know Alice is a criminal at the
    time of the transaction.

    Later, authorities investigate Alice.  Bob is concerned: did he unknowingly
    participate in money laundering?  Can Alice's identity in the receipt
    exonerate him?

*** Why the Scheme Protects Bob

    Bob received Alice's re-encrypted identity ~E_bob~ during the ~approve~
    handshake (or Bob pre-approved Alice via ~approve(alice, 0, ...)~ to
    receive from her).  The Chaum-Pedersen proof verified on-chain, so ~E_bob~
    provably contains Alice's real, issuer-certified identity.

    When authorities investigate:

    1. Bob decrypts ~E_bob~ and produces Alice's identity ~I~.
    2. The on-chain proof record establishes that ~I~ is genuine (not something
       Bob fabricated to frame Alice, and not something Alice fabricated to
       deceive Bob).
    3. Bob can demonstrate he had no way to know Alice was a criminal: the
       identity he received was a valid, issuer-signed credential at the time
       of the transaction.  The revocation (if any) came later.
    4. Bob's good faith is supported by the cryptographic record: he accepted a
       transaction from a verified participant and holds a court-presentable
       receipt.

    The system protects honest participants by creating a tamper-proof record
    of who they transacted with.  Bob is not liable for Alice's criminality;
    he is protected by the same cryptographic chain that convicts Alice.

*** What the Court Sees

    Bob holds a provably genuine receipt.  Alice's identity was valid at
    transaction time.  Bob acted in good faith.  The receipt is evidence of
    Bob's compliance, not his complicity.


** Summary: Why Chaum-Pedersen Defeats All Substitution Attacks

   Every attack that involves substituting false identity data fails for the
   same fundamental reason: the Chaum-Pedersen proof is *sound*.  A valid proof
   for a false statement does not exist.

   #+begin_example
   The proof asserts:  E_bob encrypts the same M as E_alice
   The contract reads: E_alice from the registry (issuer-signed, immutable)
   Therefore:          E_bob must encrypt Alice's real identity
   Alice cannot:       supply a different E_alice (contract reads from storage)
   Alice cannot:       produce a valid proof for different M (soundness)
   Alice cannot:       forge the issuer's signature (ECDSA unforgeability)
   Bob cannot:         produce a different plaintext (ElGamal decryption is deterministic)
   Eve cannot:         decrypt E_bob (she lacks sk_bob)
   #+end_example

   Two independent hardness assumptions protect the system:
   1. *Signature unforgeability* (ECDSA): prevents forged credentials
   2. *Discrete logarithm hardness* (alt_bn128): prevents false proofs and
      unauthorized decryption

   Both assumptions also underpin Ethereum itself.  If either breaks, Ethereum's
   own security fails first -- the Alberta Buck identity system does not
   introduce any novel cryptographic assumptions.

* Contract Identity: How DeFi Pools Become BUCK Counterparties

  A Uniswap pool contract is just an address.  For BUCK transfers to/from that
  address to pass ~isVerified~, the contract must have an identity in the
  IdentityRegistry.  But a contract can't do KYC.  The solution:

  *The pool deployer's identity becomes the contract's identity.*

  When someone creates a BUCK/USDT Uniswap pool:

  1. Deployer (a verified BUCK participant) calls the Uniswap factory to create
     the pool.  The factory deploys a new contract at a deterministic address.

  2. Deployer (or a registry agent) calls:
     ~identityRegistry.registerIdentity(poolAddress, deployer.onchainId, country)~

     This links the pool contract's address to the deployer's ONCHAINID.  The
     pool inherits the deployer's identity claims.

  3. Now ~isVerified(poolAddress)~ returns true.  BUCK transfers to/from the
     pool pass the identity check.

  4. Encrypted receipts for pool transactions list the deployer's identity as
     the counterparty, because the deployer is the legal operator of the pool.

  This means: if you deploy a BUCK DeFi pool, you are the identified legal
  operator.  Your identity is on the line for every transaction through that
  pool.  If a court subpoenas you, you can be compelled to decrypt the receipts
  (because your key is one of the encryption parties).


* The Receipt Problem: What Can Be Public, What Cannot

  *Events are public.*  Every ~emit~ on Ethereum is readable by every node.
  The previous design emitted ONCHAINID addresses and ECDH public keys in
  receipt events -- this would create a permanent, public, linkable identity
  graph of every BUCK transfer.  That defeats the entire privacy model.

  The core constraints:

  1. *The EVM is transparent*: every contract execution, every storage slot,
     every emitted event is publicly reconstructible.  The BUCK contract
     cannot hold decrypted identity data, even transiently.

  2. *The contract is trusted*: the BUCK contract enforces ~isVerified~ and
     can verify proofs.  What it cannot do is learn secrets.

  3. *The identity must be re-encrypted*: when Alice wants to share her
     identity with Bob (for a receipt), she must produce a ciphertext that
     Bob can decrypt but nobody else can -- and the BUCK contract must be
     confident this ciphertext contains Alice's *real* identity, not garbage.

  4. *DeFi timing*: in a ~transferFrom~ call, the router cannot pass receipt
     data.  Identity exchange must happen earlier (at ~approve~ time).

** The Fundamental Challenge: Proving Re-Encryption Correctness

   Alice's identity ~I~ is stored encrypted on Holochain, committed on-chain
   as ~C = hash(I, salt)~ (a Pedersen commitment or similar).  When she wants
   to share her identity with Bob for a transfer receipt, she must:

   1. Decrypt ~I~ (she has her private key)
   2. Re-encrypt ~I~ under Bob's public key: ~E_bob = Encrypt(I, ECDH(k_alice, K_bob))~
   3. Prove to the BUCK contract that ~E_bob~ encrypts the *same* ~I~ that
      ~C~ commits to -- *without revealing ~I~ to the contract*

   This is a *zero-knowledge proof of correct re-encryption*.

*** What the ZK Proof States

    #+begin_example
    Public inputs  (visible to contract and everyone):
        C          -- Alice's identity commitment (on-chain, from ONCHAINID)
        E_bob      -- Alice's identity re-encrypted for Bob
        K_bob      -- Bob's public key (from his ONCHAINID or from approve)

    Private inputs (known only to Alice):
        I          -- Alice's plaintext identity
        salt       -- commitment randomness
        k_alice    -- Alice's private key

    Proof statement:
        C == Commit(I, salt)
        AND  E_bob == Encrypt(I, ECDH(k_alice, K_bob))
    #+end_example

    The contract verifies the ZK proof (~200K-300K gas for Groth16 SNARK
    verification).  If valid, the contract knows ~E_bob~ is a correct
    re-encryption of the committed identity.  The contract never learns ~I~.

*** Proxy Re-Encryption (PRE): An Alternative

    NuCypher's Umbral scheme[fn:5] provides threshold proxy re-encryption
    where a proxy transforms a ciphertext from Alice's key to Bob's key
    without decrypting.  Umbral includes non-interactive zero-knowledge
    (NIZK) proofs of re-encryption correctness.

    In the Alberta Buck context:

    #+begin_example
    1. Alice's identity is encrypted under her key: E_alice = Encrypt(I, K_alice)
    2. Alice computes a re-encryption key: rk_{A->B} = ReKeyGen(k_alice, K_bob)
    3. The Holochain hApp (acting as proxy) applies:
       E_bob = ReEncrypt(rk_{A->B}, E_alice)
    4. The hApp produces a NIZK proof that re-encryption was correct
    5. Alice submits (E_bob, proof) to the BUCK contract
    6. Contract verifies the proof against E_alice (committed on-chain)
    #+end_example

    The proxy (Holochain hApp) never sees ~I~.  It transforms one ciphertext
    into another without decrypting.  The NIZK proof lets the contract verify
    correctness.

*** Why Holochain for Proof Generation

    ZK proof generation is computationally expensive (seconds to minutes for
    complex circuits).  Holochain provides:

    - *Trusted code*: the hApp DNA is identified by hash; anyone can verify
      the exact code that generated the proof.  Unlike a random off-chain
      service, the hApp code is deterministic and auditable.
    - *No gas cost*: proof generation runs on the participant's Holochain
      node, not on Ethereum.  The only on-chain cost is proof verification.
    - *Agent-centric storage*: Alice's identity data lives on her own source
      chain, encrypted.  The hApp decrypts locally, re-encrypts for Bob,
      generates the proof -- all within Alice's node.  Nothing leaves her
      node except ~E_bob~ and the proof.

    However, Holochain is NOT sufficient alone for trust.  Alice runs her
    own node and could theoretically run modified hApp code.  The ZK proof
    is the trust anchor: it is mathematically verifiable on-chain regardless
    of what software generated it.  Holochain provides the execution
    environment; the ZK proof provides the guarantee.

** Homomorphic Equality Check Without Decryption

   Perry's question: "Is there a homomorphic encryption process by which we
   can compare an encrypted value as equal, but the party checking for
   equality cannot decrypt the data?"

   Yes.  Several approaches:

*** Pedersen Commitments

    ~Commit(v, r) = v*G + r*H~ where G, H are generator points.  The
    commitment hides ~v~ (information-theoretically) but binds to it
    (computationally, under discrete log assumption).

    To prove two commitments ~C_1 = Commit(v, r_1)~ and ~C_2 = Commit(v, r_2)~
    contain the same value:

    #+begin_example
    Proof: reveal delta_r = r_1 - r_2
    Verifier checks: C_1 - C_2 == delta_r * H
    #+end_example

    The verifier learns nothing about ~v~, only that both commitments
    bind to the same value.  This is efficient (~1 EC point addition).

*** Pedersen + ZK Range Proof (Bulletproofs)

    If the identity is a structured value (not just a scalar), Bulletproofs[fn:6]
    can prove properties of committed values without revealing them:
    range proofs, equality proofs, membership proofs.  Verification is
    ~O(log n)~ and practical on-chain.

*** Application to Re-Encryption Verification

    The BUCK contract stores Alice's identity commitment:
    ~C_alice = Commit(I, r_alice)~

    Alice provides a re-encrypted blob ~E_bob~ and a ZK proof that the
    plaintext of ~E_bob~ is the same ~I~ committed in ~C_alice~.

    The contract verifies the proof.  It never learns ~I~.  It learns only
    that ~E_bob~ contains the correct identity, re-encrypted for Bob.

** The ~approve~ Function as Identity Handshake

   The ERC-20 ~approve~ function is the natural point for identity exchange.
   Alice is directly calling the BUCK contract -- she can provide the
   re-encrypted identity blob and the ZK proof:

   #+begin_example
   function approve(
       address spender,
       uint256 amount,
       bytes   calldata reEncryptedIdentity,   // E_bob: her identity for spender
       bytes   calldata reEncryptionProof       // ZK proof of correct re-encryption
   ) public returns (bool) {
       _approve(msg.sender, spender, amount);

       // Verify the re-encryption proof against the caller's on-chain commitment
       bytes32 identityCommitment = identityRegistry.identityCommitment(msg.sender);
       require(
           _verifyReEncryption(identityCommitment, reEncryptedIdentity, reEncryptionProof),
           "Invalid re-encryption proof"
       );

       // Store the verified re-encrypted identity for this pair
       _receiptFragments[msg.sender][spender] = keccak256(reEncryptedIdentity);

       // Emit ONLY the re-encrypted blob (opaque to everyone except spender)
       emit IdentityExchange(msg.sender, spender, reEncryptedIdentity);
       return true;
   }
   #+end_example

   What the public sees in the ~IdentityExchange~ event:
   - Alice's address approved a spender
   - An opaque blob (~reEncryptedIdentity~) that only the spender can decrypt
   - *No* ONCHAINID address, *no* public key, *no* linkable identifier

   What the contract verified:
   - The blob contains Alice's real identity (ZK proof passed)
   - The identity matches her on-chain commitment

   What the spender (Bob / pool operator) can do:
   - Decrypt the blob using ~ECDH(k_bob, K_alice)~ to learn Alice's identity
   - Store it for receipt construction

*** Receiving Transfers: ~approve(sender, 0)~ Handshake

    For an account that wants to *receive* from a specific counterparty:

    #+begin_example
    // Alice wants to receive BUCK from Bob (Alice is private-identity):
    buck.approve(bob, 0, reEncryptedIdentityForBob, proof)
    #+end_example

    Amount = 0 means no spending authorization.  The side-effect: Alice's
    re-encrypted identity is now stored and verified for Bob.  When Bob
    later calls ~transfer(alice, amount)~, the BUCK contract knows Alice's
    identity fragment exists and is verified.

** Receipt Construction: What the Contract Emits

   With the corrected design, the BUCK contract emits a *minimal* event that
   reveals only pseudonymous addresses (which the ERC-20 Transfer event
   already reveals) plus a reference to the pre-stored identity fragments:

   #+begin_example
   event BuckTransferReceipt(
       address indexed from,
       address indexed to,
       uint256 amount,
       bytes32 fromIdentityHash,    // hash of from's re-encrypted identity
       bytes32 toIdentityHash       // hash of to's re-encrypted identity
   );
   #+end_example

   The ~fromIdentityHash~ and ~toIdentityHash~ are hashes of the opaque
   re-encrypted blobs stored during ~approve~.  They add *no linkability*
   beyond what the Transfer event already provides (the addresses themselves
   are the same).

   The actual re-encrypted identity blobs are retrievable:
   - From the ~IdentityExchange~ events (emitted during ~approve~)
   - From Holochain (stored on the participants' source chains)

   Only the counterparty (who has the private key to decrypt the blob)
   can reconstruct the full receipt with real identities.

** The Complete Flow

   #+begin_example
   function transfer(address to, uint256 amount) public override returns (bool) {
       require(identityRegistry.isVerified(msg.sender), "Sender not verified");
       require(identityRegistry.isVerified(to),         "Receiver not verified");
       require(compliance.canTransfer(msg.sender, to, amount), "Compliance");

       _transfer(msg.sender, to, amount);
       compliance.transferred(msg.sender, to, amount);

       // Emit minimal receipt -- no identity data, just hashes of
       // pre-stored re-encrypted blobs
       emit BuckTransferReceipt(
           msg.sender, to, amount,
           _receiptFragments[msg.sender][to],
           _receiptFragments[to][msg.sender]
       );

       return true;
   }
   #+end_example

   The ~transferFrom~ path is identical: the receipt fragment hashes were
   stored during ~approve~, so no additional data passes through the DeFi
   router.

** Summary: What Each Layer Sees

   | Layer                | What it sees                                            |
   |----------------------+---------------------------------------------------------|
   | Ethereum (everyone)  | Transfer(from, to, amount) -- pseudonymous addresses    |
   |                      | BuckTransferReceipt -- same addresses + opaque hashes   |
   |                      | IdentityExchange -- address + opaque encrypted blob     |
   |                      | ZK proof verified (pass/fail) -- no identity content    |
   | BUCK contract        | isVerified(from) == true, isVerified(to) == true        |
   |                      | ZK proof of re-encryption correctness (verified)        |
   |                      | Hash of re-encrypted identity blob (stored)             |
   |                      | *Never sees plaintext identity*                         |
   | Counterparty (Bob)   | Decrypts re-encrypted blob -> learns Alice's identity   |
   |                      | Constructs full receipt (names, details)                 |

   |                      | Constructs full receipt                                  |

   |                      | Bob reveals receipt without revealing private key        |


* Cryptographic Receipts: 3-Party ECDH

  The BUCK contract emits trusted plaintext receipt events (previous section).
  The wallet layer encrypts these into 3-party ECDH receipts that only the
  participants can decrypt.

** The Three Keys

   For a transfer from Alice to a DeFi pool operated by Bob:

   | Key               | Held by        | Purpose                                        |
   |-------------------+----------------+------------------------------------------------|
   | ~k_s~ (sender)    | Alice          | Sender's persistent signing/encryption key     |
   | ~k_e~ (ephemeral) | Deterministic  | Derived from hash of trusted receipt event;    |
   |                   |                | reproducible by anyone who knows the event     |
   | ~K_r~ (receiver)  | Bob (deployer) | Pool operator's persistent public key          |

   The ephemeral key ~k_e~ is derived deterministically from the trusted
   receipt event (which is on-chain and immutable):

   #+begin_example
   // The receipt event fields are all on-chain, all trusted:
   receipt_data = abi.encode(
       from, to, amount,
       fromIdentity, toIdentity,
       fromPubKey, toPubKey,
       block.number
   )
   k_e = keccak256(receipt_data)    // deterministic "private key"
   #+end_example

** Encryption Scheme

   The wallet computes the encryption key via ECDH:

   #+begin_example
   // Alice's wallet computes (knows k_s, k_e, K_r):
   shared_1 = ECDH(k_s, K_r)          // sender-receiver shared secret
   shared_2 = ECDH(k_e, K_r)          // ephemeral-receiver shared secret
   encryption_key = keccak256(shared_1 || shared_2)

   // Augment the trusted receipt with off-chain identity data:
   full_receipt = {
       ...receipt_data,                 // from the on-chain event (trusted)
       sender_name: "Alice Johnson",    // from ONCHAINID private claim
       receiver_name: "Bob Smith",      // from ONCHAINID claim (via Holochain)
       purpose: "Uniswap V3 BUCK->USDT swap"
   }

   // Encrypt:
   ciphertext = AES-GCM(encryption_key, encode(full_receipt))
   #+end_example

   The encrypted receipt is stored on Holochain (both parties' source chains).
   A commitment hash can optionally be emitted on-chain:

   #+begin_example
   event ReceiptCommitment(
       bytes32 indexed transferHash,    // hash of the BuckTransferReceipt event
       bytes32 receiptHash              // hash of the encrypted receipt
   );
   #+end_example

** Who Can Decrypt

*** Sender (Alice)

    Alice has ~k_s~ and can recompute ~k_e~ from the on-chain receipt event:

    #+begin_example
    k_e = keccak256(receipt_event_data)   // deterministic from on-chain data
    shared_1 = ECDH(k_s, K_r)            // she has k_s, K_r from event
    shared_2 = ECDH(k_e, K_r)            // she has k_e, K_r from event
    encryption_key = keccak256(shared_1 || shared_2)
    #+end_example

*** Receiver / Pool Operator (Bob)

    Bob has ~k_r~ (private key) and reads the sender's public key and the
    ephemeral public key from the on-chain event:

    #+begin_example
    K_e = G * k_e                         // computable from event data
    shared_1 = ECDH(k_r, K_s)            // K_s = fromPubKey from event
    shared_2 = ECDH(k_r, K_e)            // K_e from event data
    encryption_key = keccak256(shared_1 || shared_2)
    #+end_example

*** Court-Compelled Decryption (Subpoena of Pool Operator)

    A court subpoenas Bob to decrypt a specific transaction receipt:

    #+begin_example
    // Court provides: the transaction hash
    // Bob looks up: the BuckTransferReceipt event from that transaction
    // Bob computes (using his private key k_r):
    shared_1 = ECDH(k_r, K_s)            // K_s from event
    shared_2 = ECDH(k_r, K_e)            // K_e from event
    encryption_key = keccak256(shared_1 || shared_2)
    receipt = AES-GCM-decrypt(encryption_key, ciphertext_from_holochain)
    #+end_example

    Bob reveals only the decrypted receipt.  He never reveals ~k_r~.  He
    cannot decrypt receipts where he is not a party.

*** Regulatory Escrow (Optional)

    A regulatory authority's public key ~K_reg~ adds a third shared secret:

    #+begin_example
    shared_3 = ECDH(k_e, K_reg)
    encryption_key = keccak256(shared_1 || shared_2 || shared_3)
    #+end_example

    The regulator can compute ~shared_3~ (they have ~k_reg~, and ~K_e~ is
    deterministic from the on-chain event).  But they still need ~shared_1~
    or ~shared_2~ -- requiring cooperation from a transaction participant.
    No unilateral surveillance.


* Play-by-Play: BUCK/USDT Swap on Uniswap

  Alice holds 1000 BUCK and wants to swap 500 BUCK for USDT.  Bob has deployed
  a BUCK/USDT Uniswap V3 pool.

** Prerequisites (One-Time Setup)

   #+begin_example
   Alice:
     1. Completed KYC with trusted issuer (off-chain)
     2. Trusted issuer signed claim (topic 1: KYC) on Alice's ONCHAINID
     3. Registry agent called identityRegistry.registerIdentity(
            alice_wallet, alice_onchainid, country_code)
     4. identityRegistry.isVerified(alice_wallet) == true

   Bob (pool deployer):
     1. Completed KYC (off-chain)
     2. Has KYC claim on his ONCHAINID
     3. Is registered in IdentityRegistry
     4. Created BUCK/USDT pool via Uniswap factory
        -> pool deployed at address 0xPOOL
     5. Registered pool in IdentityRegistry:
        identityRegistry.registerIdentity(
            0xPOOL, bob_onchainid, country_code)
     6. identityRegistry.isVerified(0xPOOL) == true
   #+end_example

** The Swap Transaction

*** Step 1: Alice Approves the Router (Identity Handshake)

    Alice's wallet invokes the Holochain hApp to prepare the identity
    exchange, then calls ~approve~ on the BUCK contract:

    #+begin_example
    // 1. Off-chain (Holochain hApp on Alice's node):
    //    - Decrypt Alice's identity I from her source chain
    //    - Look up the pool operator's (Bob's) public key K_bob
    //    - Re-encrypt: E_bob = Encrypt(I, ECDH(k_alice, K_bob))
    //    - Generate ZK proof: "E_bob encrypts the same I committed on-chain"

    // 2. On-chain:
    buck.approve(
        uniswapRouter,      // spender (the router, which calls transferFrom)
        500e18,              // amount
        E_bob,               // Alice's identity re-encrypted for Bob
        proof                // ZK proof of correct re-encryption
    )
    #+end_example

    The BUCK contract:

    #+begin_example
    1. Sets allowance[alice][uniswapRouter] = 500e18
    2. Reads Alice's identity commitment from IdentityRegistry
    3. Verifies the ZK proof: E_bob correctly re-encrypts the committed I
    4. Stores hash(E_bob) in _receiptFragments[alice][0xPOOL]
    5. Emits IdentityExchange(alice, 0xPOOL, E_bob)
       -- the event contains ONLY the opaque encrypted blob
       -- no ONCHAINID address, no public key, no linkable identifier
    #+end_example

    *This is the critical moment.*  Alice is directly calling the BUCK
    contract, so she can provide the re-encrypted identity and ZK proof.
    By the time ~transferFrom~ is called later (by the router), the
    verified identity fragment is already stored.

*** Step 2: Alice Calls the Router

    #+begin_example
    // Alice initiates the swap (standard Uniswap call -- no BUCK-specific data)
    uniswapRouter.exactInputSingle(
        tokenIn:  BUCK,
        tokenOut: USDT,
        fee:      3000,        // 0.3% fee tier
        recipient: alice,
        amountIn: 500e18,
        amountOutMinimum: 490e6,  // slippage protection
        sqrtPriceLimitX96: 0
    )
    #+end_example

    No identity data passes through this call.  The router is a standard
    Uniswap contract that knows nothing about BUCK identity.

*** Step 3: Router Calls BUCK.transferFrom (Minimal Receipt Emission)

    Inside the router, the swap triggers:

    #+begin_example
    buck.transferFrom(alice, 0xPOOL, 500e18)
    #+end_example

    The BUCK contract executes (no identity data passes through the router):

    #+begin_example
    function transferFrom(address from, address to, uint256 amount) ...
    {
        _spendAllowance(from, msg.sender, amount);
        require(identityRegistry.isVerified(from), "Sender not verified");
        require(identityRegistry.isVerified(to),   "Receiver not verified");
        require(compliance.canTransfer(from, to, amount), "Compliance");

        _transfer(from, to, amount);
        compliance.transferred(from, to, amount);

        // Emit minimal receipt -- ONLY hashes of pre-stored opaque blobs
        emit BuckTransferReceipt(
            from, to, amount,
            _receiptFragments[from][to],     // hash of Alice's E_bob
            _receiptFragments[to][from]       // hash of Bob's E_alice (if exists)
        );

        return true;
    }
    #+end_example

    *What the public sees*: the same from/to/amount as the standard ERC-20
    Transfer event, plus two opaque hashes.  No ONCHAINID addresses.  No
    public keys.  No linkable identity information beyond the pseudonymous
    Ethereum addresses.

    *What the contract verified* (during ~approve~, earlier): the ZK proof
    that Alice's re-encrypted identity blob is a correct re-encryption of
    her committed identity.

*** Step 4: Wallet Constructs the Full Receipt (Off-Chain)

    Alice's wallet (Holochain hApp) observes the ~BuckTransferReceipt~
    event and the ~IdentityExchange~ events to construct the full receipt:

    #+begin_example
    // 1. From on-chain events (trusted, immutable):
    transfer_data = {from, to, amount, block.number, tx_hash}

    // 2. From IdentityExchange event (emitted during approve):
    E_bob = Alice's re-encrypted identity for Bob (opaque blob)

    // 3. From Holochain (Alice's source chain):
    alice_identity = decrypt(alice_encrypted_identity, k_alice)

    // 4. From Bob's IdentityExchange (if Bob also did approve for Alice):
    E_alice = Bob's re-encrypted identity for Alice
    bob_identity = decrypt(E_alice, k_alice)  // Alice can decrypt this

    // 5. Construct full receipt
    full_receipt = {
        transfer:      transfer_data,
        sender_name:   alice_identity.name,      // "Alice Johnson"
        receiver_name: bob_identity.name,         // "Bob Smith, DeFi Ops Inc."
        operation:     "Uniswap V3 swap BUCK -> USDT",
        pool:          "0xPOOL",
        timestamp:     block.timestamp
    }

    // 6. Encrypt the full receipt with 3-party ECDH (see next section)
    k_e = keccak256(abi.encode(transfer_data))
    shared_1 = ECDH(k_alice, K_bob)
    shared_2 = ECDH(k_e, K_bob)
    encryption_key = keccak256(shared_1 || shared_2)
    ciphertext = AES-GCM(encryption_key, encode(full_receipt))

    // 7. Store on Holochain (both parties' source chains)
    holochain.store(alice_chain, ciphertext)
    holochain.store(bob_chain,   ciphertext)
    #+end_example

*** Step 5: Pool Executes the Swap

    The Uniswap pool sends USDT to Alice.  Since USDT is not an Alberta Buck
    token, the USDT transfer has no BUCK identity requirements (it follows
    whatever rules USDT imposes, which is typically none).

*** Step 6: Completed State

    #+begin_example
    On-chain (public):
      - ERC-20 Transfer event: alice_addr -> 0xPOOL, 500 BUCK
      - BuckTransferReceipt: same addresses + two opaque bytes32 hashes
      - IdentityExchange (from approve): alice_addr + opaque encrypted blob
      - Swap event: BUCK/USDT pool, 500 BUCK in, ~495 USDT out
      - ZK proof verification result: pass (no identity content leaked)

    Off-chain (Holochain, encrypted):
      - Full encrypted receipt on Alice's and Bob's source chains
      - Alice's re-encrypted identity blob (decryptable only by Bob)
      - Bob's re-encrypted identity blob (decryptable only by Alice)

    What anyone can see:
      - Two pseudonymous addresses transacted 500 BUCK
      - Both addresses pass isVerified (they are KYC'd participants)
      - Opaque blobs and hashes that reveal nothing without private keys
      - A ZK proof was verified (the identity blobs are genuine)

    What Alice can see:
      - Her own identity (trivially)
      - Bob's identity (decrypt his re-encrypted blob with her key)
      - Full receipt with names, amounts, purpose

    What Bob can see:
      - His own identity (trivially)
      - Alice's identity (decrypt her re-encrypted blob with his key)
      - Full receipt with names, amounts, purpose

    What a court can compel (via subpoena of Bob):
      - Bob decrypts Alice's blob for the specific transaction
      - Reveals Alice's identity for that one receipt
      - Bob never reveals his private key

    What the public CANNOT see:
      - Alice's real name or ONCHAINID
      - Bob's real name (as pool operator)
      - Any linkable identity across transactions
      - The purpose or terms of the transaction
    #+end_example


* Edge Cases and Design Considerations

** Flash Loans and Atomic Transactions

   A flash loan borrows and repays in a single transaction.  The borrower's
   address must be verified, and the lending contract must be registered.
   The receipt covers the full borrow-repay cycle as a single atomic event.

** Multi-Hop Swaps

   A swap routed through BUCK -> WETH -> USDT involves two BUCK transfers
   (BUCK -> pool1, pool1 -> pool2 if intermediate).  Each BUCK transfer
   generates its own encrypted receipt.  The router contract must also be
   registered if it holds BUCK transiently.

** Contract-to-Contract Transfers

   When one DeFi contract sends BUCK to another (e.g., a yield aggregator
   moving BUCK between pools), both contracts must be registered.  The
   receipt identifies both contracts' deployers as the parties.  This creates
   a clear chain of custody: every BUCK movement has an identified legal
   operator at each end.

** Wrapping and Bridging

   If BUCK is wrapped (WBUCK) for use on another chain, the wrapping
   contract must be registered.  The wrap/unwrap generates receipts.
   On the destination chain, the bridged BUCK may operate under different
   identity rules (or no identity rules if the destination chain doesn't
   enforce ERC-3643).  This is a known limitation -- the Alberta Buck's
   identity guarantees only hold on chains where the IdentityRegistry is
   deployed.

** Gas Costs

   The corrected design has two gas-consuming operations:

   | Operation                         | Cost (~gas)     | When          |
   |-----------------------------------+-----------------+---------------|
   | ~approve~ with ZK proof verify    | ~200K-300K gas  | Once per pair |
   | ~BuckTransferReceipt~ event       | ~2,000 gas      | Per transfer  |
   | (2 indexed addresses + 3 uint256) |                 |               |

   The ZK proof verification (~approve~) is expensive but happens once per
   counterparty pair.  Subsequent transfers between the same pair emit only
   the minimal receipt event (addresses + hashes), which is cheap.

   The encrypted receipt itself is stored on Holochain, not on-chain.
   No encrypted blobs are emitted as Ethereum events.

** Privacy of Pool Deployer Identity

   The pool deployer's identity is linked to the pool contract in the
   IdentityRegistry, but this mapping is on-chain and public.  Anyone can
   look up ~identityRegistry.identity(0xPOOL)~ and find Bob's ONCHAINID.

   If Bob's ONCHAINID has only private claims (KYC but not public identity),
   then the public knows "0xPOOL is operated by a verified entity" but not
   WHO that entity is.  Bob's identity is revealed only in encrypted receipts.

   If Bob has a public identity claim (e.g., "DeFi Ops Inc., Alberta"),
   then anyone can see who operates the pool.  This is likely the expected
   case for commercial DeFi operators -- public identity builds trust and
   attracts liquidity.


* Summary: Identity Requirement by Risk Level

  | Risk level | Operations                          | Identity mode   | Receipt           |
  |------------+-------------------------------------+-----------------+-------------------|
  | Highest    | BuckCredit creation/update,         | Public          | On-chain event +  |
  |            | oracle claims, governance           |                 | public record     |
  | High       | Pool deployment, compliance changes | Public          | On-chain event    |
  | Medium     | BUCK transfer, DeFi swap,           | Anonymous-valid | Encrypted 3-party |
  |            | add/remove liquidity                |                 | ECDH receipt      |
  | Low        | BUCK burn, credit activation        | Anonymous-valid | On-chain event    |
  | None       | BuckK compute, read oracle,         | Permissionless  | On-chain event    |
  |            | view balances                       |                 |                   |
