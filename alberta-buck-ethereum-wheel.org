#+begin_abstract
[[file:alberta-buck-ethereum-basket.org][The BuckBasket]] says the basket earns from tolls, from
correcting its own prices and from leaning against slow swings, and that a desk beside it steadies
the BUCK.  This paper is the machinery and the arithmetic behind those claims.

The basket is an ordinary BUCK credit holder with one unusual rule: its limit counts only the BUCKs
it has /let out/.  BUCKs it issues and holds beside its own commodity in a pool are not at risk
against the lien they came from, so they use none of the limit.  Every deposit is therefore paired
the moment it arrives, and every exit is paid in BUCKs before anything is sold.

That leaves the upkeep free to earn.  A pool of liquidity is a rebalancer that trades at stale
prices, and whoever re-pins it keeps the rebalancing premium.  The basket takes that back with
/consistency cycles/: three swaps round a commodity's three prices, needing no capital and unable to
lose.  Each commodity's share has a /target that leans against its price/, and a small policy
decides when a gap may be worked: only one that has lasted, and only once the price has stopped
running.  A /desk/ with books of its own acts on excursions of the BUCK beyond a band.  One /work
wheel/, which anyone may advance, turns all of it.

*This is a prototype.*  The contracts exist and run in a simulated economy and in a public sandbox.
None of it is deployed or audited, and the numbers below are a simulation's or a model's.
([[https://perry.kundert.ca/images/alberta-buck-ethereum-wheel.pdf][PDF]], [[https://perry.kundert.ca/images/alberta-buck-ethereum-wheel.txt][Text]])

* The machine
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: machine
:END:

The basket is shares of equity, one lien, and a set of pools.

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
    gross(mark)   =  sum over pools ( 2 L_i sqrt(P_i)  +  wallet TOKEN_i x P_i )
    equity(mark)  =  gross(mark)  +  signedBalance(basket)  +  reliefAccrued(basket)
    share price   =  equity / shares
#+END_EXAMPLE

=2 L sqrt(P)= is a full-range position's fair value at a price.  It is computed from the pool's
liquidity and a price, never from token balances, so tokens sent to a pool cannot inflate it.
There are three marks: the TWAP, HIGH (each pool at the higher of spot and TWAP) and LOW (the
lower).  A deposit is valued at LOW against a basket at HIGH; an exit at LOW.  Each side also pays
a charge of about half a pool fee, which stays with the holders: =(1 - K)/2= of the pool fee for
TOKEN in, =(1 + K)/2= of the dearest pool's fee for BUCKs in or out.  Nobody can enter or leave at
a price that moves value between the holders.

** The credit, and what counts against it
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: net-mark
:END:

The basket is an ordinary BUCK account holding one self-issued /marked/ credit.  It marks the
credit itself, before anything that spends, and Buck enforces the limit: the basket can have drawn
at most =K= times the mark.  It never mints or burns.  It spends past its held BUCKs, which draws on
the credit, and BUCKs it receives repay the lien.

The plain choice is to mark the credit at the depositors' equity.  Then every BUCK drawn counts,
including one that was drawn and set down in a pool beside the basket's own TOKEN.  Credit can
then pair only =K= of the book.  The rest of every deposit must be sold to make its own BUCK side,
or left waiting.

But a BUCK the basket issued and still holds carries no price risk against the lien it came from.
If the BUCK doubles in price, the lien is twice as dear and so are the BUCKs held.  So the basket
marks /net/: at its equity plus the BUCK side of its positions over =K=, which makes Buck's own
limit read

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
    lien  -  BUCKs held in the positions  <=   K x equity
#+END_EXAMPLE

=K= bounds the BUCKs the basket has let out, not what it still holds.  Three things follow.

- *Pairing uses none of the line.*  Every TOKEN is paired with issued BUCKs as it arrives.  Nothing
  is swapped to place a deposit.
- *An exit is paid before anything is sold.*  It is paid in BUCKs from the line; one exit of up to
  =K / (1 + K)= of the book fits.  The basket sells down afterwards, over the following turns.
- *There is no loop.*  The most TOKEN the basket can come to hold is =(1 + K) x equity=, as under
  the plain mark.  Counting gross assets as collateral, as lending protocols that loop do, would
  allow =1 / (1 - K)= and is not done.

In normal running the upkeep uses at most half of =K x equity=.  The other half is what exits
are paid from.

*No margin calls.*  A cut in =K= changes no lien; beyond its limit the account simply cannot spend.
Buck never calls anything back.

*Exits.*  The treasury takes 25% of a receipt's gain over its cost basis, as shares, and nothing of
a loss.  The rest is paid in BUCKs when Buck lets the basket spend it.  Otherwise it is paid in
kind: the exit's fraction =f= of every position and of the wallet's TOKEN, by transfer, its share
of the lien repaid from the BUCKs its positions return.  No swap is forced on anyone.

*Relief and demurrage.*  The lien earns Jubilee relief like any other, counted in equity as it
accrues.  The demurrage on BUCKs held in a pool rides with them and is borne when they come back, so
a young basket's share price shows the relief first.

* Where a pool's harvest goes
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: leak
:END:

A constant-mix portfolio earns more than the weighted average of its parts: stochastic portfolio
theory calls the difference the /excess growth rate/ (Fernholz, 2002),

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
    gamma*  =  1/2 ( sum_i w_i sigma_i^2  -  sigma_p^2 )
#+END_EXAMPLE

-- half the gap between the parts' average variance and the portfolio's own.  It is the rebalancing
premium, and for a pool half TOKEN and half BUCKs it is =sigma^2 / 8= of the position per unit time.

*A full-range position is a constant-mix rebalancer that trades at stale prices.*  It sells TOKEN
as the price rises and buys as it falls, but at its own price, which trails the market.  Whoever
moves it to the market price keeps the difference: the /loss-versus-rebalancing/ of Milionis,
Moallemi, Roughgarden and Zhang (2022),

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
    LVR  ~  V x sigma^2 / 8     per unit time, before fees
#+END_EXAMPLE

-- the same number.  So the harvest goes to the arbitrageur, and the pool keeps the fees on the
arbitrageur's volume.  For one pool reverting with a 20-day half-life at 1.5% a day, the premium is
1.0% a year of the position:

| outside arbitrage | pool fee | the pool keeps | the basket's own arbitrage keeps |
|-------------------+----------+----------------+----------------------------------|
| once a day        | 0.3%     | 0.22%          | 0.90%                            |
| once a day        | 1%       | 0.49%          | 0.90%                            |
| every hour        | 0.3%     | 0.52%          | 0.75%                            |
| every hour        | 1%       | 0.79%          | 0.83%                            |

The finer the trading against the fee, the more the fee recovers by itself; the coarser, the more
only the basket's own arbitrage can.

*A pool cannot harvest slow swings at all.*  A constant-mix rebalancer earns the variance of
short-horizon moves -- the quadratic variation.  A smooth cycle has almost none, however large its
amplitude.  A swing is harvested only by trades timed across pools: selling one constituent
near its top, buying another near its bottom.

So a basket's excess return over holding its commodities has three sources, each with its own
harvester:

| mispricing                           | harvester             | its risk                     |
|--------------------------------------+-----------------------+------------------------------|
| a stale TOKEN/BUCK pool              | the consistency cycle | none, per cycle              |
| a commodity dear against the others  | the lean              | a price that never returns   |
| the BUCK off its basket              | the desk              | persistent inventory         |

Beneath all three are the tolls: the fees on the basket's own pools, which the Sync step collects.

* The consistency cycle
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: cycle
:END:

Three pools quote each constituent:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
                 TOKEN_i
                /       \
      TOKEN/USDC         TOKEN/BUCK    (the basket's own pool)
              /           \
          USDC ----------- BUCK
                BUCK/USDC
#+END_EXAMPLE

If the three prices agree, going round the triangle returns what went in, less the fees.  If they
disagree, one direction returns more.  Going round that way, by the right amount, closes the gap
and keeps the difference.

** The algebra
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: algebra
:END:

A constant-product leg with reserves /(X, Y)/ and fee factor /g/ (one minus the fee) pays, for an
input /x/,

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
    out(x)  =  g Y x / (X + g x)  =  r x / (1 + k x),     r = g Y / X,   k = g / X
#+END_EXAMPLE

a Moebius map through the origin: /r/ is the leg's marginal rate at zero size, /k/ its curvature
(the inverse of its depth).  Such maps compose into maps of the same form, so a whole cycle is two
numbers:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
    r  =  r1 r2 r3
    k  =  k1 + k2 r1 + k3 r1 r2
    cycle(x)  =  r x / (1 + k x)

    x*  =  (sqrt(r) - 1) / k          profit*  =  (sqrt(r) - 1)^2 / k
#+END_EXAMPLE

The cycle pays exactly when /r/ > 1, and the optimum closes the gap down to the fees, no further.  A
full-range V3 position is constant product on its virtual reserves, so the closed form is exact for
the basket's own pools and a good approximation elsewhere.  =CycleMath= in =src/wheel/ArbKind.sol=
is this section in Solidity.

** Why it cannot hurt
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: safe
:END:

- *No capital.*  A V3 pool pays a swap's output before it collects the input, in a callback.  The
  first leg pays out; the other two run inside its callback; what they return pays the first.  No
  inventory, no flash loan, no flash mint.  USDC exists only between two swaps inside one
  transaction.
- *No change in supply.*  No BUCKs are issued or destroyed; value moves from the venues to the
  basket.  A cycle never trades BUCKs against the basket's value -- that is the desk's business.
- *It cannot lose.*  The cycle checks its realized output against its input and reverts below a
  minimum edge.  Someone who pushes a pool first only pays the basket to push it back.
- *It keeps the gauge true.*  The controller steers on the basket's value in BUCKs, read from these
  pools.  Closing their gaps makes that reading follow the commodities instead of lagging them by
  an arbitrageur's threshold.

The same three swaps, in the same rotation, earn the same whichever token the cycle starts from;
the start decides only what the profit is made of.  Started from the TOKEN, the profit lands in the
basket's wallet, and every share is worth more.  Started from BUCKs, it funds the wheel's callers'
reserve (below) up to a cap, and the rest goes to the basket's account.

** Who can take which gap
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: band
:END:

A searcher going round the triangle pays three pool fees.  It is tempting to think the basket pays
only two -- it is nearly the only liquidity in its own pool, so the fee it pays there comes back --
and so can take a band of small gaps nobody else can.  It cannot.  On its own share of a pool the
basket is trading with itself: at the outside price, a "capture" inside the outsiders' band gains
only the outside liquidity's share of the gap, and pays the outside cost on all of it.  That is a
rebalancing bet, not an arbitrage, and measured it gains nothing (within 0.02-0.16% a year, either
sign).

What the basket /can/ do is take the outsider's trade first.  The gaps worth taking are the ones
wider than all three fees, and on today's Uniswap V3 the basket must race searchers for them.
Winning that race by construction needs control of the order of swaps: a Uniswap v4 hook that runs
the cycle before the first swap of each block, or an auction for the right to go first (the
auction-managed AMM of Adams, Moallemi, Reynolds and Robinson, 2024).  That is the upgrade path.

* What the basket watches
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: signals
:END:

A Uniswap tick is a log price, so the basket's pools hand it log prices for free.  Write =c_i= for
constituent /i/'s log price in BUCKs, and split the vector the way a three-phase machine splits its
line voltages -- into a common mode, which carries no torque, and differential modes, which carry
all of it:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
    differential:  c_i - mean(c)        which commodity is dear against which
    common:        log(basket in BUCKs) what the whole basket costs, against par
#+END_EXAMPLE

The differential mode is numeraire-free: anything common to every constituent, including the BUCK's
own wobble, cancels exactly.  It is the rebalancing signal.  The common mode is the K controller's
process variable, and the desk's signal.  Each has its own observer, and neither trades.

*Relative price* (=PairSignals=).  For every TOKEN on every quote route (its BUCK pool and its
USDC pool) the observer reads the pool's cumulative tick.  The difference from the last reading,
over the time between, is the interval's mean log price; within a route the mean across TOKENs is
removed.  An alpha-beta filter then keeps a /level/ and a /rate/ for each, its gains set by one
response time (12 hours in the lab) and the time actually elapsed.  Beside them it keeps a running
measure of its own error, from which it reports an /allowance/ on the rate: how far the rate could
be from what it says.  For a pair of TOKENs it reports the level and rate of one against the
other.  A route is ready after three samples and two days, and falls out when a pool cannot be
read.

*Parity* (=BasketReference=).  The reference basket's cost in BUCKs from each pool's TWAP, refused
when a pool has no liquidity or its spot is more than 5% from its TWAP.  It publishes the last valid
value, whether it is fresh, and how many days running it has had a valid sample.  Above one, a
basket costs more BUCKs: the BUCK is cheap.  Below one, the BUCK is dear.  It is read raw.  No
average is taken of it, for a reason the desk taught us ([[#lag][below]]).

* The lean: targets that move against price
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: director
:END:

*Why there are tides.*  New money does not reach all prices at once (Cantillon, 1755).  Correlating
each series' year-over-year growth with the M2 money supply, lags swept from 0 to 36 months:

| series            | peak lag (months) | peak correlation |
|-------------------+-------------------+------------------|
| bitcoin           | 0                 | 0.08             |
| gold              | censored at 36    | 0.10             |
| construction      | 9-10              | 0.48-0.52        |
| energy            | 13                | 0.33-0.39        |
| labour            | 15-16             | 0.32-0.38        |
| food              | 23                | 0.21-0.26        |

Construction and energy move within a year, labour and food a year after.  (Bitcoin is shown for
comparison; it is not admitted to the basket.)  When M2 surges the fast constituents overshoot
their share and stay overweight until the slow ones catch up -- roughly a year.  Those excursions
are the harvest a pool cannot reach.

*A pool already does half the job.*  A liquidity position sells a TOKEN as its price rises and buys
as it falls.  So a constituent's share of the book moves half as far as it would in a wallet, and
a basket that only restored its declared weights would find little left to do after a large move.
Its pools would already have sold, at every price on the way.

*So the target leans.*  The shell holds a /tilt/ and, for each constituent, a /reference price/:
the price it is taken to return to.

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
    target_i  =  declared weight_i  x  (reference price_i / price_i) ^ tilt
                 shared out over the book, and held within the lean cap of the declared weight
#+END_EXAMPLE

Only one price against another matters.  A move in the BUCK itself, or in every TOKEN together,
leans nothing.  As a share's response to its own price:

| How the TOKEN is held                     | Share moves with price to the power |
|-------------------------------------------+-------------------------------------|
| In a wallet, untouched                    | +1                                  |
| In the pools, untouched                   | +1/2                                |
| Restored to its declared weight (tilt 0)  | 0                                   |
| Tilt 1/2                                  | -1/2                                |
| Tilt 1                                    | -1                                  |

With a tilt the basket sells a TOKEN that stands above its reference to below its declared weight,
and buys it back as the price returns.  That is a bet on the price returning, and it is the bet
the basket exists to make.  The reference price is the price the constituent joined at unless
governance sets another.  The tilt is 1/2 in the lab, and no target leans by more than 15% of its
declared weight.

*The book.*  Those shares are shares of a /book/, and the book leans too, against the BUCK's own
price and on one side only:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
    book  =  equity + aim
    aim   =  - gain x (the basket's cost in BUCKs - 1) x equity     while the BUCK is cheap
    aim   =  0                                                      while it is dear
#+END_EXAMPLE

While the BUCK is cheap the book is smaller than equity: TOKEN is sold and BUCKs held.  The basket
has then taken its BUCKs while they were cheap, before the fall in =K= that a cheap BUCK brings.
While the BUCK is dear the book is the size of its equity: the basket lets no BUCKs out because of
that.  The two sides are not alike.  A basket holding cheap BUCKs can lose what it paid.  A basket
short of a dear BUCK loses without bound while the BUCK goes on rising.

* When to trade
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: policy
:END:

A target says where a constituent should stand.  It does not say when to go there.  A rebalancer
that follows its target at once sells a riser all the way up, paying fees and bleeding against
the trend.  Measured on a portfolio of held tokens over twenty synthetic years, policies that
waited for an excursion to level off collected 75-90% of continuous rebalancing's premium on
30-55% of its turnover.  So a small policy (=PairRebalancePolicy=) stands between the targets and
the trades.  It owns no assets and authorizes no spending.  It answers two questions.

** Which gaps may be worked: urgency
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: urgency
:END:

A fixed band is the wrong shape.  A share 1.9 points off for a month never qualifies, and one
that touches 2.1 for an hour does.  So each constituent keeps a count:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
    urgency  <-  urgency + (|gap| - slack) x time elapsed      kept between zero and patience
    urgency  <-  zero       when the gap changes side
    the gap may be worked while urgency = patience
#+END_EXAMPLE

=gap= is the constituent's distance from its target, as a share of the book.  With a slack of a
quarter point and a patience of two point-days, a gap of 2.25 points qualifies in a day, 1.25 in
two, and a quarter point never does.  A trade does not restart the count: a gap that has
qualified stays open while it is worked off, and closes once it is back inside the slack.

This is a CUSUM, the cumulative-sum test for a small shift that persists.  It decides only
/whether/.

** How fast: desire
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: desire
:END:

For an ordered pair (sell /a/, buy /b/) the policy returns a /rate/: the fraction of what remains
of the gap to close per day.  A rate of ln 2 halves the gap each day.

1. /The evidence must be current./  Every route ready and freshly sampled.  Otherwise zero.
2. /The pair must have parted./  The log price of /a/ against /b/ must stand more than 1% above
   its accepted reference, on the filtered level and on every live pool.
3. /Let a run run./  While /a/ is still gaining on /b/ the rate is held back.  It is released as
   the pair's rate /falls/: the fall over one response time, measured against the filter's
   allowance, is the brake coming off.
4. /A pair that is closing is worked./  When even the upper allowance on the rate is below zero the
   pair is coming back, and the rate rises with the policy's confidence in that.
5. /A ceiling./  The sum is capped: ln 2 a day in the lab.

The basket's own recent trades move the pools it reads.  It reports the tick change it caused, and
the policy widens its allowance by that much, fading over two days, so the basket does not
mistake its own footprint for the market turning.

** How much now: release
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: release
:END:

A rate becomes an amount through an /allowance/: the rate a constituent has been worked at, summed
over the time since it last traded.

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
    allowance  <-  allowance + rate x time elapsed       an unobserved stretch earns a day at most
    allowance  <-  zero                                  when it trades, or is no longer worked
    share of the gap to move now  =  1 - exp(-allowance)
#+END_EXAMPLE

This composes exactly.  A day at a rate of ln 2 moves half the gap whether the wheel turns once
that day or in every block, and a turn too small to trade loses nothing: its allowance carries to
the next.  The pace does not depend on who calls, or how often.

* The upkeep
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: upkeep
:END:

The wheel turns three kinds of step on the basket (=BasketUpkeepFacet=).

- *Daily.*  Once a day: settle the relief against the lien and mark the credit.
- *Sync(i).*  Once a day for each pool: collect the position's fees and mark.
- *Respond.*  At most once an interval (30 minutes in the lab): observe, then the three things
  below, in order.

*Pair what waits.*  Every constituent's wallet TOKEN is paired with issued BUCKs, at once and in
full.  This swaps nothing.  The BUCKs stay in the position, so each marking gives the room back.

*Size the book and find each gap.*  The book is equity plus the aim.  Each constituent's target is
its leaned share of that book, and its gap is the TOKEN it holds less that target.  The gaps are
reported to the policy.

*Work each constituent on its own.*  One over its target is sold, and one under it is bought.
Neither needs the other: the credit line carries the difference.  The rate is the policy's
desire for this constituent against each of the others, weighed by their declared weights.  The
amount is the gap times its release, capped at 5% of the holding a turn and 1% of the pool's depth
a swap.  A sale takes wallet TOKEN first, then burns liquidity: a position gives up TOKEN and BUCKs
in equal value, so twice the remainder is burned and its TOKEN sold.  A purchase buys TOKEN with
half its budget and pairs it with the other half.  The credit is marked after the sales and after
each purchase.

Two rules act with no view on prices and ask the policy nothing.

- *The leash.*  A constituent more than 30% of its declared weight from its declared share is
  worked toward its target at once.  The lean times the mandate; the leash enforces it.
- *The ceiling.*  BUCKs let out beyond half of =K x equity= are bought back at once, every
  constituent giving its share.  A large exit is paid first and sold down over the next turns.

The venue (=BasketVenueFacet=) is Uniswap V3 with nothing optional.  A missing TWAP is an
unavailable price.  A swap must execute within a tolerance of the TWAP and leave the spot within
it, or the step reverts.

* The desk: monetary operations
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: desk
:END:

=BUCK_K= is sound and slow.  It reaches the economy only through credit limits, and a credit book
turns over in months; someone with real money can push the BUCK off parity in days.  The division of
work is the one a central bank makes between its policy rate and its open-market desk.

The basket and the desk are separate concerns.  The basket earns on its constituents returning to
one another.  The desk puts its own capital to work on excursions of the BUCK itself, which =K= will
in time reverse, so it is paid when the BUCK returns to its basket.

** What it does
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: quadrants
:END:

Two questions decide an operation: is the BUCK cheap or dear, and has the excursion lasted?

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
                   BUCK CHEAP                    BUCK DEAR
                   (basket costs more BUCKs)     (basket costs less BUCKs)

  ON ANY ADVICE    buy BUCKs with TOKEN, hold    sell BUCKs it holds
                   supply unchanged              supply unchanged

  AFTER 30 DAYS    buy back what it issued       draw BUCKs on its credit
  ON ONE SIDE      supply falls                  and sell them; supply rises
#+END_EXAMPLE

- *A band.*  The desk takes a side when the BUCK is more than 3% from its basket, and leaves it when
  the BUCK is back within 2%.  Inside the band it does nothing.  That ground is left to holders who
  profit by closing it, to the basket's own lean, and to =K=.
- *Persistence.*  It counts the days running that the BUCK has been outside the band on one side.  A
  missed day, a reversal or a return clears the count.  With what it holds the desk trades on any
  advice.  It draws credit, which changes supply, only after thirty days.
- *Effort.*  A quarter of the error beyond the release band, capped at 40 basis points of its size
  an operation, and only while TWAP and spot agree on the side.
- *A guard.*  After the trade the spot must still be outside the release band on the same side, or
  the operation reverts.  The desk cannot spend through to parity; that is =K='s to do.
- *Limits.*  A lifetime turnover budget that nothing refills, a floor on its net value, caps on
  its position and on the credit it has drawn, and Buck's own limit on its lien.

** Why it is built this way
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: lag
:END:

Each rule above is a lesson from an earlier, freer desk (=alberta_buck/sim/monetary_ops.py= is
its model).

*Measure fast enough, or become the destabilizer.*  The desk trades on a value read from the pools
it trades in, so the obvious guard is a slow average that one operation cannot move.  It is
exactly wrong.  Measuring on a 160-day average attenuated a 1645 bp excursion to 712 bp; that
attenuation is phase lag.  Operations arrived after the spot had turned, landed pro-cyclically,
and grew the peak to 2116 bp -- while making money.

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Profitability and stabilization are not the same objective.  "The corrective trade is the
profitable trade" holds only when the signal is timely; with lag they come apart, and a lagged
operator is a profitable destabilizer.
#+END_QUOTE

So this desk reads parity raw, and its guard is on the spot after the trade.

*An operator can silence its own alarm.*  Tested against a /genuine/ revaluation -- commodities
really are worth 8% more BUCKs, and stay there -- the earlier desk bought until it held half the
pool it traded in, showing a profit the whole way on a position it could not have unwound.
Bounding its inventory moved the runaway: it then burned most of the supply chasing a gap 8% would
have closed.  A bounded operator needs separate bounds on the /flow/, on the /position/ and on
its /cumulative/ work.  This desk has all three, and a persistence count taken from the market's
price, not from its own book.

*A desk defends only with reserves it already holds* -- the lesson of the 1992 ERM crisis.  So it
is founded with a grant of TOKEN, its own collateral.

*K reads the price alone.*  Every design that fed a desk's position into =K= pinned =K= at its
ceiling somewhere.  Nothing the desk holds or has drawn reaches the controller.

** A separate account
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: separate
:END:

The desk is =BasketDesk=: a separate contract with its own account at Buck, its own marked credit
(marked at its own net value: its TOKEN at the low marks, plus its account and the relief accrued
on it), and its own founding grant.  From the basket it reads two things: its equity, which sizes
the desk's bounds, and its constituents, so the desk trades in the same pools through the same
venue.

- D1: the desk spends only after a mark at its own net value, so Buck holds its lien within =K x=
  that value.
- D2: nothing the desk holds or owes enters the basket's books, mark or limit.
- D3: the relief on the desk's lien is the desk's, and melts its outstanding issuance.

The rule behind it: Buck keys everything by address -- one balance, one lien, one limit per account
-- so bookkeeping sub-accounts inside one contract are invisible to it.  The desk was first built
as one, and its founding grant quietly raised the depositors' limit.  A fuzzing suite
(=test/basket/BasketDeskInvariant.t.sol=) now checks the wall after every call.

* The work wheel
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: wheel
:END:

Someone has to notice the work and send the transaction.  The basket does not wait for a keeper
paid by the hour: its work runs on a /work wheel/ (=BasketMachineWheel=), a bounded state machine
any caller advances with =tick(maxWork, maxScan)=, which does each block's work once however many
callers there are.

- *Slots.*  A cursor walks the slots round robin, and each is due or not:

  | Slot            | What it does                                               |
  |-----------------+------------------------------------------------------------|
  | Sample          | reads parity                                               |
  | Compute         | runs the K controller's cycle                              |
  | Ops             | one operation of the desk                                  |
  | Daily           | the basket's relief and mark                               |
  | Sync, per pool  | collects that pool's fees                                  |
  | Respond         | pairs what waits and works the gaps                        |
  | Arb, per TOKEN  | a consistency cycle, taken only at a realized profit       |

  Sample comes first, so every other slot acts on a market already read, and no trade of the same
  turn can spoil the reading.  A target that fails cannot trap the cursor.
- *Each step is small.*  Every step marks the credit first and trades at most 1% of a pool's depth,
  so the outside arbitrage re-pins between steps and no step moves a price far.
- *Amortizing the scan.*  Deciding whether an arbitrage slot is due costs three cold pool reads,
  about 14,000 gas.  A call examines at most =maxScan= slots, the cursor moves past every slot
  examined, and the block is marked idle only once consecutive calls have found every slot not due.
  After that a further call is a storage read and a return.  Any trade that touches the basket
  re-arms it.
- *A clock per chain.*  The wheel keys its work to the chain's own block number; where that is not
  the chain's own (Arbitrum reports the L1's), a small adapter supplies the right one.

*Time without a scheduler.*  The EVM has no clock that runs unasked, and the wheel may be turned
every block or once a day.  Nothing in the machine counts turns.  The filter's gains, the urgency
count and the release allowance are all closed functions of the time elapsed since they were last
touched, so the basket lands in the same place however often it is called.  An unobserved stretch
is credited one day at most: silence earns no authority to trade.

** Paying the caller
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: pay
:END:

The goal: the calling pays for itself on every chain, without the basket knowing the gas price or
the price of ETH.

- *No cure, no pay.*  A tick pays only for work done.  Callers simulate first and send only when
  there is work; an idle call is the caller's loss, never the basket's.
- *A share of the value.*  A slot that captures value pays its caller a share /s/ of it.  A caller
  calls when /s/ x value exceeds its gas, so the action margin sets itself per chain: on an L1 the
  wheel acts on large gaps, on a rollup on small ones.
- *A reserve for the work that captures nothing.*  Upkeep earns nothing by itself.  A slice of the
  basket's income flows into a reserve, and each working tick pays its caller /kappa/ of the
  reserve's balance.  The reserve builds when the wheel is under-called and pays less when it is
  over-called; at equilibrium a working tick pays the funding rate over the calling rate, and
  callers call until that equals their gas.  The calling rate sets itself, with no oracle.  A cap
  sends the excess to the basket: the reserve is a gas budget, not a second yield.
- *Competition is harmless.*  Callers racing for a tick bid their priority fees up to the pay less
  their gas; that transfer is the block builder's, out of the pay.  The basket's cost is fixed at
  /s/ and /kappa/.

The reserve and its pay are built.  The slice is agreed -- a tenth of the fees and the arbitrage
credited to the basket -- and not yet wired, so no result below is net of it.

* What the simulations say
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: evidence
:END:

There are three kinds of evidence here, and they are not equally strong.

** A model of the pools
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: evidence-model
:END:

Synthetic worlds (=alberta_buck/sim/basket_harvest.py=): independent TOKENs at about 1.5% a day in
three regimes -- fast reversion (a 10-day half-life), slow damped cycles with momentum, and a
random walk as the control -- with an outside arbitrageur re-pinning every pool each step.  Eight
seeds over four years; a year's return over simply holding the starting commodities:

| regime         | pools alone | with the basket's own arbitrage |
|----------------+-------------+---------------------------------|
| fast reversion | +0.66%      | +1.63%                          |
| slow cycles    | +1.09%      | +1.52%                          |
| random walk    | -1.19%      | +0.55%                          |

The basket's arbitrage recaptures what the outsiders took, to the basis point.  Costless, perfect
rebalancing at the market price would have earned 2.6% in the fast world: pools alone keep about a
quarter of it, and the arbitrage lifts that to about two thirds.

** The lab
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: evidence-lab
:END:

The lab (=alberta_buck/sim/control_lab/=) runs the real contracts for 120 days on three TOKENs
with a basket that starts at one million BUCKs.  The wheel turns four times a day.  Every gain is
measured against the same basket doing no rebalancing, valued at outside prices.  Its price
shapes are made up, and its cycles are far faster than real commodities, so read it for which
rule matters, not for what a basket earns.

*The lean does most of the work.*  Gain over 120 days, in BUCKs, on the one-million basket:

| Prices                      | No lean | Tilt 1/4 | Tilt 1/2 | Tilt 1   |
|-----------------------------+---------+----------+----------+----------|
| Clean cycles                | +54,908 | +76,611  | +96,241  | +131,337 |
| Commodity-like              | +20,764 | +26,888  | +34,633  | +47,822  |
| One TOKEN reprices for good | -3,940  | -5,803   | -7,787   | -11,410  |

Every step up in the tilt buys more of both: more gain where prices return, more loss where one
does not.  At one half the loss on the one-way move is 0.7% of the basket.

*The pace does not depend on the calling.*  Turned 24 times a day in place of 4, the basket moves
the same BUCKs for the same gain, in two or three times as many pieces.  Between half a day and
two days, how fast a gap is released hardly matters.

*A count beats a band.*  The urgency count earns about half again what a fixed two-point band
does, and makes more trades to do it.  Per trade the band earns more, so which is better depends
on what a trade costs, and that waits for the callers to be paid.

*On realistic prices rebalancing is a small bonus.*  Fifty years of data say basic commodities
follow inflation, each with its own delay: six of eight series stayed within 0.3% a year of it.
A lab run built that way, a decade long, had the basket's cost rise 47% in dollars.  The BUCK kept
pace, within 0.3% of its basket on average, and rebalancing added 2.3% over the decade.  On this
evidence the basket's first job is to hold value through inflation.

*The arbitrage is real, and small.*  Against a deep outside market, owning the cycles was worth
7,000 to 19,000 BUCKs on the one-million basket in 120 days, more where prices are noisy.  It comes
from one simulated arbitrageur with no competitor.  Do not count on it.

*What it costs.*  The upkeep is about 8 million gas a day at four turns a day, nearly all of it
trading.  Gas does not grow with the basket and the gain does, so there is a size below which
upkeep costs more than it earns, much smaller on a rollup than on an L1.

** A simulated economy
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: evidence-economy
:END:

The strongest test runs the real contracts for five years on an in-process EVM
(=alberta_buck/sim/=): five commodity TOKENs -- labour, construction, food, energy and gold -- on
their real price histories, and on a copy made to revert; depositors who come and go; households
refinancing mortgages into BUCKs; savers; arbitrageurs; the K controller; the desk; and the wheel,
turned twice a day.

| Five years                                      | Real prices  | Reverting prices |
|                                                 | (two seeds)  | (two seeds)      |
|-------------------------------------------------+--------------+------------------|
| A share, from 1.00                              | 1.19, 1.29   | 1.28, 1.40       |
| Ahead of holding the same commodities           | +17%, +29%   | +26%, +41%       |
| The BUCK from its basket, on average            | 0.5%, 0.8%   | 0.5%, 0.8%       |
| The same, at its worst                          | 3.1%, 5.0%   | 4.0%, 4.4%       |
| =K=, from 0.72, at the end                      | 0.74, 0.76   | 0.77, 0.77       |
| Equity it could spend on an exit, at the least  | 62%, 69%     | 66%, 67%         |

That is 3 to 5% a year over holding on real prices, and 5 to 7% where prices revert.  =K= stays
in the middle of its range, and the BUCK within a percent of its basket on average.

The net mark is most of it.  The same shell marked plainly, with the upkeep that mark needs,
ended 4 to 7% behind in all four runs, and on three days in four had under 5% of its equity to
spend on an exit, because pairing used its whole limit.

*Harder casts.*  The same contracts ran five years in thirteen other worlds built to strain them:
waves of households arriving to refinance, debtors saving their surplus into the basket, forced
excursions of the BUCK, an arbitrage raid.  In every one the BUCK stayed within half a percent of
its basket on average, the raid included.  In five of them =K= ended at its upper limit of 0.95.
Those worlds open far from parity, which carries =K= there within months, and with the BUCK back
at parity nothing pulls it down again.

** The desk
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: evidence-desk
:END:

The desk made no trade in any of the lab's 310 runs, and few in the simulated economy.  The BUCK
seldom left its band, and never for the thirty days the desk waits before it issues.  That is
what a desk beyond a band should do when the band holds.  It also means nothing here tests the
desk.  Its rules are lessons from an earlier desk's model ([[#lag][above]]), not measurements of
this one.

** Cautions
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: cautions
:END:

- The simulated arbitrageurs demand a 60-basis-point edge over the fees, so they leave wider gaps
  than competitive searchers would.  Against a real market the wheel's take will be smaller.
- Nothing above is net of gas, or of what the wheel's callers would be paid.
- A simulated economy is only as good as its cast.  An earlier cast, written for a different
  basket, kept every BUCK an exit paid it and spent none.  That manufactured a shortage of BUCKs
  which drove =K= to its limit, and for a while it looked like a flaw in the basket.  Reading
  every account's balance found it.  When a world shows a pressure that never lets up, look for
  an agent that never closes its loop.
- Two seeds of the economy, and one of the lab.

* Open questions
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: open
:END:

1. *The desk.*  It needs worlds built to take the BUCK outside its band and hold it there: an
   attack, a squeeze, a genuine revaluation it should lose to.
2. *What holds the BUCK inside the band.*  The basket's lean helps on one side, =K= is slow, and the
   desk waits outside.  The rest is left to holders who profit by closing a gap.  How wide a gap
   they leave is not measured.
3. *Paying the callers.*  What the results are net of the reserve, and what the smallest trade
   worth making is.  Today the basket trades whenever a gap is open, however small the piece, and
   a turn that trades nothing still re-reads every pool.
4. *A BUCK that keeps rising.*  The basket's limit is =K= times an equity counted in BUCKs, so a
   dearer BUCK lowers it, and the basket then buys BUCKs when they are scarcest.  Counting the limit
   in baskets instead would stop that, at the price of a larger loss should the BUCK never come
   back.  No world with a sound cast has needed it.
5. *First values.*  The tilt of one half, the lean against a cheap BUCK, and the ceiling at half
   of =K x equity= were each chosen once.  Holding cheap BUCKs carries demurrage; whether it pays
   for that is not settled.
6. *Demurrage in equity.*  Whether equity should deduct the demurrage on BUCKs held in the pools
   as it accrues, and not when the BUCKs come back.
7. *Real speed.*  The lab's cycles are far faster than real commodities.
8. *Stress.*  A run of large exits, and a cut in =K= to its floor.
9. *A competitive market.*  How much of the cycles remains when real searchers compete the large
   gaps down to gas, and which of a v4 hook or an auction for the right to go first should take
   the gaps v3 must race for.
10. *Depth.*  A thin BUCK/USDC pool caps every cycle and every exit that is sold for dollars.  The
    basket owns no BUCK/USDC position, and should not; who provides that depth is a question for
    any deployment.

* Risks
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: risks
:END:

- *A price that never returns.*  The lean trusts each constituent's reference price.  When a TOKEN
  reprices for good the reference is wrong, and the basket stays short of that TOKEN, by at most the
  cap, until governance corrects it.  The admission rule is the real defence
  ([[file:alberta-buck-ethereum-basket.org::#the-asset-that-never-reverts][the asset that never comes back]]).
- *The callbacks and the credits.*  The swap callback must accept only the pool it expects, only
  inside its own cycle; the cycle entry only from the wheel itself; the basket's upkeep only from
  its wheel.  The contracts do all three and test them, unaudited.
- *A policy that is wrong.*  The signals and the policy hold no funds.  A stale or adversarial
  policy can mis-time trades within the caps -- 5% of a holding a turn, 1% of a pool a swap -- and
  cannot move value out of the basket.
- *A farmable reserve.*  The share /s/ cannot be farmed: pushing a pool costs more than closing the
  gap returns.  /kappa/ per working tick is not tied to the work's size, so a large reserve might
  reward many small, cheaply opened gaps.
- *Gas on an L1.*  A working tick is expensive; on an L1 only large gaps pay, and those are the ones
  searchers race for.  The case is strongest on rollups.
- *Liveness.*  No calls, no harvest, and new deposits wait in the wallet.  Nothing already placed is
  at risk, and exits are paid from the line or in kind.
- *A desk that looks healthy.*  Every runaway of the earlier desk showed a profit while it ran.
  Watch the desk's inventory beside its P&L, always.

* Conclusion
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: conclusion
:END:

The first BuckBasket assumed that the world's arbitrageurs keep it honest and pay it to be kept.
The first half is true.  The second is only partly true, because the price of keeping a pool honest
is paid by the pool.

This basket brings the work home.  It counts against its limit only the BUCKs it has let out, so a
deposit is at work at once and an exit is paid before anything is sold.  It closes its own gaps
with cycles that need no capital and cannot lose.  It leans each commodity against its price, and
waits for a run to stop before it trades against it.  A desk with its own books stands outside
the band, and one wheel that anyone may turn does all of it.

Most of the gain comes from the simplest idea in it: sell what is dear and buy what is cheap,
among things that come back.  The machinery is there to do that patiently, with nobody in charge.
