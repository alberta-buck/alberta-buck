#+BEGIN_ABSTRACT
The BuckBasket is the liquidity of its own TOKEN/BUCK pools.  Every time a
commodity's price moves in the wider market, the basket's pools are left
behind, and an arbitrageur who closes the gap takes the difference out of
the basket's position.  The fees they pay are the basket's compensation --
and, by a well-understood result in market-making, not all of it.  The
BasketWheel lets the basket close those gaps itself: a /consistency cycle/
through a commodity's three prices (TOKEN/BUCK, TOKEN/USDC, BUCK/USDC)
that needs no capital, changes no supply, and cannot lose money; run by a
/work wheel/, a bounded state machine any caller can advance and that pays
its callers from what it captures.  The harvest goes to the basket's
depositors; a side effect keeps the basket's own valuation -- the number
=BUCK_K= is steered by -- closer to the truth.

*This is a prototype.*  The contracts exist (=src/wheel/=) and run in a
simulated economy; none of it is deployed, audited, or settled.  This
paper sets out the design and its theory, what it intends to capture that
the BuckBasket's other embodiments leave on the table, what the simulation
says so far, and the questions and risks still open.
#+END_ABSTRACT

* Status: a refinement under test
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: status
:END:

The BasketWheel refines the BuckBasket; it does not replace it.  It sits
/beside/ an existing basket as its own contract, holding two narrow
permissions: to credit captured value to the basket's depositors, and to
credit BUCK to its treasury.  Everything else it does, anyone could do.

* Where it sits among the BuckBasket's embodiments
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: embodiments
:END:

Three documents describe the BuckBasket, each a layer on the last:

- *The Direct embodiment* ([[file:alberta-buck-ethereum-direct.org][alberta-buck-ethereum-direct]])
  defines BUCK directly against its commodities.  Each constituent has a
  TOKEN/BUCK Uniswap V3 pool; the basket's value in BUCK -- /bvib/ -- is read
  from those pools, and the K controller steers bvib to 1.  A depositor
  presents a commodity TOKEN, the basket mints the matching BUCK and adds
  both to the pool as full-range liquidity, and a receipt records the
  claim.
- *The BuckBasket as a savings facility* ([[file:alberta-buck-ethereum-basket.org][alberta-buck-ethereum-basket]])
  explains why that liquidity earns: base commodities revert to the mean,
  a constant-product pool sells what has risen and buys what has fallen,
  and the world's arbitrageurs -- who neither know nor care what a BUCK is
  -- do the trading and pay the pools' fees for the privilege.  A
  director ([[file:alberta-buck-rebalance.org][alberta-buck-rebalance]]) and
  the monetary operations ([[file:alberta-buck-operations.org][alberta-buck-operations]])
  steer the basket's weights and BUCK's supply.
- *The BasketWheel* (this paper) keeps all of that, and changes one thing:
  it stops leaving the /consistency/ of the basket's pools to strangers.

The basket paper's engine is the arbitrageur "working for free".  The
arbitrageur is not working for free.  Closing a gap on the basket's pool is
precisely how they are paid -- and they are paid by the basket.

* What the basket leaks today
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: leak
:END:

A liquidity provider whose pool trails an external price loses to whoever
brings it back.  The loss has a name -- /loss-versus-rebalancing/, LVR
(Milionis, Moallemi, Roughgarden and Zhang, 2022) -- and a size: for a
constant-product pool of value /V/ whose asset moves with volatility
/sigma/, about

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
    LVR  ~  V * sigma^2 / 8     per unit time
#+END_EXAMPLE

before fees.  Fees claw some of it back; how much depends on how often and
how far the price moves between trades.  The BuckBasket is exactly this
provider: it is (nearly) the only liquidity in its TOKEN/BUCK pools, and
every move of a commodity's USD price opens a gap on the basket's own
position.

Two costs follow.

1. *Value.*  The arbitrageur who closes the gap keeps the LVR net of the
   fees.  In the simulated economy, whose about $32M of basket liquidity moves
   with an inflated 50-67% annual volatility, that is on the order of
   $3,000 a day; at a more ordinary 20% volatility, about $400 a day.  (The
   simulation's noise overstates it; the order of magnitude is the point.)
2. *Truth.*  bvib is a time-average of the TOKEN/BUCK pools.  An
   arbitrageur acts only when a gap pays more than the fees and a margin,
   so the pools sit anywhere inside that threshold, and bvib -- the number
   the K controller steers -- lags the commodities by up to the threshold.
   In the simulated economy the residual gap at each day's end has a median
   of 117-126 basis points and a 90th percentile of 196-257.

* The consistency cycle
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: cycle
:END:

For a constituent /i/, three pools quote the same commodity:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
                 TOKEN_i
                /       \
      TOKEN/USDC         TOKEN/BUCK    (the basket's own pool)
              /           \
          USDC ----------- BUCK
                BUCK/USDC
#+END_EXAMPLE

If the three prices are consistent, going round the triangle returns what
went in, less the fees.  If they are not, one direction returns more.
Going round in that direction, by the right amount, closes the gap and
keeps the difference.  That is a /consistency cycle/.

** The algebra of a cycle
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: algebra
:END:

A constant-product leg with reserves /(X, Y)/ and fee factor /g/ (1 minus
the fee) pays, for an input /x/,

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
    out(x)  =  g Y x / (X + g x)  =  r x / (1 + k x),     r = g Y / X,   k = g / X
#+END_EXAMPLE

-- a Moebius map through the origin: /r/ is the leg's marginal rate at zero
size (its price after the fee), /k/ its curvature (the inverse of its
depth).  Such maps compose into maps of the same form.  Feeding one leg
into the next,

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
    r = r1 r2 r3
    k = k1 + k2 r1 + k3 r1 r2
    cycle(x) = r x / (1 + k x)
#+END_EXAMPLE

so a whole cycle is summarized by two numbers.  /r/ is the /gap/: the
product of the three prices after fees; the cycle pays exactly when r > 1.
Its profit, cycle(x) - x, is greatest at

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
    x*  =  (sqrt(r) - 1) / k          profit*  =  (sqrt(r) - 1)^2 / k
#+END_EXAMPLE

where the cycle's marginal rate has fallen to 1: the optimal cycle closes
the gap down to the fees, no further.  A full-range Uniswap V3 position is
constant product on its virtual reserves, so the closed form is exact for
the basket's own pools and a good approximation elsewhere; where a trade
leaves a concentrated range, the realized output falls short of the quote
(by about 1.2-1.5% of the value in the simulation).

** What makes a cycle safe
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: safe
:END:

- *No capital, no inventory.*  A Uniswap V3 pool pays a swap's output
  /before/ it collects the input, in a callback.  The wheel's first leg
  pays out; the other two legs run inside its callback; what they return
  pays the first.  So a cycle needs nothing on hand, from any starting
  token -- not even a flash loan or a flash mint.  USDC exists only between
  two swaps inside one transaction: the basket never holds a USDC position.
- *No change in supply.*  No BUCK is issued or destroyed; value moves from
  the venues to the basket.  A consistency cycle is not a monetary
  operation, and it never trades BUCK against its basket value (bvib) --
  that is the undertakings' and the desk's business, under their own rules.
- *It cannot lose money.*  The cycle checks its /realized/ output against
  its input and reverts below a minimum edge.  An attacker who pushes a
  pool first only pays the basket to push it back.  (What an attacker /can/
  do is discussed under [[#risks][Risks]].)
- *It keeps the gauge true.*  Closing the TOKEN/BUCK pools' gaps to the
  USD venues makes bvib read the external valuation, instead of whatever
  the last trade left behind -- what the external arbitrageurs do today,
  without their threshold.

** Where the profit lands
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: profit
:END:

The same three swaps, in the same rotation, earn the same whichever token
the cycle starts from; the start decides only what the profit is made of.

- *Start from the TOKEN*, and the profit is the commodity.  It is added to
  the basket as /depositor/ liquidity, with freshly minted partner BUCK
  booked as bonus principal -- exactly the mechanics the basket's stress
  fee already uses to pay the remaining depositors -- so every receipt's
  claim grows pro rata, and the basket still burns exactly what it minted.
- *Start from BUCK*, and the profit is BUCK: it funds the callers' reserve
  (below) up to a cap, and the rest goes to the treasury.

* Who can take which gap
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: band
:END:

A searcher going round the triangle pays three pool fees.  The basket pays
two: on its own TOKEN/BUCK pool it is the only liquidity provider, so the
fee it pays comes back to it.  With the simulation's fee tiers (TOKEN/USDC
0.30%, TOKEN/BUCK 0.30%, BUCK/USDC 0.05%):

| The gap (roughly)                     | Who takes it                                                      |
|---------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------------|
| under 0.35%, plus gas                 | nobody: a cycle costs more than it earns                          |
| 0.35% to 0.65%, plus gas              | only the basket: the fee it pays on its own pool comes back to it |
| over 0.65%, plus gas and a margin     | searchers, directly -- and they pay the basket's fee on the way   |
| any gap the basket's own trade opens  | the basket, in the same transaction: there is no race             |

The middle row is the wheel's natural domain: value no one else can take.
The top row it must race for, and on today's Uniswap V3 pools it cannot
stop a searcher from going first.  Capturing the large gaps too needs
control of the order of swaps -- a Uniswap v4 hook that runs the wheel
before the first swap of each block, or an auction for the right to go
first (the auction-managed AMM of Adams, Moallemi, Reynolds and Robinson,
2024).  That is the upgrade path, not the prototype.

* The work wheel
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: wheel
:END:

Someone has to notice a gap and send the transaction.  The BasketWheel
does not wait for a keeper it pays by the hour: it is a /work wheel/, a
bounded state machine that any caller advances with =tick(maxWork,
maxScan)=, and that does the block's work once, however many callers there
are.

- *Slots over task kinds.*  Each kind of work -- a consistency cycle for
  each constituent, the director's signal refresh, the treasury's sweep,
  the monetary quadrants, the controller's =compute= -- owns a range of
  slots.  A cursor walks them round-robin.  Kinds are Solidity mixins over
  a common chassis, each claiming the slots after its bases' (the C3
  linearization fixes the layout), so a basket's wheel is assembled from
  the kinds it wants; a kind with no target configured has no slots.
- *Amortizing the scan, not just the work.*  Deciding whether an
  arbitrage slot is due costs three cold pool reads, about 14,000 gas; scanning
  six constituents idle would cost more than a piggybacked tick should.  So
  a call examines at most =maxScan= slots, the cursor moves past every slot
  examined, and the block is marked idle only once consecutive calls have
  found every slot not due.  Many small calls in a block share one scan;
  after it, each further call is a storage read and a return.  Any trade
  that touches the basket re-arms the wheel within the block.
- *A clock per chain.*  The wheel keys its work to the chain's own block
  number; where that is not the chain's own (Arbitrum reports the L1's), a
  small adapter supplies the right one.

Folded into transactions a caller sends anyway -- the basket's own
deposits and redemptions, an Alberta Buck agent's routine calls -- a tick
whose block is done costs a few thousand gas.  A tick that works costs
what its work costs: about 365,000 gas a cycle, about 630,000-780,000 for a working
arbitrage tick with its depositor credit.

* Paying the caller
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: pay
:END:

The design goal: the calling pays for itself on every chain, without the
basket knowing the gas price or the price of ETH.

- *No cure, no pay.*  A tick pays only for work done.  Callers simulate
  first and send only when there is work; an idle call is the caller's
  loss, never the basket's.
- *A share of the value.*  A kind that captures value pays its caller a
  share /s/ of it.  A caller calls when /s/ x value exceeds its own gas, so
  the action margin sets itself per chain: on an L1 the wheel acts on large
  gaps, on a rollup on small ones.  /s/ trades how quickly the pools are
  made consistent against how much of the profit is kept.
- *A reserve for the work that captures nothing.*  Upkeep -- the
  director's refresh, the sweep, the controller -- earns nothing by
  itself.  A small slice of the basket's yield (and of the arbitrage's
  value) flows into a reserve; each working tick pays its caller /kappa/
  of the reserve's balance.  So the reserve /builds up/ when the wheel is
  under-called and /pays out less/ when it is over-called; at equilibrium a
  working tick pays about the funding rate divided by the calling rate,
  and callers call until that equals their gas.  The calling rate sets
  itself, per chain, with no oracle.  A cap on the reserve sends the excess
  to the treasury: the reserve is a gas budget, not a second yield.
- *Competition is harmless.*  Callers racing for the same tick bid their
  priority fees up to the pay less their gas; that transfer is the block
  builder's, out of the pay.  The basket's cost is fixed at the share and
  kappa.

* What it intends to capture
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: capture
:END:

A basket's excess return over simply holding its commodities comes from
three different mispricings, each with its own harvester:

1. *Venue inconsistency* -- a TOKEN/BUCK price that disagrees with
   TOKEN/USDC times BUCK/USDC.  Its harvester: *the wheel's consistency
   cycles*.  Its risk: none on a cycle; a side effect on the gauge.
2. *Commodity relative value* -- one constituent against another, or
   against its own trend.  Its harvester: the director's rebalancing.  Its
   risk: whipsaw in a secular trend.
3. *BUCK against its basket* -- bvib away from 1.  Its harvester: the
   undertakings, the desk, the ladder.  Its risk: inventory that persists
   -- a monetary matter, under the monetary rules.

Beneath all three lies the base: the fees on the basket's own pools, less the LVR nobody
recaptures.  The other embodiments earn the base and leg 2, and leave leg 1
to strangers -- who keep it.  The wheel's claim is leg 1 alone:

- *LVR, recaptured.*  The value a gap represents goes to the basket's
  depositors (or its treasury), not to the searcher who happens to close
  it; the fee band's value, which today no one takes, is the basket's by
  construction.
- *A truer gauge.*  bvib stops lagging the commodities behind an
  arbitrageur's threshold, so the K controller steers on the basket's
  value rather than on its pools' staleness.
- *Upkeep that pays for itself.*  The same wheel, and the same reserve,
  turn the basket's other maintenance -- work that today waits for a
  keeper, or for a depositor to happen by.

It deliberately does not reach for legs 2 or 3.  Relative value is the
director's, on its own slow timescale; BUCK against its basket changes the
money supply, and belongs to the monetary rules.

* What the simulation says so far
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: evidence
:END:

The simulated economy (=alberta_buck/sim/=) runs the real contracts on an
in-process EVM: six commodity TOKENs on their real 2020-2025 price
histories, the basket and its director, the K controller, depositors,
savers, debtors, and a cast of arbitrageurs.  The readings below are its
calm arm, days 30-59 (the first month is the pools' own settling).

1. *It pays, on both chains.*  The prototype captured about $1,300-1,700 a day
   on a $16-28M basket -- 2-3% of its value a year, on this thin and noisy
   cast -- and its calls paid for themselves with room to spare even at
   5-gwei L1 gas.
2. *About half of it was the external arbitrageurs'.*  Their cycles fell by
   30-35%: the wheel took roughly half its value from gaps no one else
   could take, and half from gaps the arbitrageurs would have closed.  (On a
   real chain searchers would keep more of the large gaps; the simulation
   orders its actors, not their fees.)
3. *The gauge became truer.*  Measured against the basket valued at the
   commodities' reference prices, bvib's error fell by a third at the median
   and by 43% at the 90th percentile.  bvib also moved more -- the
   commodities' real movement reaching it instead of lagging -- and more,
   smaller cycles per call gave most of that added variance back.
4. *The contract matches the prototype.*  The Solidity v1, driven by the
   simulation, captured what the prototype captured (within 10%), and --
   starting from the TOKEN -- credited $51.8k to the depositors in 30 days,
   about 3% a year on the savings basket, at about 365,000 gas a cycle through the
   real identity-bound BUCK.

Two cautions about what these numbers are.  The simulation's arbitrageurs
demand a 60-basis-point edge on top of the fees, so they leave wide gaps
that real searchers, competing toward their gas cost, would not; the
wheel's take against a competitive market will be smaller, and more of it
will come from the fee band.  And one calm month says nothing yet about
bursts -- demand and supply shocks, a book-loading attack, a liquidity
exit -- which are being run now, wheel off and on, over two simulated years
each.

* Open questions
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: open
:END:

1. *The fee band, in practice.*  v1 takes only /direct/ profit -- gaps wider
   than all three fees.  The band only the basket can take needs the
   basket's own fee to come back within the cycle: a small TOKEN float, or
   the wheel inside the basket's shell (the savings basket's shell has
   about 8 KB to spare; the monetary shells about 1-3 KB).  That is v2.
2. *The share and kappa.*  Fixed, per chain, or adaptive to the reserve's
   balance and the calling rate?
3. *Cycle sizing.*  v1 caps a cycle at a fraction of its /first/ leg's
   reserve, so the two starting tokens size differently; every leg should
   be capped.
4. *Legs 1 and 2 together.*  The wheel re-prices the pools the director
   reads, and the director's trades open gaps the wheel closes.  Do their
   harvests add, or does one eat the other?  (Being measured.)
5. *A competitive market.*  How much remains when real searchers compete
   the large gaps down to gas -- and does thinning their participation
   widen the gaps beyond the wheel's reach?
6. *The large gaps.*  A v4 hook that runs the wheel first in each block, or
   an auction for the right to, would capture what v1 must race for; each
   has its own trust and liveness questions.
7. *The reserve's funding.*  From pool-fee yield, from the arbitrage's
   value, or both -- and which of the upkeep kinds it should pay for.
8. *Depth.*  A thin BUCK/USDC pool caps every cycle.  The wheel owns no
   BUCK/USDC position (and should not); how much depth the cycles need, and
   who provides it, is an open question for any deployment.

* Risks
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: risks
:END:

- *The callbacks and the credits.*  The wheel's swap callback must accept
  only the pool it is expecting, only inside its own cycle; the cycle entry
  is callable only by the wheel itself; the basket's credit entry points
  only by the wheel.  v1 does all three and tests them, but these are the
  surfaces an attacker would probe first, and they have not been audited.
- *A farmable reserve.*  Pushing a pool to open a gap costs the attacker
  more than the share /s/ of closing it returns, so the share cannot be
  farmed.  The reserve's kappa, paid per /working/ tick, is not tied to the
  size of the work: when the reserve is large, an attacker might open many
  small gaps cheaply and collect kappa of the reserve for each.  The
  minimum edge, the cap on the reserve and a pay bounded by the work's
  value are the defences to test.
- *Displacing the arbitrageurs.*  The wheel takes some of what external
  arbitrageurs earn.  If they respond by thinning out, the gaps beyond the
  wheel's reach -- the large ones on v3 -- may close more slowly.
- *The wheel's own impact on the gauge.*  bvib moves more with the wheel.
  The evidence so far is that it moves /truer/; a wheel with coarse cycles
  adds its own lumpiness, which smaller, more frequent cycles remove.  K
  reads bvib, so this matters more than it appears.
- *Liveness.*  No calls, no harvest.  The reserve pays for calling, and
  the basket's own traffic can carry ticks, but a wheel nobody turns is
  simply the BuckBasket without it -- no worse, and no better.
- *Gas on an L1.*  A working tick is expensive (about 630,000-780,000 gas); on
  an L1 only large gaps pay, and those are the ones searchers race for.
  The wheel's case is strongest on rollups.
- *Size.*  The monetary basket's shells are close to Ethereum's contract
  size limit, so the wheel lives beside them as its own contract, holding
  narrow permissions -- a second contract to trust and to upgrade.
- *A trending market.*  The wheel does not change the basket's exposure:
  in a secular trend a constant-product basket still trails a
  buy-and-hold of the runaway commodity.  The wheel recaptures leakage; it
  does not cure the basket's one real risk
  ([[file:alberta-buck-ethereum-basket.org::#the-asset-that-never-reverts][the asset that never reverts]]).

* Conclusion
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: conclusion
:END:

The BuckBasket's promise is that the world's arbitrageurs keep it honest
and pay it to be kept.  The first half is true; the second half is only
partly true, because the price of keeping a pool honest is paid by the
pool.  The BasketWheel moves that work in-house: the basket closes its own
gaps, with cycles that need no capital and cannot lose, turned by anyone
who finds it worth their gas, and it hands the difference to its
depositors.  If the prototype's early readings survive the bursts, a
competitive market and an audit, the savings basket earns a little more,
and -- as much to the point -- its valuation tells the K controller the
truth a little sooner.  Until then it is what this paper calls it: a
refinement under test.
