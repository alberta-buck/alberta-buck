A citizen should be able to prove who they are without wearing a name tag
on every purchase. A counterparty should be able to keep an honest receipt.
A court should be able to examine evidence. These are compatible ambitions.

Bob wants to pay Carol 100 BUCK. Mallory wants to watch. We will build the
payment one layer at a time, beginning before either citizen has an account.

Ethereum is an open ledger. Its addresses, transactions and token movements
are observable. The privacy question is which observations can be joined:
an account to a person, two accounts to one owner, or a payment into a pool
to a payment out. Like a VPN or onion router, Notes introduce separation
between endpoints. Here the separation comes from funded commitments and
proofs, rather than a chain of packet relays. The result sought is
/pseudonymity and compartmentalisation/: a public business account, private
savings and everyday spending need not advertise their common owner or all
their relationships. Encryption is a door. Privacy also needs walls.

This paper describes the current v2 design and prototype, not an operating
Alberta government service. Its credential privacy argument awaits
independent review, and its development proof setups require production
ceremonies before deployment. The illustrations assume honest certification,
sound cryptography and wallets that retain their secrets.[fn:status]

* How to read the pictures

Bob stays left, Carol right, Mallory in the middle. The lower strip names
the /separate/ issuer, wallet checker or blockchain contract doing the work;
Mallory is never the verifier merely because she occupies the middle column.

Each picture carries forward the relevant contents of the last. A /plus/
adds material, a /lock/ marks encrypted contents, an /opening arrow/ exposes
contents to their intended reader, and a /check/ marks a completed check.
An arrow crossing Mallory's column gives her a view of the traffic, not a
decryption key. Her column says what she can actually read or do.

We need only a few objects:

- A *record* contains the name and other certified particulars. Its fixed
  encoding produces an identity fingerprint, called *M* in the pictures.
  A fingerprint lets a wallet check a record; it does not spell out a name.
- A *credential* is the issuer's signature on that identity. Its private
  original stays in the wallet; a *presentation* proves possession without
  publishing the original.
- An *envelope* is encrypted data. A *proof* lets a checker verify a
  particular fact about its contents without opening it.
- A *commitment* is a sealed description with a short public fingerprint.
  Its *opening* supplies the hidden contents and randomness. A *spent marker*
  records redemption once, without normally identifying the commitment.
- A *membership root* is a published summary of certified records. A
  *path* connects one record to that summary; a private membership proof
  can establish the connection while hiding the record and path.

The three Notes stories are alternatives for the same payment, not
conversions of B1 into A1 into A2. We give the 100-BUCK note a place in a
500-BUCK batch so that minting does not announce its individual value.
Earlier disclosures are never erased by changing note style.

* First, give the person a credential

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** I1. Alberta's issuer meets Bob; separately, it meets Carol

Each applicant provides the evidence the issuer requires. The issuer checks
it and prepares a consistently encoded identity record. Bob and the issuer
know Bob's record. Carol's enrollment is a separate conversation.

Mallory may notice an appointment or a network connection. A protected
enrollment channel keeps the submitted documents out of that observation.
Cryptography preserves the issuer's statement; the quality of the original
identity check remains the issuer's responsibility.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt I1. Separate protected enrollments provide identity evidence to Alberta's issuer.
[[file:images/privacy/i1.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** I2. The issuer returns a signed credential

The issuer fingerprints the canonical record and signs it. Each citizen
receives the record and credential through protected delivery, and checks
the signature against the issuer's recognised public key. The issuer keeps
its own issuance evidence. The raw credential is not published on Ethereum.

This is certification, not an account password. The issuer already knows
the identity it certified. The objective is to avoid giving it a new test
for recognising every future account carrying that identity.

#+ATTR_LATEX: :width \linewidth :float nil
#+ATTR_HTML: :alt I2. Each citizen privately receives and verifies a signed identity credential.
[[file:images/privacy/i2.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** I3. The citizen makes the keys

At enrollment, each wallet derives an independent *receiving secret* from
its owner's secret seed, using a dedicated key-derivation domain. Only the
public receiving key is available to payers. The seed restores the private
key; an identity record cannot. A photocopy of your identification should
not open your mail.

Enrollment also certifies the identity-to-mailbox association in salted
commitments. One supports later private spending proofs; a separate one
lets a payer check the public receiving key. Different salts keep the two
records from being trivially matched. The seed and receiving secret stay
with the citizen. Authorities know the associations they certify; public
observers see hiding records and their published root.[fn:enrollment]

#+ATTR_LATEX: :width \linewidth :float nil
#+ATTR_HTML: :alt I3. Wallet seeds derive private receiving keys locally; certification records hide the associations.
[[file:images/privacy/i3.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** I4. The wallet packages a fresh account

An externally owned account, or *EOA*, has an Ethereum signing key. BUCK
adds a separate identity encryption key for that account. Neither is the
Notes receiving secret.[fn:ethereum]

Bob's wallet encrypts his identity fingerprint under his new identity key.
It adds a freshly masked credential presentation and a proof: /this is a
certified identity, this envelope contains that identity, and I hold the
corresponding identity key/. Bob's EOA signs the registration transaction.
Carol does the same. The credential's original signature stays private.

#+ATTR_LATEX: :width \linewidth :float nil
#+ATTR_HTML: :alt I4. Each registration package adds a hidden credential presentation, identity ciphertext and proof.
[[file:images/privacy/i4.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** I5. The registry checks the package, without opening it

The registry checks the issuer, proof and key ownership, including the
specific account, chain, registry and action to which the proof belongs.
It stores the account's public identity key, ciphertext and issuer. The
transaction history retains the presentation and proof.

Bob can repeat this locally with fresh keys and randomness. The design's
credential presentation gives Mallory no identity-equality test between
accounts, even if she later learns Bob's record. Funding links, reused
addresses and network observations are separate clues. Fresh cryptography
does not make an old public connection disappear.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt I5. Valid registration becomes a stored account record; fresh presentations support separate accounts.

#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

* Now, pay between two EOA accounts

Here Bob and Carol use private-identity accounts. They first establish
identity evidence in both directions, then Bob authorises the payment.
The chain will see the payment between their addresses. The envelopes
protect the identities inside that visible relationship.

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** E1. Bob addresses an identity envelope to Carol

Bob reads Carol's registered public identity key and makes a fresh
encryption of his own fingerprint for her. He adds a proof that its contents
match his registered identity, and calls BUCK's identity-bearing =approve=.

For this direct =transfer= example, the spending allowance is *zero*. This
creates the identity evidence without giving Carol permission to withdraw
Bob's funds. A nonzero allowance would additionally authorise delegated
spending up to that amount. Identity disclosure and authority to move money
are distinct jobs, even when an API can do both.

#+ATTR_LATEX: :width \linewidth :float nil
#+ATTR_HTML: :alt E1. Bob builds an identity envelope and proof for Carol, with no delegated spending allowance.
[[file:images/privacy/e1.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** E2. The contract verifies the envelope's provenance

The checker uses Bob's existing registry record, not a replacement supplied
by the request. It verifies possession of Bob's identity key and that the
new encryption carries the same identity under Carol's key.

BUCK records a fingerprint of the checked envelope. The envelope and proof
remain available in the transaction's public input data. Mallory can copy
them, but cannot replace Bob's identity with another person's and preserve
the proof. Carol now has something worth opening.

#+ATTR_LATEX: :width \linewidth :float nil
#+ATTR_HTML: :alt E2. Verified re-encryption becomes a receipt fragment; Carol retrieves the proven ciphertext.
[[file:images/privacy/e2.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** E3. Carol opens the envelope and checks the name

Carol decrypts with her account's private identity key. Out comes Bob's
fingerprint, *M_B*. Bob separately delivers his canonical identity record
over an authenticated, encrypted data channel. Carol fingerprints that
record and compares it with the decrypted point.

The proof answers /is this the identity bound to Bob's account?/ The record
answers /whose identity is it?/ Both must agree. Ethereum cannot itself
ensure that a human-readable record was delivered. The wallet checks
delivery before accepting the relationship. Holochain is the companion
papers' proposed data channel; the diagram does not assume a deployed hApp.

#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** E4. Carol returns the same assurance

Carol encrypts her identity for Bob, supplies the corresponding proof and
establishes the reverse receipt fragment, again with allowance zero. She
sends her record privately; Bob opens the envelope and checks the match.

The construction now contains two checked directions. Each citizen has
received a certified identity record. Mallory has observed two approvals
between addresses. No payment has happened yet. Recognising someone is
not the same as paying them.

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** E5. Bob signs the payment; the contract executes it

Bob's EOA signs =transfer(Carol, 100)=. BUCK checks the registered endpoints,
both identity directions and available funds, then executes the transfer
and emits its receipt references. The wallets retain the identity records,
proofs and confirmed payment reference together.

Mallory sees the two addresses, 100 BUCK and the time. Learning who owns
either address elsewhere can identify this activity. The gain here is
confidential identity binding. The next gain is separating the payment's
two ends.[fn:eoa]

* The Notes pool is also a participant

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** N1. Establish the pool relationship

Notes hold BUCK in a contract and pay it out when the required proofs pass.
The BUCK transfers into and out of that pool retain the ordinary identity
rules. A private Bob identifies himself to the pool before funding it;
private Carol does so before receiving its payout. They use the same
envelope-and-proof procedure just illustrated.

The pool operator holds the identity key bound to the pool and can decrypt
these approvals. It can associate identities with direct pool activity.
This is an institutional privacy boundary, not an invisible intermediary.
A1 and A2 still avoid publishing an exact note-to-redemption link; the
operator's records and timing clues must be considered separately.[fn:pool]

#+ATTR_LATEX: :width \linewidth :float nil
#+ATTR_HTML: :alt N1. Private users supply identity envelopes to the pool; its operator can decrypt these direct relationships.
[[file:images/privacy/n1.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

* B1: a bearer note from a public issuer

Bob writes the note; he is now the /note issuer/. Alberta remains the
/identity issuer/. These are different roles.

For B1 and A1, Bob uses a publicly identified note-issuing contract under
his control: his “note desk.” This matches the current public-issuer
contract-binding interface; ordinary EOA registration does not itself
switch on a public-identity flag.[fn:public-desk]

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** B1.1. Bob prepares a bearer opening

Bob chooses the 100-BUCK face value and a fresh random opening secret. He
packages the note's B1 style, value and public-issuer material into a
commitment. His registered identity key authenticates the batch.

There is no recipient lock yet. Whoever obtains the complete bearer
opening can seek redemption, provided they meet the identity requirements.
Bob's published identity lets a future holder know who wrote it.

#+ATTR_LATEX: :width \linewidth :float nil
#+ATTR_HTML: :alt B1.1. Bob builds a public-issuer bearer note with a secret opening and no designated recipient.
[[file:images/privacy/b1.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** B1.2. Funding turns the draft into a note

Bob's desk submits the commitments, issuer signature and batch-mint proof.
The proof establishes that the committed values add to 500 BUCK and that
the commitments extend the current note tree correctly. The contract
checks the public-issuer mode and signature, and pulls the funding.

Funding and insertion succeed together or revert together. Mallory sees
the issuer, total, batch size and commitments. She does not receive the
individual openings. A convincing picture of a note would not establish
any of these facts.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt B1.2. Proof verification and escrow funding atomically create the committed notes.
[[file:images/privacy/b2.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** B1.3. Carol receives and inspects the bearer package

Bob hands Carol the opening and mint reference, physically or through
protected delivery. Her wallet reconstructs the commitment, authenticates
the mint and issuer, and checks whether its spent marker has appeared.

Here interception is theft-enabling: Mallory with a copied opening and an
eligible account can race Carol. Bob can retain a copy too. The check says
/unspent now/, not /reserved for Carol/. Bearer delivery is convenient
precisely because possession carries authority; copies inherit it.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt B1.3. Carol reconstructs the funded note; copying its bearer opening permits a redemption race.
[[file:images/privacy/b3.png]]

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** B1.4. Carol adds an envelope identifying the depositor

Carol derives the spent marker from her opening and prepares a note-spend
proof. She also encrypts her own identity for Bob's registered issuer key.
A binding proof ties that envelope to the identity in her payout account;
a membership proof establishes current certification of that same identity.

Those checks share a properly blinded identity commitment. Mallory cannot
substitute a convenient certified person in one check and a different
person in the other. Carol's separate approval to the pool from N1 is also
in place before payout.

[[file:images/privacy/b4.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** B1.5. The contract checks the layers and pays

Notes verifies that this is a funded /B1/ note, that its marker is unused,
that the named issuer is the original issuer, and that Carol's identity
binding and sufficiently recent membership proof pass. It records the
marker and pays 100 BUCK atomically. A second redemption fails.

The current B1 interface supplies the *exact issuance commitment* publicly
to authenticate the issuer. Mallory can therefore link this redemption to
its mint. Carol's private identity remains encrypted, but the mint-to-payout
edge does not. This distinction is essential to the comparison.[fn:b1-link]

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** B1.6. Bob opens the depositor's envelope

Bob reads the spend event and decrypts Carol's identity point. A verifiable
decryption proof lets him substantiate what it contains without handing
over his private key. A separately delivered canonical record, or an
authorised match against issuer records, supplies the human name.

Until that record is available, he has a certified point, not a complete
named receipt. The evidence concerns Bob and the final depositor. B1 may
have passed through other hands; those handoffs are not magically written
into its blockchain history.

* A1: address the note to Carol

A1 adds a recipient. Bob remains publicly identified, but a copied note
package is no longer a bearer authorisation. Carol can redeem through a
registered account of her identity without giving Bob its address in
advance. Her mailbox and her payout account do different jobs.

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** A1.1. Carol supplies a certified public receiving key

Carol sends Bob her identity record, public receiving key and evidence of
their certified association. Bob checks the mailbox commitment's salt and
path against an authenticated, acceptable root before paying.

This is a private disclosure to Bob. It lets him recognise that mailbox
association, so it belongs in his confidential records. Its salt differs
from the hidden spending association's salt. Neither the receiving secret
nor Carol's seed travels. A public key is the lock she lends him, not the
key that opens it.

#+ATTR_HTML: :alt A1.1. Carol supplies an identity and certified public mailbox key; Bob verifies the association.
[[file:images/privacy/a1.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** A1.2. Bob adds Carol's lock

Bob encrypts the value for Carol's receiving key. The note's committed
identity payload combines that encrypted value with his public issuer
material. The delivery also carries an encryption of Carol's identity for
her own mailbox.

The opening secret, value and value-encryption randomness travel as
separately masked fields in the delivery blob. Bob can construct the masks
from his fresh encryption randomness and Carol's public key. Carol can
reconstruct them with her private receiving secret. A courier can do neither.

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** A1.3. Mint, then deliver the funded package

The same batch funding checks run, this time for addressed notes from a
public issuer. Once the mint is confirmed, Bob supplies Carol with the
delivery blob, issuer evidence and mint reference.

Mallory sees 500 BUCK enter the pool and may intercept the blob. The
wrapped opening prevents her from computing this note's spent marker or
reading its value from that package. Endpoint and traffic observations
remain possible. Transport privacy still has work to do.

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** A1.4. Carol takes the package apart

Her receiving secret opens the masked fields. Her wallet checks that the
value ciphertext contains 100, that the supplied randomness fits, and that
the recovered opening reconstructs a commitment in the authenticated mint.
It checks Bob's public-issuer evidence and the spent status too.

Each wrapper has now served its purpose: delivery protection becomes
private witness material in Carol's wallet. A wrong key or altered field
fails the relevant consistency checks. Bob's retained opening still cannot
satisfy Carol's identity-and-mailbox spending requirement.

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** A1.5. Prove that the pieces belong to one citizen

Carol chooses her submitting account and prepares two proofs: one for the
funded note and one /folded/ proof joining the identity conditions. The
latter checks that her receiving secret opens this note, her account holds
the matching identity, and a certified hidden leaf binds that identity to
that receiving secret. A1 also checks the encrypted value against the
public payout amount.

The proofs share the note's spent marker. Showing “I have a key” beside
“I have an identity” would not be enough. The checked association makes
them belong to the same citizen.[fn:addressed]

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** A1.6. Redeem without publishing the mint handle

Notes checks both proofs, their roots, the A1 style, face, payout address
and unused marker. BUCK's pool-to-recipient identity checks also apply.
The operation consumes the marker and pays 100; any failure reverts the
state changes.

A1 supplies no exact issuance commitment at spend. Mallory sees the
payout account, amount and time, with no explicit mint handle to join.
Bob and Carol can still derive the marker from their retained opening and
recognise this payment. Privacy from a stranger need not mean amnesia for
the participants.

* A2: put Bob's identity inside the envelope too

A2 keeps the addressed protection and makes the note issuer's identity
private. Read this as an alternative using an appropriately separate
private account. Publishing Bob's name in the B1 story did not become
reversible when we turned the page.

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** A2.1. Add a private issuer envelope and a key tie

Bob uses Carol's already verified mailbox key. Alongside the encrypted
value, he encrypts /his own identity/ for that mailbox. An issuer-binding
proof connects this envelope to his registered account while hiding the
receiving key.

He also adds a blinded *key tie*, labelled *T*. The note commits to T
together with its ciphertexts. Later this ties the key used in the issuer
proof to the key Carol actually holds. Without it, an honest recipient
could be tricked into naming the wrong certified identity.

#+ATTR_LATEX: :width \linewidth :float nil
#+ATTR_HTML: :alt A2.1. Bob adds an encrypted issuer identity, its registered-account binding and the committed key tie T.
[[file:images/privacy/p1.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** A2.2. Seal the tools needed to open and prove it

Carol needs more than ciphertexts. Her eventual proof needs the opening
secret, value, issuer-encryption randomness, Bob's separate naming salt
and the secret that opens T. Bob wraps each field with a distinct mask
derived from the value envelope's shared encryption material.

That protects the construction's seams. Sending the issuer randomness or
T's opening in the clear would reveal Bob through another route. Sending
the naming salt openly would help test his membership. The outer package
must protect its inner tools as carefully as its apparent message.

#+ATTR_LATEX: :width \linewidth :float nil

[[file:images/privacy/p2.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** A2.3. The mint verifies Bob's claim and escrows the batch

Notes checks value conservation, insertion into the current note tree,
the private-issuer mode and Bob's issuer-binding proof. The mint proof
also ties the checked ciphertexts and T to the committed notes. Funding
and insertion remain atomic.

Mallory sees Bob's minting /address/, 500 BUCK, commitments, encrypted
issuer material and blinded proofs. A2 conceals the issuer's identity
inside those objects; it does not conceal which address submitted them.
Anyone who already knows that address belongs to Bob keeps that knowledge.

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** A2.4. Carol unwraps, decrypts and cross-checks

Bob delivers the blob with the full mint evidence and his identity record
over protected communication. Carol unwraps it with her receiving secret,
checks the value and opening, and reconstructs the funded commitment.

She decrypts Bob's point, compares his canonical record, and checks T
against her actual receiving key. Bob's naming salt lets her locate and
prove his separate certified naming association. It exposes that
association to Carol; it does not give her Bob's receiving secret or his
other salts. The distinction is deliberate and worth preserving in backups.

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** A2.5. Join the extra checks into the spend proof

Carol builds the addressed fold with her account identity, receiving
secret and certified association. A2 adds two checks: the decrypted issuer
is a member of the accepted identity tree, and T opens using /Carol's own/
receiving key.

The first prevents a spendable note that names an uncertified invented
point. The second prevents one that names a different certified person.
“Someone certified” and “the person who wrote this note” are different
claims. The proof needs both, and binds them to the same note marker.

#+ATTR_LATEX: :width \linewidth :float nil
#+ATTR_HTML: :alt A2.5. The folded proof adds issuer membership and a key tie preventing a different certified identity from being named.
[[file:images/privacy/p5.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** A2.6. Rewrap for the public spend; then execute

Carol produces a /fresh/ encryption of the same issuer identity for her
mailbox. Her proof checks its relationship to the original note. Publishing
the original mint envelope again would hand Mallory an exact match.

Notes verifies the funded note, unused marker, correct style, accepted
roots and complete folded statement, then pays Carol atomically. The
original envelope, T and private paths remain inside the proof's hidden
inputs. Mallory sees 100 BUCK leave for an address, together with fresh
ciphertext and proofs. She still has timing and amount clues, not a newly
published exact mint handle.

#+ATTR_LATEX: :width \linewidth :float nil
#+ATTR_HTML: :alt A2.6. Fresh re-encryption avoids repeating mint ciphertext; complete proof validation consumes the marker and pays Carol.
[[file:images/privacy/p6.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** A2.7. Keep a useful receipt

Both parties retain their identity records, note opening, relevant proofs
and confirmed chain references. Bob's evidence comes from his mint
randomness and Carol's certified mailbox association. Carol can prove
what she decrypted without disclosing her receiving secret; her receipt
also checks the issuer binding and T.

A receipt reader learns the disclosed names and this payment's links.
They do not thereby receive either citizen's credential or wallet keys.
The current full-record receipt carries more identity data than its short
printed label suggests; encoding it as a blob is not encryption. Keep it
as deliberately as any other private financial record.[fn:receipt]

* Private commerce, usable evidence

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** R1. Disclose the relevant record

A dispute can start with an ordinary citizen producing a receipt. An
auditor or investigator acting under the applicable legal process can
seek relevant records from a participant or another holder of the evidence.
Here Carol supplies a selected receipt and decryption proof. She need not
give away her seed or receiving secret to establish what that envelope said.

This is a design for evidence held by people who actually took part.
Institutions also hold their direct relationships, as N1 showed. Privacy
does not depend on pretending those records do not exist.

#+ATTR_LATEX: :width \linewidth :float nil
#+ATTR_HTML: :alt R1. Carol discloses a selected receipt and proof to an authorised reviewer while retaining her wallet secrets.
[[file:images/privacy/r1.png]]
#+LATEX: \end{privacystep}

#+LATEX: \begin{privacystep}
** R2. Check the record; let people judge the case

The reviewer first checks internal consistency: names match identity
fingerprints, proofs fit their envelopes, and the note opening matches the
claimed payment. Then authenticated chain records establish that the
registration, mint and successful spend actually occurred. A plausible
offline packet alone does not prove a payment happened.

Bob can examine the same evidence and offer context or contest its use.
Certification quality, compromised keys and human intent remain questions
for investigation and adjudication. A signature is evidence of key use,
not a verdict. Good cryptography gives civil governance better facts to
work with while leaving citizens room to conduct ordinary private commerce.

* What Mallory can join

The practical gain is choice over relationships, not an invisible ledger.
This table concerns a stranger with chain access and no participant secrets.
Public identity records and outside knowledge can add to her view.

#+ATTR_LATEX: :font \small :align p{0.12\linewidth}p{0.25\linewidth}p{0.25\linewidth}p{0.25\linewidth}
| Payment | Public connection | Identity disclosure | Who can recognise the note? |
|---------+-------------------+---------------------+-----------------------------|
| Direct EOA | Exact sending and receiving addresses; amount | Private names in bilateral envelopes | Participants; anyone with an address-to-name link can attach it |
| B1 | Exact mint commitment at redemption; public issuer; payout and amount | Issuer public; depositor encrypted for issuer | Public observers link mint and spend; opening holders can also recognise it |
| A1 | Public mint and payout records; no exact mint handle at spend | Issuer public; recipient privately bound | Opening holders; outsiders retain timing and value clues |
| A2 | Public mint and payout records; fresh ciphertext at spend | Both identities concealed from an otherwise uninformed outsider | Opening holders; outsiders retain timing and value clues |

An individual can use A2 to move value between accounts of their own
identity. It removes the direct address-to-address transfer edge that an
ordinary transfer would publish. Reusing an address still links activity
on that address. A distinctive amount, a sparse pool, immediate redemption,
common gas funding or observable delivery can suggest links across accounts.
Cryptographic independence is useful; operational independence must accompany it.

Five boundaries make the positive claim precise:

- *Counterparties know their own dealings.* A note issuer retains its
  openings and can recognise their eventual spends. Disclosure of a receipt
  shares some of those links. It is not a general view into the other
  citizen's accounts.
- *Certification is a public responsibility.* An issuer knows its clients
  and can issue credentials; a dishonest issuer can falsely certify or
  duplicate an identity. Hiding presentations limit recognition of honest
  holders' registrations, not the issuer's power to lie.
- *Current standing is a governed fact.* Membership authorities can remove
  an association; accepted older roots provide a bounded transition window.
  Notes currently accepts retained roots up to seven days old. This affects
  eligibility without publishing all the holder's accounts. Ordinary EOA
  transfer checks do not themselves impose this fresh-membership test.
- *Intermediaries have a view.* Pool operators, custodial wallets and data
  services can accumulate their direct observations. Their security and
  retention practices matter. Notes does not promise that one compromised
  institution can never disclose many customers.
- *Secrets require recovery and care.* The seed restores the receiving key;
  the identity record does not. A leaked long-lived receiving secret exposes
  addressed material available under that key, including old deliveries.
  A new account alone does not replace it. Complete wallet compromise and
  future breaks of the underlying cryptography remain outside these promises.

The useful arrangement is modest: let strangers verify that the rules were
followed, let citizens recognise and document their own dealings, and let
public institutions examine evidence through accountable processes. Buying
a bicycle should not require publishing the rest of your life.
