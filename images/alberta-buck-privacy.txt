                       THE ALBERTA BUCK — PRIVACY
            Paying Carol: private commerce, honest records,
                        and nothing for Mallory

                             Perry Kundert
           ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━


                               2026-09-24


Buy a coffee with a card and at least four businesses learn about it:
the coffee shop, its payment processor, the card network and your
bank. Each keeps the record, and several build products to sell from
what they learn. Show your licence at a bar, a rental counter or a
dispensary and a scanner may keep a copy. In September 2026 a criminal
service offered searchable scans of more than 153 million American and
Canadian driver's licences, about 1.1 million of them Canadian, taken
from one such scanning company's systems over more than a year: photos,
licence numbers, and a timestamp for every scan.[1]

Meanwhile, those who launder money for a living buy their privacy with
shell companies, nominees and couriers. The honest citizen is the one
left standing in the light.

This paper shows another arrangement. Alberta certifies who you are
once. After that you can prove you are a certified person without
showing who, show a counterparty exactly who you are without showing
everyone, and keep a receipt a court can rely on. Commerce between free
citizens stays between them; the record exists for the people entitled
to it.

We follow one payment. Bob owes Carol 100 BUCK. Mallory, who has no
special access, watches everything public. We will build the payment one
layer at a time, and every step below is running code: each fragment
executes against a live local Ethereum chain, and its output is printed
beneath it.[2]


The cast, and how to read this
══════════════════════════════

  • *Bob* and *Carol* are private citizens of Alberta. Bob pays; Carol
     is paid.
  • *Mallory* sits in the middle of every picture. She sees whatever the
    blockchain shows anyone: every transaction, every account, every
    event. She holds nobody's keys.
  • *Alberta's issuer* certifies identities. *Aspen Mutual Credit Union*
    is Bob's credit union and, like any bank, a public
    institution. Every name here is invented for the demonstration.

  Each step has three parts. A short account of what happens. A code
  fragment showing the data gathered in that step, where each value
  comes from, and what it produces. And a picture: Bob on the left,
  Carol on the right, Mallory in the middle, with the service doing the
  work below. A /plus/ adds material, a /lock/ marks sealed contents, an
  /opening arrow/ exposes contents to their intended reader, and a
  /check/ marks a completed check. An arrow crossing Mallory's column
  gives her a view of the traffic, not a key.

  Payloads are drawn as cards. Every field carries a badge saying who
  can read it:

  ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
   Badge    Meaning                                          
  ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   PUBLIC   anyone can read it, Mallory included             
   PRIVATE  known to the named parties only; never published 
   SEALED   encrypted: only the named reader can open it     
   SECRET   never leaves its holder, not even inside a proof 
   PROVEN   established by a proof without being shown       
  ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

  When the code prints a transaction as Mallory sees it, an account
  shows only as its address.  The `[reader: ...]' annotation after it is
  for you, not for her. Public identities, such as the credit union,
  show their names because Mallory can look those up herself.

  The demonstration starts a private chain with the real contracts: the
  identity registry, the BUCK token and the Notes pool. The
  zero-knowledge proofs that take a laptop 15 to 20 seconds each were
  generated in advance from exactly the inputs used below; everything
  else happens here.

First, a credential
═══════════════════

I1. Carol proves who she is, once
─────────────────────────────────

  Carol visits Alberta's issuer with her documents, as she would for a
  licence. The issuer checks them and writes a short /core record/: her
  name at first certification, date of birth, and a person number the
  registry never reuses. Bob does the same on another day.

  The record is hashed into a single number, and from it a point on an
  elliptic curve: Carol's /identity point/, *M*. It is a
  fingerprint. Given the record anyone can check it; given only *M*
  nobody can recover the record. Mallory might notice an appointment,
  but the conversation is private.

  ┌────
  │ record = canonical_identity_data(CAROL)         # the core record, fixed at first certification
  │ m = identity_scalar(record)                     # m = keccak(tag || record) mod the group order
  │ print(record)
  │ print(f"m = {hex(m)[:18]}..  and M = m*G: Carol's identity point, a fingerprint of the record")
  │ card(cards.identity_record(CAROL, "Carol"), "record")
  └────

  ┌────
  │ {"date_of_birth":"1994-10-27","epoch":42,"family_name":"Nakamura","given_name":"Carol","id_number":"AB-P-8812-5507","id_type":"Alberta Identity","issued_at":"2026-04-09T10:05:00Z","issuer_id":"alberta-identity","jurisdiction":"Alberta, Canada"}
  │ m = 0x78540a5aa5db6031..  and M = m*G: Carol's identity point, a fingerprint of the record
  └────

I2. The issuer signs, and Carol keeps the signature
───────────────────────────────────────────────────

  The issuer signs Carol's number with its private key and hands her the
  record and the signature.  Her wallet checks the signature against the
  issuer's published key. The raw signature never leaves her wallet:
  whenever she uses it, she shows a freshly masked version that proves
  the same thing and matches nothing she has shown before.

  ┌────
  │ creds = {who: alberta.issue(fields, applicant_addr=0, rng=rng)        # no account exists yet
  │          for who, fields in (("bob", BOB), ("carol", CAROL), ("aspen", ASPEN))}
  │ cred = creds["carol"]
  │ print(f"the issuer signed Carol's m:          {cred.m == m}")
  │ print(f"her wallet verifies the signature:    {alberta.verify_credential(cred)}")
  │ card(cards.credential(cred, "Carol"), "credential")
  └────

  ┌────
  │ the issuer signed Carol's m:          True
  │ her wallet verifies the signature:    True

Details that change
───────────────────

  Carol will move house, change her phone number, perhaps her name. None
  of that may change *M*, or every account and payment tied to it would
  lose its footing. So the core record holds only what never changes,
  and the current details go in a separate /particulars/ certificate
  that the registry signs over *M*. Each detail is sealed on its own, so
  Carol can show a bar her photo and her age band, and nothing
  else. That is what an ID scan should have been.

  When she moves, the registry issues version 2. *M* is untouched, and
  so is everything bound to it.[3]

  ┌────
  │ from alberta_buck.registry.certificate import registry_keygen
  │ from alberta_buck.registry.particulars import disclose, issue_particulars, verify_disclosure
  │ 
  │ registry = registry_keygen(rng)                 # the registry's signing key
  │ now = {"legal_name": "Carol Nakamura", "address": "1204 10 Ave SW, Calgary AB",
  │        "phone": "+1 403 555 0142", "photo": "sha256:5b1c0e9f", "age_band": "25-34"}
  │ details = issue_particulars(registry, "alberta-identity", P["carol"].M, 1, "2026-04-09", now, rng)
  │ at_the_door = disclose(details, "photo", "age_band")
  │ print("the bar sees:", verify_disclosure(at_the_door, registry.pk, P["carol"].M))
  │ moved = issue_particulars(registry, "alberta-identity", P["carol"].M, 2, "2026-09-01",
  │                           dict(now, address="77 Main St, Canmore AB"), rng)
  │ print("after the move, the lender sees:", verify_disclosure(disclose(moved, "address"),
  │                                                              registry.pk, P["carol"].M))
  │ print("and M is unchanged:", moved.certificate.M == details.certificate.M)
  │ card(cards.particulars(details, "Carol", disclosed=("photo", "age_band")), "particulars")
  └────

  ┌────
  │ the bar sees: {'photo': 'sha256:5b1c0e9f', 'age_band': '25-34'}
  │ after the move, the lender sees: {'address': '77 Main St, Canmore AB'}
  │ and M is unchanged: True

I3. Carol's wallet makes her keys
─────────────────────────────────

  Carol's wallet starts from a secret seed only she holds. From it the
  wallet derives a /receiving key/, the mailbox that addressed payments
  will be sealed to. The receiving key is not derived from her identity:
  a photocopy of your identification should not open your mail.

  The registry records that Carol's identity owns that mailbox, and two
  other facts about her, each as a sealed entry under its own random
  salt. It publishes only one number summarising them all, the /identity
  root/. The code rebuilds that root from nothing but Bob's and Carol's
  own wallet secrets.

  ┌────
  │ from alberta_buck.wallet.recvkey import receiving_key
  │ from alberta_buck.wallet.salt import derive_salt
  │ from alberta_buck.registry.merkle_service import rooted_registry
  │ from alberta_buck.registry.tree import identity_leaf_salted, mailbox_leaf, receiving_leaf
  │ 
  │ bob, carol = P["bob"], P["carol"]
  │ k, pk_recv = receiving_key(carol.seed)          # from her seed, never from her identity
  │ salts = {purpose: derive_salt(carol.seed, world.kyc, n)
  │          for n, purpose in enumerate(("receiving", "mailbox", "naming"))}
  │ print(f"receiving key and salts come from Carol's seed: {k == carol.k and salts == carol.salts}")
  │ 
  │ tree = rooted_registry("registry:kyc")          # the registry's private subtree
  │ for leaf in (receiving_leaf(bob.m, bob.k, bob.salts["receiving"]),
  │              identity_leaf_salted(bob.M, bob.salts["naming"]),
  │              receiving_leaf(carol.m, k, salts["receiving"]),      # identity <-> receiving secret
  │              mailbox_leaf(carol.M, pk_recv, salts["mailbox"]),    # identity <-> public mailbox
  │              identity_leaf_salted(carol.M, salts["naming"])):     # identity, to be named by
  │     tree.insert_leaf(leaf)
  │ print(f"identity root {hex(tree.root())[:18]}..  (the proofs were made against it: "
  │       f"{tree.root() == world.identity_root})")
  └────

  ┌────
  │ receiving key and salts come from Carol's seed: True
  │ identity root 0x2243cb2b517b536a..  (the proofs were made against it: True)
  └────

I4. The wallet packages a fresh account
───────────────────────────────────────

  To use BUCK, Carol opens an account: an ordinary Ethereum address with
  its own signing key. Her wallet adds a second key pair, the account's
  /identity key/, and seals her identity point under it. Then it builds
  the registration package: the sealed identity, a freshly masked
  credential, and a proof that the sealed point is one Alberta certified
  and that Carol holds the key.

  Nothing in the package says "Carol". It says "a certified person", and
  proves it.

  ┌────
  │ pkg = chain.registration_package(A["carol"], cred, rng)
  │ print(f"account             {pkg.account.address}")
  │ print(f"sealed identity E   decrypts to Carol's M with the account key: "
  │       f"{eq(add(pkg.E.C, neg(mul(pkg.E.R, A['carol'].sk))), carol.M)}")
  │ card(cards.registration(pkg, "Carol"), "registration")
  └────

  ┌────
  │ account             0xf269DcD36ce4672f09f804e7b808F32029644F43
  │ sealed identity E   decrypts to Carol's M with the account key: True

I5. The registry checks the proof, without opening anything
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  The registry contract verifies the proof: an Alberta credential, the
  identity sealed in this account, and the key held by whoever sent the
  transaction, all bound to this account on this chain. It stores the
  account's identity key and sealed identity. Carol opens a second
  account, for savings, from the same credential. Bob opens his.

  Mallory sees three registrations. She cannot tell that two of them are
  the same person, and even if she later learned Carol's record she
  could not test these accounts against it.

  ┌────
  │ reg = chain.register(pkg)
  │ chain.register(chain.registration_package(A["carolSavings"], cred, rng))
  │ chain.register(chain.registration_package(A["bob"], creds["bob"], rng))
  │ print(chain.observe(reg).render())
  │ chain.bind_public(A["aspen"])                   # the credit union: a public identity
  │ chain.post_identity_root(tree.root())           # the aggregator publishes the identity root

Paying Carol directly
═════════════════════

  The simplest payment is a transfer from Bob's account to Carol's. The
  chain will show it: two addresses, an amount, a time. What it will not
  show is who owns them. BUCK adds one rule: before money moves between
  two private accounts, each party must have given the other a sealed
  copy of its identity. Nobody gets paid by a stranger they cannot name.

  First, Bob's money. Under BUCK a person's credit lives in an account
  of its own, and how that works is another paper. Bob moves 500 BUCK of
  spending money from his credit account to his everyday account, the
  one he pays from. That transfer is public too: anyone who can tie his
  credit account to him can tie this one. He could have moved it
  privately with the A2 cheque at the end of this paper.

  ┌────
  │ credit = world.new_account("bob", "bobCredit", rng)        # a second account, same Bob
  │ chain.observer.label(credit.address, "Bob's credit account")
  │ chain.register(chain.registration_package(credit, creds["bob"], rng))
  │ chain.fund(credit, 1_000 * U)                               # credit: outside this paper
  │ chain.fund(A["aspen"], 5_000 * U)                           # the credit union's own funds
  │ for a, b in ((credit, A["bob"]), (A["bob"], credit)):
  │     chain.approve(chain.envelope(a, b.address, b.pk, rng))  # sealed identities: next step
  │ chain.transfer(credit, A["bob"].address, 500 * U)
  │ print(f"Bob's everyday account holds {chain.balance(A['bob'].address) / U:,.2f} BUCK")
  └────

  ┌────
  │ Bob's everyday account holds 500.00 BUCK
  └────


E1. Bob seals his identity for Carol
────────────────────────────────────

  Bob's wallet reads Carol's public identity key from the registry and
  seals his own identity point under it. It adds a proof that the sealed
  point is the same one his account registered, and asks BUCK to record
  it with an allowance of zero: this says who Bob is, and gives Carol no
  right to spend his money.

  │ card(cards.envelope(to_carol, "Bob", "Carol"), "envelope")

E2. The contract checks it and keeps a fingerprint
──────────────────────────────────────────────────

  The contract checks the proof against Bob's existing registration, not
  against anything the transaction could substitute, and records a
  fingerprint of the sealed envelope. Mallory can copy the envelope from
  the transaction. She cannot open it, and she cannot swap in someone
  else's identity and keep the proof valid.

E3. Carol opens it and checks the name
──────────────────────────────────────

  Carol's account key opens the envelope, and out comes Bob's identity
  point. Bob sends his core record over a private channel. Carol hashes
  the record and compares: the proof says /this is the identity behind
  Bob's account/, and the record says /whose identity it is/. Both must
  agree. A record for anyone else, even one letter different, does not
  match.

  ┌────
  │ from alberta_buck.wallet.elgamal import elgamal_decrypt
  │ opened = elgamal_decrypt(to_carol.E, A["carol"].sk)
  │ print(f"sealed point is Bob's record:    {eq(opened, point(BOB))}")
  │ print(f"...and not 'Robert Tremblay':    {eq(opened, point(dict(BOB, given_name='Robert')))}")
  └────

  ┌────
  │ sealed point is Bob's record:    True
  │ ...and not 'Robert Tremblay':    False
  └────

E5. Bob pays; both keep a receipt
─────────────────────────────────

  Bob's account sends 100 BUCK to Carol's. The contract finds both
  envelopes in place and moves the money. Mallory sees two addresses,
  100 BUCK and a time; if she ever learns who owns either address, she
  learns this payment too. What she never gets is either name from the
  chain.

  Carol's wallet prints a receipt naming both parties. Its proofs let
  anyone holding the receipt check it without trusting Carol, which is
  what makes it evidence rather than a claim.

Notes: breaking the visible link
════════════════════════════════

  A direct transfer publishes an edge from Bob's account to
  Carol's. /Notes/ remove it. Money goes into a shared pool as a sealed
  note, and comes out later to whoever can prove the right to it.  The
  pool sees many notes go in and many come out, and nothing on the chain
  says which is which.

  Notes come in three kinds, and we will pay Carol with each: a /bearer/
  note from a public issuer (B1), a note /addressed/ to Carol from a
  public issuer (A1), and a note addressed to Carol from a /private/
  issuer (A2). They are alternatives, not stages.


N1. Introductions to the pool
─────────────────────────────

  The pool is a contract with a public identity and an operator. Money
  into and out of it follows the ordinary rule, so private accounts give
  the pool their sealed identities first, just as Bob and Carol did for
  each other. The operator can open those envelopes: it knows who uses
  the pool.  It cannot see which note is whose.[4]

  │ print(f"the operator's key opens Carol's envelope: "
  │       f"{eq(elgamal_decrypt(to_pool.E, chain.pool_sk), carol.M)}")
  └────

B1: cash from the credit union
══════════════════════════════

  A B1 note is digital cash with a known maker, like a banknote or a
  cashier's cheque. Its issuer is a public institution, here Aspen
  Mutual, and anyone holding the note's secret can cash it.  Bob never
  appears on the chain at all.


B1.1. Bob buys a note
─────────────────────

  Bob pays Aspen Mutual 100 BUCK from his account, sealing his identity
  for the credit union first.  It knows him anyway: he is a
  member. Aspen Mutual adds his note to a batch of four it is issuing
  for different members. The note is a value and a random secret, locked
  into a /commitment/: a fingerprint that can be published without
  revealing either.

B1.3. Bob hands Carol the note
──────────────────────────────

  Bob gives Carol the note: printed as a code, sent in a message, it
  does not matter. Her wallet rebuilds the commitment from the secret,
  finds it in Aspen Mutual's batch, confirms the chain records Aspen
  Mutual as its issuer, and checks it has not been cashed.

  This is cash, with cash's weakness: anyone who copies the secret can
  race Carol to cash it. So B1 suits handing over in person and cashing
  promptly. The addressed notes below close that gap.

B1.4. Carol seals her identity for the issuer
─────────────────────────────────────────────

  To cash a note, Carol's wallet derives its /spent marker/ from the
  secret, which lets the chain refuse a second cashing without learning
  which note it was. It proves she holds a note in the tree. And,
  because B1 notes come from a public institution, it seals her identity
  for Aspen Mutual and proves the sealed identity is the certified one
  behind her account.

  That last part is the rule of law in miniature: the note carried no
  name, but whoever cashes it is named to the institution that issued
  it, and to nobody else.

B1.5. The pool checks everything and pays
─────────────────────────────────────────

  The contract checks the note proof, that the spent marker is new, that
  Aspen Mutual really issued this note, the identity binding, and that
  Carol's identity is currently certified. Then it records the marker
  and pays her 100 BUCK. A second attempt fails.

  A B1 cashing names the exact note it redeems, because a public maker's
  notes are meant to be traceable to their maker. Mallory can see that
  one of Aspen Mutual's notes was cashed into this account. Bob is
  nowhere in it.[5] Only coincidence connects him: an earlier 100 BUCK
  payment from his account to the credit union, and a 100 BUCK note
  cashed later. Among a credit union's thousands of members that says
  little; in a demonstration with one, it says a lot.  Standard amounts
  blur it further, as the last section explains.

A1: a draft payable to Carol
════════════════════════════

  An A1 note is a bank draft made out to one person: still issued by a
  public institution, but sealed to Carol's mailbox. A copied note is
  useless to anyone else. Carol can deposit it into any of her accounts
  without telling anyone which in advance, and the cashing names no
  note.


A1.1. Carol gives Bob her mailbox
─────────────────────────────────

  Carol gives Bob her public receiving key and the registry's evidence
  that it belongs to her certified identity. Bob, or his credit union,
  checks that evidence against the published identity root. A public key
  is a lock Carol lends out; the key that opens it never leaves her
  wallet.

  ┌────
  │ from alberta_buck.wallet.recvkey import verify_receiving_binding
  │ mailbox = world.mailbox_binding()               # Carol's key, a salt, and a path to the root
  │ print(f"this mailbox is certified as Carol's: "
  │       f"{verify_receiving_binding(point(CAROL), mailbox, world.identity_root)}")
  └────

  ┌────
  │ this mailbox is certified as Carol's: True

A1.2. The credit union seals the draft
──────────────────────────────────────

  Aspen Mutual encrypts the value to Carol's mailbox and builds the note
  around it. Everything Carol will need later, the note's secret and the
  randomness, travels in a /delivery package/, each field masked so that
  only her receiving secret can unmask it. A courier, or Mallory, can
  carry the package and learn nothing from it.

A1.5. One proof that the pieces are all hers
────────────────────────────────────────────

  Carol proves two things at once: she can read this note, and she is
  the certified person it is addressed to. Proved separately, a thief
  could satisfy the first with a stolen package and the second with his
  own identity. So they are /folded/ into one proof over one set of
  secrets: her receiving secret opens the note, her account holds the
  certified identity, and the registry's sealed entry ties that identity
  to that receiving secret.

  The proof takes a laptop about 15 seconds. It shows the chain what the
  chain needs, and nothing else.[6]

A2: Bob's own private cheque
════════════════════════════

  Now Bob writes the note himself, from his own account, with no
  institution in between. His identity travels inside the note, sealed
  to Carol's mailbox. Only Carol will learn who wrote it.


A2.1. Bob seals his identity inside the note
────────────────────────────────────────────

  Bob encrypts the value and his own identity point to Carol's
  mailbox. He adds a proof that the sealed identity is the one his
  account registered, and a /key tie/, marked *T*, that will force
  Carol's later proof to use the mailbox Bob actually addressed. Without
  it, a dishonest issuer could make a note that names someone other than
  himself.

A2.2. The package carries its own tools, sealed

A2.4. Carol opens it and learns who wrote it

A2.5. The folded proof grows two checks

  A few boundaries keep the claim honest:

  • *Counterparties know their own dealings.* Bob can recognise his note
    when it is cashed; Carol knows who paid her. That is what makes
    receipts possible.
  • *Institutions see their customers.* Aspen Mutual knows Bob bought a
    note and Carol cashed one; the pool's operator knows who uses the
    pool. They answer to regulators for that knowledge.
  • *Standing is current.* Removing someone from the registry stops new
    proofs of their standing; Notes accept an identity root up to seven
    days old.
  • *Behaviour leaks.* An unusual amount, a quiet pool, cashing the
    moment a note is minted, or paying gas from a linked account can
    suggest a connection the cryptography does not make.
  • *Habits help.* Standard amounts, like banknote denominations, make
    one note look like many.  An addressed note is safe to hold, so
    cashing it days later separates the cashing from the minting. A
    bearer note is safer cashed promptly, so its cover is a busy issuer
    and a common denomination. These are the habits of any privacy pool,
    mixers included. The difference is who can use this one: every
    depositor is a certified person, named to the counterparty and, for
    a bearer note, to its issuer.
  • *Keys matter.* Lose the seed and you lose the receiving key; leak it
    and old deliveries sealed to it can be read. A weakness in the
    underlying cryptography, or a compromised wallet, is outside these
    promises.

  Let strangers verify that the rules were followed, let citizens know
  and prove their own dealings, and let public institutions examine
  evidence through accountable process. Buying a bicycle should not
  require publishing the rest of your life.
