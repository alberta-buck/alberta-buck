A currency is only as steady as what it is tied to.  The Alberta Buck
(BUCK) is tied to a basket of base commodities – energy, metals, food –
whose prices are held in place, over the long run, by physics and by
human ingenuity rather than by anyone's promise.  This article is about
that basket: why it has to be heavy, why the things in it keep coming
back, and how the *BuckBasket* turns their coming back into a return for
anyone who parks savings in it.

It is written for everyone.  The machinery, and the arithmetic behind
each claim, is in the companion paper, [The BasketWheel].  ([PDF],
[Text])

1 Anchored to what?
═══════════════════

  Every kind of money eventually has to answer one question: /anchored
  to what?/ Gold standards tied paper to a metal.  Modern money is tied
  to a committee's judgement.  Stablecoins are tied to the dollar, which
  is tied to the same committee.

  The BUCK is tied to a basket of the things every economy runs on, and
  it is measured against that basket /directly/: one BUCK is, by
  definition, one basket's worth.  There is no dollar in between.  That
  matters more than it sounds.  Measure yourself with a tape measure
  that shrinks a little every year and you will conclude that you are
  growing.  Measure a currency in dollars and it inherits the dollar's
  shrinkage without anyone noticing.  So the BUCK measures itself
  against the basket, and a slow on-chain controller adjusts how much
  BUCK the system lets people draw, so that the two stay together.

  For that to work the basket has to be *heavy*.  A light anchor is
  worse than none: one big trade, a thin market or a passing fashion
  could drag it.  A heavy one – deep, wide, and constantly traded
  against the rest of the world – barely moves.  Its weight is not a
  side effect.  It is the product.


2 Why basics come back
══════════════════════

  A share can compound to the moon or fall to zero; nothing in nature
  forbids either.  A bushel of wheat is different, and the difference is
  physical.

  The price of a basic commodity lives between a floor and a ceiling
  that markets cannot escape for long:

  • *The floor is thermodynamic.* You cannot mine copper, smelt iron or
    grow wheat for less than the energy it physically takes.  Sell below
    that and production stops, supply shrinks, and the price climbs
    back.  The periodic table enforces the floor.
  • *The ceiling is substitution.* When a basic gets dear, people switch
    (aluminium for copper, gas for oil), conserve, recycle, or do
    without.  Demand bends.  Human ingenuity, which is plentiful and
    impatient, enforces the ceiling.

  Between them the real price of a basic – in hours of work, or joules
  of energy – wanders but does not run away.  Over years it is noisy.
  Over generations it is remarkably flat: a barrel of oil, a bushel of
  wheat and an ounce of silver have bought roughly the same amount of
  human effort for a very long time.

  Three things can break this: acts of God, war, and autocrats who seize
  a market by force.  All three are real, and all three are historically
  local and temporary.  A /wide/ basket of independent basics is
  diversified against each.  Coming back is not a hope about the future;
  it is what the physical economy does unless someone is shooting at it.


3 A pantry, not a portfolio
═══════════════════════════

  The simplest way to picture the BuckBasket is as a shared pantry.

  You bring something to it – a commodity token, or BUCK – and you are
  given a share of the whole pantry for it.  From then on your share
  rises and falls with everything in the pantry together, not just with
  what you brought.  When you want out, you hand back your share and
  receive its worth in BUCK, whenever you like (or, on a day the basket
  is short of BUCK, your slice of what it holds).  There is no lock-up,
  no notice period, and nobody to ask.

  Two small rules keep it fair to the people already inside:

  • *You can't buy into yesterday's harvest.* A newcomer's goods are
    valued at the cautious end of the market and the pantry at the
    generous end, so nobody joins at a price that quietly takes a slice
    of what others have earned.  Leaving works the same way in mirror.
    The difference is a small fee, about what it costs to trade.
  • *The house takes a quarter of your winnings and none of your
    losses.* When you leave with more than you brought, a quarter of the
    gain goes to the basket's treasury.  If you leave with less, it
    takes nothing.

  There is no fund manager, no forecast and no opinion about the market.
  The BUCK system cannot offer professional asset management and should
  never pretend to.  What it offers is a published, mechanical process
  that anyone can inspect: less a fund than a mill, whose wheel turns
  because the water runs.


4 Where the return comes from
═════════════════════════════

  Here is the part that surprises people.  A basket of things that
  wander but come back does not merely hold its value.  Kept in balance,
  it /earns/.

  ┌────
  │ Two assets, each just wobbling around a flat average (no trend at all):
  │ 
  │    hold either one alone ................ you end where you started
  │    hold half of each, kept at half ...... you end UP
  │ 
  │ Keeping the halves even means selling a little of whatever just rose
  │ and buying a little of whatever just fell: buy low, sell high, done
  │ mechanically on every wobble.  The wobbles themselves pay.
  └────

  Claude Shannon described this decades ago, and it has gone by many
  names since: /Shannon's demon/, /volatility harvesting/, /the
  rebalancing premium/.  It pays most when the parts are volatile, keep
  coming back, and do not move together – which is a fair description of
  energy, metals and food.  An oil shock is not a wheat shock is not a
  copper shock.

  The BuckBasket earns from three places:

  1. *Tolls.* Each commodity in the basket sits in a public trading
     pool, paired with BUCK.  Anyone in the world who trades through it
     pays a small fee, and the fee is the basket's.
  2. *Keeping its own prices straight.* Every time a commodity's price
     moves elsewhere, the basket's pool is briefly out of date.  Early
     in this design we believed the world's arbitrageurs fixed that for
     free.  They do fix it, but not for free: whoever brings a stale
     pool back to the market price pockets the difference, and in a
     market that keeps coming back that difference is almost exactly the
     rebalancing premium above.  The demon works for whoever gets there
     first.  So the basket goes for it too: it corrects its own pools,
     through a loop of three trades that needs no money up front and
     cannot lose, and keeps the change.
  3. *Timing the tides.* New money does not reach every price at once.
     It reaches money-like assets first, energy and building materials
     in about a year, and wages and food a year or two after that – so
     the basket's commodities drift apart and come home again on a tide
     about a year long.  A trading pool harvests the chop, the
     day-to-day wobbles, but it cannot harvest a tide at all.  Only
     deliberate timing can: trimming a commodity that is rich against
     its own long memory, adding to one that is cheap, and never
     trimming one that is still rising.  /Let a run run/, then lean
     against it once it turns.  An optional /director/ does exactly
     that, and nothing more clever.

  The details – how the three trades close a gap, how the director
  decides a tide has turned, and the simulations behind every claim here
  – are in [The BasketWheel].

5 The world's traders, working for the basket
═════════════════════════════════════════════

  The basket's pools sit in the open sea of decentralised finance.  A
  trader hunting for a cheaper route from dollars to gold neither knows
  nor cares what a BUCK is.  If the cheapest route runs dollars to BUCK
  to gold, they take it, and pay the basket's toll on the way.  In our
  simulations such BUCK-unaware traders route through the basket's pools
  as a matter of course.

  That is why weight and traffic reinforce each other.  The deeper and
  wider the basket, the more routes run through it, the more tolls it
  collects, and the harder it is to push.  A heavy basket is a busy
  basket, and a busy basket pays.


6 The basket is a customer too
══════════════════════════════

  Everyone in the Alberta Buck system draws BUCK the same way: against
  something of value, up to a fraction of it.  That fraction is called
  `K', and it is the one number the on-chain controller moves to keep
  BUCK steady against the basket.

  The basket gets no special privileges.  It is an ordinary account
  drawing on a credit like anyone else's, and its collateral is its
  depositors' share of everything it holds.  It may draw up to `K' times
  that worth of BUCK, and it uses the BUCK to pair its commodities in
  their trading pools.  When `K' falls, the offer shrinks; nothing
  already drawn is called back.  There are no margin calls, in the
  basket or anywhere else in the system.

  The upkeep – placing new deposits in the pools, collecting tolls,
  trimming and topping up – happens on a /work wheel/: a small machine
  that anyone may turn with a transaction, and that pays whoever turns
  it out of what the turning captures.  There is no keeper on a salary.
  If nobody turns it, new deposits wait in the basket's wallet and it
  earns less; nothing already in it is put at risk.


7 A tugboat beside the tanker
═════════════════════════════

  The controller that moves `K' steers like a supertanker.  It works
  only through how much credit people may draw, and a book of credit
  takes months to turn.  Someone with deep pockets can shove BUCK off
  course in days.

  So beside the basket there may be a /monetary desk/: a tugboat.  When
  BUCK is cheap it buys; when BUCK is dear it sells.  Most of the time
  that is all it does, and the two cancel out as the price comes back.
  Only when a push persists for weeks does it do something permanent –
  retire BUCK it issued, or issue more – and then only within a
  published limit.  That is how a central bank splits the work between
  its policy rate and its open-market desk.

  The best thing about the desk is when it loses.  Against an attack it
  buys cheap BUCK from the attacker and sells it back dearer, and in our
  model every attacker left poorer.  Against a real change in the world
  – commodities genuinely worth more BUCK – it loses a little money and
  gets out of the way.  A desk that seems to profit from everything has
  not been tested properly.  An early version of ours did, right up
  until we noticed it had quietly bought half the pool it traded in.

  The desk is a separate machine with its own account, its own credit
  and its own wheel.  Two households, two sets of books: we learned that
  one the honest way, when a simulated desk's capital briefly counted
  toward the depositors' borrowing, and a test now guards the wall
  between them.  The details are in [The BasketWheel].

8 Yield against demurrage
═════════════════════════

  The BUCK is designed to /circulate/.  Idle balances pay a small
  holding fee, demurrage, which keeps money moving (see [Demurrage and
  the Jubilee Fund]).  That is good for the economy and mildly annoying
  for a saver, who would like a rainy-day fund to at least hold its
  ground.

  The BuckBasket is the saver's answer.  Savings parked in it earn the
  tolls and the harvest instead of paying demurrage, and remain
  available on demand.  In simulation the harvest has run at roughly one
  to three percent a year over simply holding the same commodities, and
  less when prices wander off and do not come back.  Treat those as a
  simulation's numbers, not a promise.

  It is a medium-to-long-term proposition.  On the day you leave, a
  commodity may be below its average, and you get what the basket is
  worth that day.  Two things shrink that risk: *time*, because coming
  back is the physical default, and *width*, because the more
  independent basics the basket holds, the smaller the chance that they
  are all down at once.  The basket rewards patience and diversification
  – exactly the habits a stable money should encourage.

9 What it is for
════════════════

  Anywhere money has to stay available but should not sit idle:

  • *a household's savings account*, earning the basket's harvest
     instead of paying demurrage;
  • *a business's working cash*, kept liquid for payroll and suppliers,
     and working in between;
  • *an insurer's reserves*, which prudence requires to be both
    available and preserved – the two things the basket is built to be
    at once.


10 The one real risk: the asset that never comes back
═════════════════════════════════════════════════════

  Every honest design names the case in which it loses.  The
  BuckBasket's is simple.

  Rebalancing pays /because/ things come back.  If something in the
  basket simply rises forever, the basket keeps dutifully trimming the
  one thing it should have held, and a plain holder of that asset does
  better, permanently.

  This is not hypothetical; it is the explicit pitch of an entire asset
  class.  An asset designed to be scarce, like Bitcoin, is built to rise
  and not come back – "number go up" is the whole idea.  Put much of it
  in the basket and you have installed a gear that turns the wrong way
  in a machine whose every gear assumes coming back.  The rebalancing
  premium and the ever-scarcer store of value are mathematically
  opposed.[1]


11 The discipline: only civilization's basics
═════════════════════════════════════════════

  So the rule is strict.  *The BuckBasket admits only low-level,
  civilizationally basic commodities* – energy, labour, structural
  materials, staple foods: the raw inputs every society needs and
  physics forces back toward their costs.  It excludes anything whose
  value rests on scarcity by design, a network effect or speculation.
  Not because those things are bad, but because they are the wrong
  ingredients for this recipe.

  The reward for the discipline is the BUCK's whole reason for being:
  value that holds across generations.  A unit of money your
  grandchildren will recognise has to be tied to something with the
  weight of the physical economy, not to the fashion of a decade or the
  supply schedule of a protocol.  The basket gives up the spectacular
  upside of speculative assets for the one thing a monetary base must
  have and a speculative asset can never offer: the near certainty of
  coming back.

13 Conclusion: an anchor of stone, kept honest by the crowd
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

  The BuckBasket is a heavy, open, liquid pantry of civilization's
  basics.  The whole world's trading keeps it priced, it corrects its
  own prices before strangers can profit from them, and it pays its
  depositors what reversion to the mean has always paid the patient.

  It gives the BUCK the heavy anchor a generational currency needs, and
  savers, businesses and insurers a place for idle value to earn rather
  than decay.  It needs no manager, no forecast and no promise – only a
  published mechanism, the appetite of strangers for a bargain, and the
  fact that the crust of the Earth and the laws of thermodynamics will
  not, short of catastrophe, let the basics run away.

  It cannot manage your portfolio, and was never meant to.  It does
  something rarer: it stands still under the weight of the physical
  economy, and lets you lean on it.
